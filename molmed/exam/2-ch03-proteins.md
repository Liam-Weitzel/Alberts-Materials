Q: **Official sample question 2.** Under stress conditions the levels of the p53 protein (tumor suppressor / DNA-binding protein) regulate cellular decisions about proliferation, apoptosis and senescence.

**a)** Arginine residues in the interface between p53 and DNA are frequently mutated in tumors. What type of interaction do you expect between an Arg and DNA?

**b)** What other types of amino acids could make this interaction?

**c)** A mutation could also affect the ability of p53 to fold into a three-dimensional structure and thereby affect its stability. Different classes of amino acids can cause different folding problems. Please discuss two different ways in which a mutation could cause a problem.

**d)** A protease causes the degradation of other proteins to amino acids. What bonds will it break?

**e)** The proteasome has 14 subunits, at what level of folding do these come together?
A: **In brief:** a) Arg is **positively charged**: an **ionic (salt bridge)** interaction with the negatively charged **phosphate backbone**, plus hydrogen bonds. b) The other **basic** residues, **Lys** and **His**; for hydrogen bonds, **polar** residues (Ser, Thr, Asn, Gln, Tyr). c) For example a polar/charged or bulky residue in the **hydrophobic core**, or losing a **Gly** or adding a **Pro** that disrupts the backbone or secondary structure. d) **Peptide bonds**, by hydrolysis. e) **Quaternary** structure.

**a)** Arginine is a **basic, positively charged** amino acid (its guanidinium group is charged at pH 7). DNA carries a **negative charge** on every phosphate of its backbone. The main interaction is therefore **electrostatic (ionic / a salt bridge)** between the Arg side chain and the **phosphate backbone**. The guanidinium group is also an excellent **hydrogen bond donor** (several N–H groups), so Arg makes **hydrogen bonds** too, to phosphate oxygens or to the edges of bases (particularly guanine) in the **major groove**, which can give sequence specificity. Losing this Arg removes both, so p53 no longer binds its target sites and cannot activate its target genes.

**b)**
- For the **electrostatic** interaction: the other **positively charged (basic)** amino acids, **lysine** and, when protonated, **histidine** (its pKa is close to 6, so it is only partly charged at physiological pH).
- For **hydrogen bonding** to DNA: **polar** side chains with donors or acceptors, such as **Ser, Thr, Tyr, Asn, Gln** (and Lys/His again). Backbone N–H groups can also hydrogen bond to phosphates.

**c)** Any two of the following, each explained:
- **A change in the hydrophobic core.** A folded protein keeps its **hydrophobic (aliphatic/aromatic) side chains buried**. Replacing a buried hydrophobic residue with a **charged or polar** one buries a charge or leaves **hydrogen bond donors/acceptors unsatisfied**, which is very unfavourable; replacing a small residue with a **large** one causes **steric clashes**; a large-to-small change leaves a **cavity** and loses van der Waals packing. Because folded proteins are only **marginally stable** (a difference of roughly 5 to 15 kcal/mol, a few hydrogen bonds), one such change can be enough for p53 to unfold at body temperature.
- **A change in a "conformational" residue.** **Glycine** can adopt backbone (φ/ψ) angles that are forbidden for other residues, so mutating a Gly in a tight turn forces a strained conformation. **Proline** is rigid and has no backbone N–H, so introducing a Pro into an **α-helix** or β-strand breaks the main-chain hydrogen-bond pattern of the secondary structure.
- **Loss of a cofactor site.** p53's DNA-binding domain holds a **zinc ion** coordinated by Cys and His side chains; mutating a coordinating residue loses the zinc and destabilises the fold.
- **A hydrophobic residue on the surface** does not stop folding but creates a sticky patch that promotes **aggregation** (compare Glu→Val in sickle haemoglobin).

**d)** **Peptide bonds**, the covalent bonds that link the amino acids of the chain (the primary structure). The protease breaks them by **hydrolysis**, adding water: the reverse of the condensation reaction that formed them.

**e)** At the level of **quaternary structure**: the association of several separate polypeptide chains (subunits), each folded on its own, into one complex, held together by noncovalent interactions. (Proteolysis in the proteasome core happens inside such a multi-subunit cylinder.)

**Where marks are lost:** answering (a) with "hydrogen bond" alone without the charge-charge interaction with the phosphate backbone, or listing amino acids in (b) without saying *why* they can make the interaction.

Q: Sickle cell anaemia is caused by a single change in β-globin: a **glutamate on the surface** of the protein is replaced by a **valine**. The mutant haemoglobin folds normally, yet in the deoxygenated state it polymerises into long fibres that distort red blood cells.

**a)** Why does this mutation not disrupt the fold of the protein?

**b)** Explain, in terms of the hydrophobic effect and of how identical subunits associate, why the mutant haemoglobin forms long fibres rather than, for example, a closed dimer.

**c)** Predict what would happen instead if a valine in the hydrophobic core of β-globin were replaced by a glutamate.
A: **In brief:** The residue is on the **surface**, so the fold is untouched. Valine is **hydrophobic** and sticks into a hydrophobic pocket on a **different surface** of the next haemoglobin: a **head-to-tail** contact, which is not self-limiting, so molecules polymerise into fibres. A charged Glu in the **core** would destabilise the fold instead.

**a)** The residue is on the **surface**. Surface side chains face water and contribute little to the **hydrophobic core** or the main-chain hydrogen bonds that stabilise the fold. Replacing one polar surface residue does not remove any interaction the folded state depends on.

**b)** Valine is **hydrophobic**. On the surface it is exposed to water, which becomes ordered around it; this is unfavourable, so the valine is pushed to bury itself, and it does so in a **hydrophobic pocket on a neighbouring haemoglobin** molecule (exposed in the deoxy form). The sticky patch and the pocket are **different surfaces** of the same molecule, so the binding is **head-to-tail (heterologous)**: every molecule that binds leaves a free sticky patch or pocket for the next one. Such an interaction is **not self-limiting**, so the molecules add on indefinitely and form a **long helical fibre**. A head-to-head (isologous) interaction, using the same patch on both partners, uses up both binding sites and would stop at a dimer.

**c)** A **charged** Glu buried in the core cannot be solvated there, and a charge in a nonpolar environment is very unfavourable; it also disrupts the packing of the core. The protein would be **destabilised or fail to fold**, and would likely be degraded or aggregate, a folding (loss-of-function) defect rather than the gain-of-stickiness seen in sickle cell.

Q: A typical folded protein contains hundreds of hydrogen bonds, yet the difference in free energy between the folded and unfolded state is only about 5 to 15 kcal/mol, the equivalent of a few hydrogen bonds.

**a)** What drives a protein to fold?

**b)** The polypeptide backbone carries an N–H and a C=O group at every residue, and it must pass through the hydrophobic core. Why is this a problem, and how is it solved?

**c)** Why is the net stability of a folded protein so small, and what is one consequence of that for the cell?

**d)** Many proteins need chaperones to fold in the cell. Does that contradict Anfinsen's principle? Explain.
A: **In brief:** Folding is driven by the **hydrophobic effect** and by satisfying every **hydrogen bond**. The polar backbone crosses the core by forming **secondary structure**, which pairs its own N–H and C=O groups. Stability is marginal because folding mostly **exchanges** hydrogen bonds with water for internal ones. **Chaperones** only prevent aggregation and add no information, so Anfinsen's principle holds.

**a)** Two things. (1) The **hydrophobic effect**: burying nonpolar side chains in the interior prevents water from being **ordered** around them, which is entropically favourable. (2) Making sure that **every hydrogen bond donor and acceptor is satisfied** in the folded state. The chain behaves like an **oil drop in water**: nonpolar inside, polar and charged side chains outside.

**b)** The backbone is **hydrophilic**. Burying an N–H or C=O without a partner would lose its hydrogen bonds to water and gain nothing, which is very unfavourable. The solution is **secondary structure**: in an **α-helix** each C=O(i) bonds to the N–H(i+4), and in a **β-sheet** the strands hydrogen bond to their neighbours, so the backbone **satisfies its own hydrogen bonds** and can cross the core.

**c)** In the unfolded state, almost all polar groups are already **hydrogen bonded to water**. Folding mostly **exchanges** hydrogen bonds with water for hydrogen bonds within the protein, so each one gains very little; the net balance of large favourable and unfavourable terms is small. Consequence: **small changes** (one mutation, a temperature or pH shift) can unfold a protein; conversely, marginal stability allows **flexibility** and regulation of shape.

**d)** No. Anfinsen showed that the information for the native structure is **in the amino acid sequence** (denatured ribonuclease refolded on its own). **Chaperones act as catalysts**: they bind exposed hydrophobic surfaces of partly folded chains, prevent **aggregation** in the crowded cell and give the chain repeated chances to fold, but they carry **no information** about the final structure.

Q: Insulin is synthesised as a single chain, **proinsulin**, which folds and forms **disulfide bridges** in the endoplasmic reticulum. A middle segment is then cut out, leaving two chains held together only by the disulfide bridges.

**a)** Why are disulfide bridges found in secreted proteins such as insulin, but not normally in proteins of the cytosol?

**b)** Why is it useful for extracellular proteins in particular to be reinforced in this way?

**c)** Compare activation by proteolytic cleavage (as for proinsulin or trypsinogen) with regulation by phosphorylation. Why is cleavage well suited to digestive enzymes and blood clotting?
A: **In brief:** Disulfides form by **oxidation**, so only in the oxidising **ER** and outside the cell, not in the reducing cytosol. The **covalent** cross-link stabilises proteins in the harsh extracellular environment (insulin's two chains are held together only by them). **Proteolysis is irreversible**, ideal for one-way, spatially controlled and amplified activation (zymogens, clotting cascade).

**a)** Forming a disulfide bridge between two cysteines is an **oxidation**, so it needs an **oxidising environment**. The **ER lumen** (the start of the secretory route) is oxidising, while the **cytosol and nucleus are reducing**, so disulfides do not form (or are reduced) there. Secreted proteins make their disulfides in the ER and keep them outside the cell.

**b)** The extracellular environment is **harsher and less controlled** than the cytosol: no chaperones, variable conditions, proteases. A disulfide is a **covalent** link between distant parts of the chain, so it adds **extra stability** to a fold that is otherwise held only by weak noncovalent interactions. For insulin it is essential: once the connecting peptide is removed, the two chains stay together **only** through the disulfides.

**c)** Proteolysis is **irreversible**: a peptide bond, once cut, cannot be re-formed. Phosphorylation is **reversible** (kinase on, phosphatase off) and suits switches that must turn on and off. Irreversibility is ideal where activation must be **one-way and spatially controlled**: trypsin is made as inactive **trypsinogen** so it cannot digest the pancreas that makes it, and is activated only in the intestine; in **blood clotting**, a **cascade** of proteases activating each other **amplifies** a small signal quickly and commits to forming a clot.

Q: The same lysine side chain in a protein can be **acetylated**, **methylated** or **ubiquitinated**.

**a)** How does acetylation of a lysine change its chemical properties, and what is the consequence when the lysine sits in a histone tail?

**b)** Methylation of a lysine does not change its charge. How can it still change the function of the protein?

**c)** Explain the difference between a single ubiquitin and a Lys48-linked ubiquitin chain attached to a protein.

**d)** Phosphorylation of a tyrosine can switch a protein into a partner for another signalling protein. Explain how, using the example of the SH2 domain.
A: **In brief:** **Acetylation** removes the lysine's positive charge, loosening the histone's grip on DNA. **Methylation** keeps the charge but creates a **binding site** for reader domains. A **single ubiquitin** is a signal; a **Lys48 polyubiquitin chain** sends the protein to the proteasome. **Phosphotyrosine** is bound by **SH2 domains**, so phosphorylation recruits signalling partners.

**a)** Acetylation **neutralises the positive charge** of the lysine amino group. Histones bind DNA largely through electrostatic attraction between their positive lysines and arginines and the negative DNA backbone; acetylation **weakens that grip**, which is associated with more **open chromatin**.

**b)** Methylation **creates a binding site**. It changes the shape and hydrophobicity of the side chain, and specific **"reader" domains** in other proteins recognise the methylated lysine. What the mark means depends on which reader binds it, not on its chemistry.

**c)** **Monoubiquitination** acts as a **signal** that changes the protein's function or interactions (for example on histone H2A). A **polyubiquitin chain linked through Lys48** marks the protein for **degradation by the proteasome**.

**d)** Phosphorylation by a **kinase** adds a large **negative charge** to a tyrosine. **SH2 domains** specifically **bind phosphotyrosine**, so the phosphorylated protein can now recruit any protein that carries an SH2 domain. Because domains can be **mixed and matched**, SH2 domains are found in adaptors, kinases, phosphatases and many others, so one phosphorylation event can assemble a signalling complex. A **phosphatase** reverses it.

Q: Ras is a small GTP-binding protein that relays growth signals. Mutant forms of Ras that cannot hydrolyse GTP are found in many cancers.

**a)** Describe how Ras works as a molecular switch, including the roles of a GEF and a GAP.

**b)** Explain why a mutation that blocks GTP hydrolysis is oncogenic.

**c)** Phosphorylation by kinases is another widespread molecular switch. What do the two kinds of switch have in common?
A: **In brief:** **Ras-GTP is on, Ras-GDP is off**; a **GEF** switches it on by exchanging GDP for GTP, a **GAP** switches it off by speeding GTP hydrolysis. Ras that cannot hydrolyse GTP is **stuck on** and signals for proliferation constantly. Kinase/phosphatase and GTPase switches both change **conformation** in response to a phosphate and are reset by a second enzyme.

**a)** **Ras-GTP is the active "on" state**: in this conformation it binds downstream effectors and passes on the signal. Ras **hydrolyses its GTP to GDP**, and **Ras-GDP is inactive**. A **GEF** (guanine nucleotide exchange factor) switches Ras **on** by releasing GDP so GTP (abundant in the cell) can bind; a **GAP** (GTPase-activating protein) switches it **off** by **accelerating** the otherwise slow GTP hydrolysis. The difference between the two states is a **conformational change** in the switch regions, triggered by the presence or absence of the γ-phosphate.

**b)** If Ras cannot hydrolyse GTP (for example because GAPs can no longer accelerate hydrolysis), it is **stuck in the GTP-bound, active state**. It then signals for proliferation **continuously**, independent of any growth factor, which drives uncontrolled cell division.

**c)** Both are **switches driven by a conformational change**: an input (a covalent phosphate, or a bound nucleotide) changes the shape of the protein, and the new shape changes its activity or partners. Both are **reset by a second enzyme** (a phosphatase, or GTP hydrolysis assisted by a GAP), and both use the energy of a phosphate bond (ATP for kinases, GTP for GTPases) so that the cycle runs in one direction.

Q: You want to determine the three-dimensional structure of three different proteins: (i) a small, 12 kDa domain that is partly flexible and does not crystallise; (ii) a very large, 1 MDa membrane protein complex that adopts several conformations; (iii) a stable enzyme that crystallises well, bound to a candidate drug, where you need atomic detail of the binding site.

**a)** Which structure-determination method would you choose for each, and why?

**b)** A colleague shows you an AlphaFold model of a two-domain protein. Both domains are coloured dark blue, but the predicted aligned error (PAE) matrix shows light regions between the two domains. What can and can't you conclude from this model?

**c)** Give one fundamental limitation of AlphaFold that would matter for a protein that switches between two conformations.
A: **In brief:** **NMR** for the small flexible domain, **cryo-EM** for the large membrane complex with several states, **X-ray crystallography** for atomic detail of the drug in a well-crystallising enzyme. Dark blue domains are **confidently folded**, but light PAE between them means their **relative arrangement is unreliable**. AlphaFold shows **one state only** (compare Mad2).

**a)**
- **(i) NMR.** Works on proteins **in solution** without crystals, reports **dynamics and flexibility**, and is suited to **small** proteins (it becomes impractical for large ones). Requires ¹⁵N/¹³C labelling.
- **(ii) Cryo-EM.** No crystals needed, works best for **large complexes and membrane proteins**, and can **sort particles into classes**, capturing several conformational states from one sample. Small proteins are hard, which is not a problem here.
- **(iii) X-ray crystallography.** Gives **very high resolution**, ideal for seeing exactly how a small molecule sits in a binding site; the protein crystallises well, so the main drawback (needing crystals) is solved. The phase problem can be solved by **molecular replacement** with a known structure.

**b)** The dark blue colouring (high pLDDT) means each **domain's structure is confidently predicted**. The light areas in the PAE matrix mean the **relative position** of the two domains is **uncertain**. So you can trust each domain's fold, but not the way they are arranged relative to each other; the arrangement shown may be arbitrary.

**c)** AlphaFold predicts **a single state** per protein. A protein that switches conformations (for example **Mad2**, open versus closed when bound to Mad1) will be shown in only one form, which may not be the one you care about. (Other limitations: it lacks many cofactors, metals and ligands, and AlphaFold3 can **hallucinate** structure in disordered regions.)

Q: Many proteins inside cells are regulated by phosphorylation and dephosphorylation. Imagine a metabolic reaction that is catalyzed by an enzyme which is completely active when not phosphorylated and completely inactive when phosphorylated (see figure).

![An enzyme drawn as a circle labelled ON when unphosphorylated, interconverting with a square carrying a phosphate group labelled OFF](assets/img/slide-9-21.png)

Inside the cell the rate of this metabolic reaction varies continuously between very fast and very slow (or zero) at a given substrate concentration.

How can the reaction in the cell proceed at any rate between very fast and zero, when the enzyme can only be either on or off?
A: **In brief:** A cell contains **many copies** of the enzyme. Each molecule is either on or off, but the rate depends on the **fraction** of molecules that is unphosphorylated at that moment. That fraction is set continuously by the **balance between the kinase and the phosphatase**, so the population behaves like a dimmer rather than a switch.

**Many molecules, not one.** The reaction rate in a cell is the sum of the activity of **all** the enzyme molecules. If 100% are unphosphorylated the reaction runs at its maximum; if 50% are, at half; if none are, it stops. Any rate in between is reached by changing the **proportion** of active molecules.

**What sets the proportion.** Each molecule is continually phosphorylated by a **protein kinase** and dephosphorylated by a **protein phosphatase**, so it cycles between on and off. At steady state the fraction that is on depends on the **relative activities of the kinase and the phosphatase**, which are themselves regulated by signals. Raising kinase activity shifts the population towards off; raising phosphatase activity shifts it towards on. Because phosphorylation is **reversible**, the fraction can be moved up and down continuously and quickly.

(Also acceptable: over time a single molecule spends a certain fraction of time in each state, so its time-averaged activity is also graded.)

**Where marks are lost:** answering only "by changing substrate concentration": the question fixes the substrate concentration.
