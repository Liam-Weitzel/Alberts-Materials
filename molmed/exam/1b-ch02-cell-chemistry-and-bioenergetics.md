Q: In the citric acid cycle, the enzyme **succinate dehydrogenase** converts succinate into fumarate. In the process, two hydrogen atoms are removed from succinate (a C–C single bond becomes a C=C double bond) and the carrier **FAD** becomes **FADH₂**.

**a)** Which molecule is oxidised and which is reduced? Justify your answer using the definitions of oxidation and reduction.

**b)** A student writes: "This is an oxidation reaction, because both succinate and FAD are oxidised." Explain why this cannot be true.

**c)** Is the ΔG of this reaction in the cell positive or negative? Explain your reasoning.

**d)** How is FAD regenerated from FADH₂, and what happens to the electrons?
A: **In brief:** Succinate loses two hydrogens (two electrons plus two protons), so it is **oxidised**; FAD takes them up and is **reduced**. Redox reactions always come in pairs because electrons are conserved. ΔG is negative (the reaction runs in the cell), and FAD is regenerated by **oxidising** FADH₂, whose electrons go to the respiratory chain and end on O₂.

**a)** **Succinate is oxidised; FAD is reduced.** Oxidation is the **loss of electrons**, and in organic molecules it usually shows as the loss of hydrogen atoms (fewer C–H bonds) or gain of oxygen. Succinate loses two hydrogen atoms, that is **two electrons plus two protons**. Reduction is the **gain of electrons**: FAD takes up those two hydrogens to become FADH₂.

**b)** Oxidation and reduction **always occur together**, because electrons are conserved: electrons lost by one molecule must be accepted by another. If succinate loses electrons, something has to gain them, and here that is FAD. The correct description is a **redox reaction** in which one partner is oxidised and the other reduced (OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain).

**c)** **Negative.** The reaction proceeds in the cell as part of a cycle that runs forward, so under cellular conditions it must be energetically favourable (ΔG < 0).

**d)** By **oxidising** FADH₂, not by reducing it. FADH₂ hands its electrons to the **electron-transport chain** in the inner mitochondrial membrane (entering at complex II / ubiquinone), and they finally reduce **O₂ to water**. FAD is then free to accept electrons again.

**Where marks are lost:** writing that FAD is regenerated "by reducing FADH₂" (that is the reverse), or defining oxidation only as "adding oxygen".

Q: The one-carbon compounds below can be arranged in a series: **methane (CH₄), methanol (CH₃OH), formaldehyde (CH₂O), formic acid (HCOOH), carbon dioxide (CO₂)**.

**a)** In which direction along this series does oxidation proceed? What changes at each step?

**b)** Which of these molecules can yield the most energy when it is oxidised in a cell, and which yields none? Explain.

**c)** Use this principle to explain why fat stores roughly a month's worth of energy in a human, while glycogen stores only about a day's.
A: **In brief:** Oxidation runs from **methane to CO₂**, each step replacing a C–H bond by a bond to oxygen. The most **reduced** carbon (methane) yields the most energy, CO₂ none. Fat is made of hydrocarbon chains of highly reduced carbons, while glucose is already partly oxidised, so fat stores more energy per gram (and is stored without water).

**a)** Oxidation runs **from methane to CO₂**; reduction runs the other way. At each step a **C–H bond is replaced by a bond to oxygen**, so the carbon progressively **loses electrons** (they are pulled towards the more electronegative oxygen). Oxidation = removal of electrons = H replaced by O = fewer C–H bonds.

**b)** **Methane**, the most **reduced** carbon, has the most electrons to give up, and so the most energy to release when those electrons are passed to O₂. **CO₂** is fully oxidised: its carbon has nothing left to give, which is why it is the end product of respiration.

**c)** The fatty acid chains of fat are long **hydrocarbon** tails, made of highly **reduced** carbons (like the methane end of the series). Glucose carbons already carry an –OH on almost every carbon, so they are **partly oxidised**. Per carbon, and per gram, fat therefore delivers more electrons to the electron-transport chain and more ATP. Fat is also stored without water (as triacylglycerol in droplets), while glycogen is hydrated, which further increases the difference per gram.

Q: A living cell is a highly ordered structure: it builds macromolecules from small building blocks and keeps them organised.

**a)** The second law of thermodynamics states that the disorder of the universe always increases. Explain why a cell does not violate this law.

**b)** The synthesis of glutamine from glutamic acid and ammonia has a positive ΔG and does not occur on its own. Describe how the cell uses ATP to make it happen, and explain why the overall process is favourable.

**c)** Why does building any macromolecule (a protein, a nucleic acid, a polysaccharide) cost the cell energy?
A: **In brief:** The cell releases heat to its surroundings, so total disorder still rises. For glutamine, ATP first phosphorylates glutamic acid (an activated intermediate) and ammonia then displaces the phosphate: the ΔGs add up to a negative total. Joining subunits is a condensation, which is unfavourable, so every polymerisation must be coupled to an energy source.

**a)** The cell is not a closed system. It takes in energy from **food** (or light) and uses it to create order inside, but it **releases heat** and waste to its surroundings. Heat is energy in its most disordered form: it increases the random motion of molecules around the cell. The increase in disorder outside **exceeds** the decrease in disorder inside, so the total disorder of the universe still increases.

**b)** By **reaction coupling** through a **high-energy intermediate**, in two steps:
1. **Activation:** ATP transfers a phosphate to glutamic acid, making a **phosphorylated (activated) intermediate** and ADP.
2. **Condensation:** ammonia reacts with that intermediate to give **glutamine**, releasing inorganic phosphate.

Net: glutamic acid + NH₃ + ATP → glutamine + ADP + Pᵢ. The large **negative ΔG of ATP hydrolysis** is larger than the positive ΔG of forming glutamine, so the **sum of the ΔGs is negative** and the coupled process proceeds. The coupling is chemical: the two reactions share an intermediate.

**c)** Macromolecules are made by **condensation** reactions, which join subunits by removing water, and condensation is **energetically unfavourable (ΔG > 0)**; the reverse, **hydrolysis**, is favourable. Building a polymer and keeping it ordered also lowers entropy. So every polymerisation step must be driven by coupling to an energy source, ultimately from food via activated carriers such as ATP.

Q: Glucose in a sugar bowl can sit in air for years without reacting, even though its oxidation to CO₂ and water has a large negative ΔG.

**a)** Explain why the reaction does not happen, although it is "spontaneous".

**b)** When the same reaction is catalysed by enzymes in a cell, which of the following change, and which do not: (i) ΔG of the reaction, (ii) the activation energy, (iii) the equilibrium position, (iv) the rate? Explain.

**c)** Why is it an advantage to the cell that glucose is oxidised in many small enzymatic steps rather than burned in one?
A: **In brief:** A negative ΔG says a reaction *can* proceed, not how fast; the activation energy is too high for it to happen at a measurable rate. An enzyme lowers the activation energy and so raises the rate, but changes neither ΔG nor the equilibrium. Small steps let the energy be captured in carriers such as ATP and NADH instead of being lost as heat.

**a)** "Spontaneous" (ΔG < 0) says only that the reaction is **energetically favourable**, that it needs no net energy input; it says nothing about **speed**. To react, molecules must first pass through a high-energy **transition state**, and the **activation energy** needed to reach it is too high to be supplied by thermal motion at room temperature, so the reaction is immeasurably slow.

**b)** An enzyme **lowers the activation energy** (ii changes) by providing an alternative path through a more easily reached transition state, so the **rate increases** (iv changes). It does **not change ΔG** (i), because the free energies of the reactants and products are the same with or without it, and so it does **not change the equilibrium position** (iii): the catalysed reaction reaches the same equilibrium, only faster.

**c)** Burning releases all the energy at once as **heat**, which the cell cannot capture. In stepwise oxidation each step releases a smaller amount of free energy, and much of it can be **captured in activated carriers** (ATP, NADH, FADH₂) rather than lost. The steps are also individually controllable, and their intermediates can be drawn off for **biosynthesis**.

**Where marks are lost:** saying that an enzyme "makes the reaction more favourable" or "shifts the equilibrium".

Q: The liver provides glucose to the rest of the body between meals. It does so by breaking down glycogen, forming glucose 6-phosphate which is subsequently converted to glucose by splitting of the phosphate by hydrolysis:

G6P → glucose + Pᵢ (ΔG° = −3.3 kcal/mole).

Conversion of glucose 6-phosphate to glucose could also occur by reversing the reaction

glucose + ATP → G6P + ADP (ΔG° = −4.0 kcal/mole), see figure below.

![Hexokinase phosphorylating glucose on its 6 carbon using ATP, giving glucose 6-phosphate, ADP and a proton](assets/img/slide-9-20.png)

By the reversed reaction G6P + ADP → glucose + ATP the liver would generate both glucose *and* ATP!

Why does the liver remove the phosphate by hydrolysis rather than by the reaction which also generates ATP? Explain your answer.
A: **In brief:** Reversing hexokinase has ΔG° = **+4.0 kcal/mol**, so it is energetically unfavourable and would not run towards glucose; hydrolysis has ΔG° = **−3.3 kcal/mol** and proceeds. The phosphate in G6P sits in a lower-energy bond than the one it would need to form in ATP, so it cannot be used to make ATP.

**The energetics.** If glucose + ATP → G6P + ADP has ΔG° = −4.0 kcal/mol, the reverse, **G6P + ADP → glucose + ATP, has ΔG° = +4.0 kcal/mol**. A reaction with a positive ΔG does not proceed on its own: its equilibrium lies far on the side of G6P and ADP. In the cell it is even less favourable, because cells keep the **ATP/ADP ratio high**, which pushes this reaction further towards the left. The liver would make essentially no glucose this way.

**Why.** The phosphate in G6P is held by a **phosphoester bond** of relatively low energy (its hydrolysis releases 3.3 kcal/mol), while making ATP means forming a **phosphoanhydride bond** of higher energy (its hydrolysis releases about 7.3 kcal/mol). The difference (−7.3 + 3.3 = −4.0) is exactly why hexokinase runs forward. You cannot move a phosphate "uphill" into ATP without an energy input.

**The hydrolysis** by glucose 6-phosphatase has a **negative ΔG**, so it runs to completion and delivers free glucose that can leave the liver cell. Using a **separate enzyme** for the release direction also means uptake (hexokinase) and release (phosphatase) can be regulated independently, instead of one reversible reaction that would simply run back and forth. The "lost" phosphate-bond energy is the price of making the reaction go in one direction.

**Where marks are lost:** answering only "because it would cost energy" without using the sign of ΔG, or suggesting the liver could run the reaction backwards by adding more enzyme (an enzyme does not change ΔG).

Q: The energetic trajectory of a series of reactions in a catabolic pathway can be plotted on a graph like that in the figure. The graph shows a hypothetical pathway similar to glycolysis, in which the catabolic intermediates are designated A, B, C, etc. For the following questions, explain your answers.

![Free-energy profile of a hypothetical pathway A to G: A near 19 kcal/mol, uphill to B near 24, then C near 22, D near 18, a steep drop to E near 4, then F near 5 and G near 2, with an activation barrier between each pair; the barrier between F and G is the smallest](assets/img/slide-9-25.png)

**a)** Which reaction(s) is most likely to occur without an enzyme in the cell?

**b)** Which reaction(s) is most likely to be catalyzed by an enzyme that simultaneously uses ATP as a substrate?

**c)** Which reaction(s) is most likely to be catalyzed by an enzyme that simultaneously uses ADP as a substrate and produces ATP?

**d)** Many important molecules in cells are polymers. Which is thermodynamically more disordered, a polymer or a collection of monomers? On the basis of your answer, is the ΔG° of a polymerization reaction positive, negative, or zero?
A: **In brief:** a) **F → G**: downhill, with by far the smallest activation barrier. b) **A → B**: the only clearly uphill step (ΔG° about +5), so it must be coupled to ATP hydrolysis. c) **D → E**: a drop of about 14 kcal/mol, more than the ~7 needed to make ATP. d) The **monomers** are more disordered, so polymerisation lowers entropy and its **ΔG° is positive**.

**a) F → G.** It is **downhill** (ΔG° negative) and its **activation barrier is very low** (a couple of kcal/mol), so thermal energy alone can carry molecules over it at a useful rate. Every other step has a barrier of 5 kcal/mol or more above its starting point, and needs an enzyme to lower it.

**b) A → B.** It is the one step that is clearly **uphill**: B lies about 5 kcal/mol above A, so the reaction is unfavourable on its own. Coupling it to **ATP hydrolysis** (about −7.3 kcal/mol) makes the combined ΔG negative. This is like the energy-investment steps at the start of glycolysis.

**c) D → E.** It **releases about 14 kcal/mol**, more than the roughly **7.3 kcal/mol needed to make ATP** from ADP and Pᵢ. So the enzyme can couple this step to ATP synthesis and the combined reaction is still favourable. This is like the energy-generating steps of glycolysis. (The other downhill steps release too little to pay for an ATP.)

**d)** A **collection of monomers** is more disordered than a polymer, because the monomers can move independently while a polymer ties them together in a fixed order. Polymerisation therefore **decreases entropy**, and (being a condensation) is energetically unfavourable: its **ΔG° is positive**. That is why cells must couple polymerisation to ATP or another activated carrier.

**Where marks are lost:** choosing the step with the largest drop for (a); what decides whether a reaction needs an enzyme is the **activation barrier**, not ΔG.
