Q: Nuclear import and export of proteins larger than about 40 kDa both depend on the small GTPase **Ran**.

**a)** Describe the steps of nuclear import of a protein carrying a nuclear localisation signal (NLS).

**b)** Ran-GTP has opposite effects on importins and exportins. Explain how this, together with the location of RanGEF and RanGAP, gives both import and export their direction.

**c)** Predict what happens to nuclear import and export if RanGAP is experimentally redirected into the nucleus, so that Ran-GTP is no longer concentrated there.

**d)** Does the nuclear pore itself do work during transport? Where does the energy go?
A: **In brief:** **Importin** binds the NLS cargo, crosses the pore, and **Ran-GTP** in the nucleus releases the cargo; **RanGAP** in the cytosol recycles. **RanGEF (nucleus)** and **RanGAP (cytosol)** keep Ran-GTP inside: it breaks importin-cargo binding but is needed for exportin-cargo binding, giving both directions. RanGAP in the nucleus collapses the gradient: **both stop**. The pore does no work; the energy maintains the **gradient**.

**a)** (1) An **importin** binds the **NLS** of the cargo in the cytoplasm. (2) The importin-cargo complex binds the **FG repeats** of the nucleoporins and passes through the **nuclear pore complex**. (3) In the nucleus, **Ran-GTP binds the importin**, which **releases the cargo**. (4) The **importin-Ran-GTP** complex returns to the cytoplasm. (5) There **RanGAP** stimulates hydrolysis of Ran-GTP to **Ran-GDP**, releasing the importin for another round.

**b)** **RanGEF is in the nucleus** (it loads Ran with GTP) and **RanGAP is in the cytosol** (it makes Ran hydrolyse GTP), so Ran is **GTP-bound inside and GDP-bound outside**. For an **importin**, Ran-GTP **breaks** cargo binding, so cargo is released **in the nucleus**. For an **exportin**, Ran-GTP is **required** for cargo binding, so cargo is picked up in the nucleus and released in the **cytosol**, where Ran-GTP is destroyed. Direction is built into the **spatial separation of the two enzymes**, not into the pore or the receptors.

**c)** Ran-GTP would be converted to Ran-GDP in the nucleus, so the **gradient collapses**. Importins could no longer release their cargo efficiently in the nucleus and would not be recycled, so **import fails** and NLS proteins accumulate in the cytoplasm. Exportins could no longer bind cargo in the nucleus, so **export fails** too, and NES proteins accumulate in the nucleus. Both directions stop, because they run on the same gradient.

**d)** No. The **pore does no work**: cargo-receptor complexes cross it by diffusion, in both directions, through the FG-repeat mesh. The energy (GTP hydrolysis by Ran, and the GTP spent by RanGEF to reload it) is used to **maintain the Ran-GTP gradient**, which makes net movement go one way.

**Where marks are lost:** saying that Ran hydrolyses its GTP "by itself" (RanGAP is needed, and it is in the cytosol), or describing import and export as two different pathways rather than one gradient read in two ways.

Q: Mutations in the nuclear localisation signal of the transcription factor **FOXP2** are associated with a speech and language disorder.

**a)** Describe two independent experiments you would use to show that an NLS-mutant FOXP2 protein is mislocalised. For each, state what you would expect for the wild-type and the mutant protein.

**b)** In a nuclear/cytoplasmic fractionation, why is the first lysis carried out in a **hypotonic** buffer and the second in a **hypertonic** buffer?

**c)** Why should a control protein, such as the cytoplasmic protein VCP, be analysed on the same blot?

**d)** Explain why a mutation in the NLS of a transcription factor causes a complete loss of function, even if the protein folds normally. How does the effect of NLS mutations in FUS differ?
A: **In brief:** Show mislocalisation by **immunofluorescence** (nuclear versus cytoplasmic staining) and by **fractionation** and western blot. The **hypotonic** buffer bursts only the plasma membrane; the **hypertonic** (high-salt) buffer extracts chromatin-bound nuclear proteins. A **control protein** proves the fractions are clean. A transcription factor that cannot reach DNA is **inactive**; mislocalised FUS also **aggregates** (toxic gain).

**a)** (1) **Immunofluorescence**: stain FOXP2 (or express a GFP fusion) together with a nuclear stain (DAPI). **Wild-type** FOXP2 overlaps the DAPI-stained **nucleus**; the **NLS mutant** is found in the **cytoplasm**, outlining the whole cell and excluded from the nucleus. (2) **Nuclear/cytoplasmic fractionation and western blot**: wild-type FOXP2 is in the **nuclear extract**, the NLS mutant switches to the **cytoplasmic extract**. The two methods are complementary: imaging shows **where** in the cell the protein is, the blot shows **how much** is in each compartment.

**b)** The **hypotonic** buffer makes water enter the cell, so the cell swells and the **plasma membrane bursts**, while the **nuclei stay intact**; centrifugation then gives cytoplasm in the supernatant and nuclei in the pellet. The **hypertonic (high-salt)** buffer is used on the nuclei because nuclear proteins are bound to **chromatin**, and high salt is needed to compete them off (breaking electrostatic interactions) and extract them.

**c)** It shows that the **fractionation worked**: a cytoplasmic marker should appear only in the cytoplasmic fraction (and a nuclear marker only in the nuclear fraction). Without it, a protein appearing in the "wrong" fraction could be an artefact of nuclei breaking during lysis, rather than real mislocalisation.

**d)** A transcription factor can only act by binding **DNA in the nucleus**. If it cannot be imported, it is made and folded normally but never reaches its target genes, so it is **completely inactive**, however well it folds. **FUS** behaves differently: the mislocalised protein **aggregates in the cytoplasm**, a **toxic gain of function** (in ALS and frontotemporal dementia) in addition to its loss from the nucleus.

Q: A signalling protein X contains both a nuclear localisation signal (NLS) and a nuclear export signal (NES). In unstimulated cells it is found mainly in the cytoplasm.

**a)** Is X really a cytoplasmic protein? Explain what its steady-state location tells you and what is happening at the molecular level.

**b)** Cells are treated with a drug that blocks the exportin that recognises X's NES. Predict where X will be found, and what that tells you.

**c)** What amino acids characterise an NLS and an NES? In which compartment is each signal "active"?

**d)** How could you exploit NLS and NES sequences to study the function of a protein specifically in one compartment?
A: **In brief:** X **shuttles**: its location is the balance of import and export. Blocking export makes it **accumulate in the nucleus**, showing it is imported continuously. NLS = **basic** residues, read by importins in the cytoplasm; NES = **hydrophobic** pattern, read by exportins in the nucleus. Fusing an **NLS or NES** forces a protein into one compartment.

**a)** X is a **shuttling** protein: it moves **continuously in both directions**. Its **steady-state** distribution reflects the balance between import (NLS) and export (NES); mainly cytoplasmic means export currently wins, for example because the NLS is partly **masked** (by a partner protein or a modification). This is how a transcription factor can be held in the cytoplasm until a signal unmasks its NLS or masks its NES.

**b)** X will **accumulate in the nucleus**. This shows that X was being **imported all along** and was only kept cytoplasmic by continuous **export**. (If X stayed cytoplasmic, its NLS would be inactive in these conditions.)

**c)** A classical **NLS** is a cluster of **basic** residues (lysine, arginine), either **monopartite** (a single basic run) or **bipartite** (two basic clusters separated by a spacer). An **NES** is a spaced pattern of **hydrophobic** residues (leucine, isoleucine, valine, phenylalanine, methionine). The NLS is recognised by **importins** in the **cytoplasm**; the NES is recognised by **exportins** (with Ran-GTP) in the **nucleus**.

**d)** Signals are **transferable**. Fuse the protein (for example with **GFP** to see it) to an extra **NLS** to force it into the **nucleus**, or to an **NES** to keep it in the **cytoplasm**. Comparing the two tells you which function the protein performs in which compartment.

Q: Two fluorescently tagged membrane proteins, A and B, are studied by **fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP)**. For protein A, fluorescence in the bleached area recovers quickly to about 95% of its original value. For protein B, it recovers more slowly, and only to about 40%.

**a)** Explain the principle of FRAP and interpret the results for A and B.

**b)** Suggest a reason for the behaviour of protein B.

**c)** How did the experiment of Frye and Edidin, in which a mouse cell was fused with a human cell, first show that membrane proteins can move? Predict what would happen if the fused cells were kept at a low temperature.

**d)** What is the effect of cholesterol on the fluidity of a membrane, and why?
A: **In brief:** Recovery **rate** = diffusion speed, **plateau** = mobile fraction: A is fast and almost fully mobile, B is slower with about 60% **immobile** (anchored, for example to the cytoskeleton). Frye and Edidin saw mouse and human proteins **intermix by diffusion**, which slows in the cold. **Cholesterol** makes the membrane **less fluid**.

**a)** A fluorescent protein in the membrane is **bleached** with a strong laser pulse in a small area, destroying the fluorescence there. **Unbleached molecules diffuse in** from the surroundings and fluorescence **recovers**. The **rate** of recovery reflects the **diffusion coefficient** (how fast the mobile molecules move); the **plateau** reflects the **mobile fraction** (what proportion can move at all). Protein A diffuses **quickly** and almost all of it is **mobile**. Protein B diffuses **more slowly** and **about 60% is immobile**.

**b)** Much of B is **anchored**: for example bound to the **cytoskeleton** (via adaptor proteins), to the extracellular matrix, to other proteins in large complexes, or confined in **lipid rafts**. Its mobile part may also be slowed by these interactions.

**c)** Mouse and human membrane proteins were labelled with antibodies carrying different fluorophores. Immediately after fusion they sat in **separate halves** of the heterokaryon; at 37 °C they **intermixed** within tens of minutes, without any energy input or transport machine, showing that proteins **diffuse laterally** in a fluid membrane. At **low temperature** the membrane is **less fluid** and diffusion is much slower, so mixing would be slowed or blocked.

**d)** Cholesterol inserts between phospholipids with its hydroxyl near the head groups and its **rigid, flat steroid rings** against the first part of the hydrocarbon tails, **restricting their movement**, so it makes the bilayer **less fluid (more rigid)**. (Shorter tails and more *cis* double bonds, which prevent tight packing, increase fluidity.)

Q: Proteins can associate with a lipid bilayer in several different ways.

**a)** A protein crosses the membrane once with a stretch of about 20 hydrophobic amino acids. Why is this stretch almost always an α-helix?

**b)** Porins in the outer membrane of mitochondria and bacteria cross the membrane as a β-barrel instead. What does this architecture achieve?

**c)** Explain how each of the following is held at the membrane: (i) a GPI-anchored protein, (ii) cytochrome *c*, (iii) fibronectin bound to an integrin.

**d)** Which of these associations are easily reversible, and why does that matter for the cell?
A: **In brief:** An **α-helix** satisfies all backbone hydrogen bonds internally and exposes only hydrophobic side chains. A **β-barrel** closes the sheet and creates a **water-filled pore**. GPI-anchored: **covalent lipid**; cytochrome c: **electrostatic**; fibronectin: **bound to integrin**. Non-covalent attachments are **reversible**, so the cell can regulate them.

**a)** The polypeptide backbone is **polar**: each residue has an N–H and a C=O that would have no hydrogen-bond partner in the hydrocarbon core. Folding into an **α-helix** satisfies **every backbone hydrogen bond internally**, leaving only the side chains facing the lipids, and these can all be **hydrophobic**.

**b)** A β-sheet also has unsatisfied backbone hydrogen bonds at its edges; **closing the sheet into a barrel** satisfies them, with hydrophobic side chains facing the lipid. The barrel also creates a **water-filled channel** down the middle, which is exactly the function of a porin (letting small molecules through).

**c)** **(i)** Covalently attached to a **lipid (glycosylphosphatidylinositol) anchor** that inserts into the **extracellular** leaflet; the lipid part, not the sugars, is in the membrane. **(ii)** By **electrostatic attraction**: a positively charged patch on the protein binds negatively charged lipid head groups. **(iii)** Fibronectin **never touches the bilayer**: it is **membrane-associated**, held there by binding the transmembrane integrin, which in turn binds adaptor proteins and actin inside.

**d)** **Electrostatic** and non-specific hydrophobic associations (and protein-protein binding) are **weak and reversible**: they can be switched, for example by **phosphorylation** changing a protein's charge, or by release of cytochrome *c* during apoptosis. A **covalent lipid anchor** is not reversible on that timescale. Reversibility lets the cell **regulate** whether a protein is at the membrane.

Q: TGFβ/BMP signalling is important in embryonic development and in cancer.

**a)** Describe the steps from binding of the ligand at the cell surface to regulation of transcription in the nucleus.

**b)** For each component, indicate what type of membrane protein it is, or whether it is not a membrane protein.

**c)** The receptors and SARA are thought to sit together in **lipid rafts**. What are lipid rafts, and how could they help signalling?

**d)** The same activated Smad complex regulates different genes in different cell types. Suggest how.
A: **In brief:** Ligand binding makes **type I/II receptors** oligomerise and autophosphorylate; **SARA** presents the **R-Smad**, which is phosphorylated, joins **Smad4** and enters the nucleus with a **partner transcription factor**. Receptors: **transmembrane**; SARA: **membrane-associated**; Smads: cytosolic. **Lipid rafts** keep the components together. Different **partner TFs** give cell-specific responses.

**a)** (1) The **TGFβ/BMP ligand binds** the receptors, increasing the affinity of **type I and type II receptors** for each other. (2) The receptors **oligomerise** (two of each type). (3) The receptors are kinases and the complex **phosphorylates itself**. (4) **SARA** presents the **R-Smad** to the activated receptors, which **phosphorylate** it. (5) Phosphorylated R-Smads leave the receptor and **assemble with Smad4** (two R-Smads plus one Smad4). (6) The complex **enters the nucleus** and binds DNA together with a **partner transcription factor**, regulating transcription.

**b)** **Type I and II receptors:** **transmembrane** proteins with their own kinase (catalytic) domains. **SARA:** a **membrane-associated** protein, at the membrane before ligand binding. **Smurf1/2:** membrane-associated regulators. **R-Smads and Smad4:** cytosolic proteins that move to the nucleus; not membrane proteins. The **ligand** is extracellular.

**c)** Lipid rafts are **clusters of cholesterol and glycosphingolipids** that **move as units** within the fluid membrane. Proteins embedded in the same raft are held **close together**, so signalling components (receptors, SARA, R-Smads) meet much more often and faster than by free diffusion. Rafts can therefore **regulate signalling** by organising which proteins encounter each other.

**d)** The Smad complex does not act alone: it binds DNA **together with a separate partner transcription factor**, and **different cell types express different partners**. The same signal is therefore read in combination with each cell's own transcription factors, giving cell-type-specific outcomes.
