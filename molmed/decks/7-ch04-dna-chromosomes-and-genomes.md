Q: The 1953 model of DNA is usually summarised in four words. State them, and say what each one claims.
A: **Double-stranded**: two chains, not one. **Helical**: wound around a common axis. **Complementary**: the sequence of one chain dictates the other. **Anti-parallel**: the two chains run in opposite chemical directions.

Read more: [The 1953 model, and the evidence behind it](#/chapter/ch04-dna-chromosomes-and-genomes/the-1953-model-and-the-evidence-behind-it)

Q+: Which of the four claims came from X-ray diffraction rather than from model building?
A: **Helical**, together with the **dimensions** the model had to fit. Franklin's diffraction pattern is what constrained the geometry; complementarity came from the base-pairing chemistry once the geometry was fixed.

Q: A DNA strand has a chemical direction. What creates it, and why does it matter that it exists?
A: The **phosphodiester bond** joins the **5' carbon** of one sugar to the **3' carbon** of the next, so one end of a chain terminates in a free 5' position and the other in a free 3' position. Every enzyme that reads or copies DNA works in one direction only, so polarity is what makes "which way" a meaningful question.

![A phosphodiester bond joining the 5′ carbon of one sugar to the 3′ carbon of the next, with the 5′ end of the chain at the top and the 3′ end at the bottom](assets/img/fig-4-04.png)

Q: In the double helix, the sugar-phosphate backbones sit {{on the outside}} and the bases sit {{on the inside}}, with the two strands running {{anti-parallel}} to each other.
A: The backbone is charged and hydrophilic, so it faces the water; the bases are flat and hydrophobic, so they are buried and stacked in the core.

Q: A base pair is always a purine with a pyrimidine. What would go wrong if two purines paired, and what does the rule buy the molecule?
A: Two purines would be too wide and two pyrimidines too narrow, so the helix would bulge and pinch. Pairing one two-ring base with one one-ring base makes **every base pair the same width**, which is what lets the helix keep a **constant diameter** whatever the sequence.

![Hydrogen bonds forming between A and T and between G and C, and four base pairs viewed from the side of the helix lying perpendicular to its axis](assets/img/fig-4-05.png)

Q+: A and T are held together by {{two}} hydrogen bonds, G and C by {{three}}.
A: This is one of the two reasons GC-rich DNA is harder to melt than AT-rich DNA. The other is base stacking.

Q: Which of these stabilise the DNA duplex?
- [x] Hydrogen bonds between the paired bases, acting across the helix
- [x] Stacking interactions between successive base pairs, acting along the helix
- [ ] Covalent bonds between the two strands
- [ ] Hydrogen bonds between the two sugar-phosphate backbones
A: The two contributions act along **different axes**, which is why they are worth keeping apart. There are no covalent bonds between the strands, which is exactly why the duplex can be opened without breaking chemistry; and the two backbones repel each other, since both are negatively charged.

Read more: [What holds the two strands together](#/chapter/ch04-dna-chromosomes-and-genomes/what-holds-the-two-strands-together)
Tags: type/discriminate, structure, core

Q: True or false: GC base pairs stack better than AT base pairs because a G with a C has more aromatic rings than an A with a T.
- [ ] True
- [x] False
A: **Every** base pair is one purine plus one pyrimidine, so every base pair has the same **three** rings. Stacking energy differs because it depends on the **electronic character of the two stacked bases**, that is on the dinucleotide step, not on how many rings are present.

Read more: [What holds the two strands together](#/chapter/ch04-dna-chromosomes-and-genomes/what-holds-the-two-strands-together)

Q: Separating the two strands of DNA by heat alone takes temperatures far above anything a cell experiences. What follows from that for how a cell reads its genome?
A: The duplex is **thermodynamically stable at body temperature**, so nothing opens by itself. Every process that needs single-stranded DNA has to open it **enzymatically and locally**, paying with ATP, and close it again afterwards.

Read more: [What holds the two strands together](#/chapter/ch04-dna-chromosomes-and-genomes/what-holds-the-two-strands-together)
Tags: type/why, structure

Q: How can a protein recognise a specific DNA sequence without unwinding the helix?
A: Through the **grooves**. The edges of the base pairs face outward into the major and minor groove, and each base pair presents a **distinct pattern of hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors** there. The **major groove** carries the more informative pattern, so most sequence-specific binding is major-groove binding.

![Space-filling model of the double helix showing the major and minor grooves and a 2 nm scale bar](assets/img/fig-4-06.png)

Q: In an electron micrograph of a nucleus, which material is electron-dense, and what does its distribution tell you?
A: **Heterochromatin** is the dark, electron-dense material and **euchromatin** the lighter material. Heterochromatin is concentrated at the **nuclear periphery**, against the lamina, and around the nucleolus, so the compacted, transcriptionally quiet chromatin is spatially segregated from the accessible chromatin.

Q: Distinguish genic from intergenic DNA, and exon from intron.
A: **Genic** DNA lies within a gene, meaning the whole transcription unit including its introns; **intergenic** DNA lies between genes. Within a gene, an **exon** is retained in the mature RNA and an **intron** is spliced out.

Q+: Where do regulatory DNA sequences sit relative to the gene they control?
A: Often **well outside the transcription unit**, upstream of it or at a distance. They are not transcribed into the gene's RNA, which is why a variant in one changes expression without changing any protein.

Q: Zooming into a human chromosome in steps of tenfold, from the whole chromosome down to a single gene, what changes about what you see?
A: Genes turn out to be **sparse and unevenly spaced** along the arm, and a single gene turns out to be **mostly intron**, with short exons and a block of regulatory sequence. Coding sequence is a small minority of a gene, and genes are a small minority of the chromosome.

Q: Chromosome painting gives a karyotype directly. What is being painted, and what does a chromosome's number record?

Q+: Roughly 45% of the genome is transcribed, but only about 1% is protein-coding exon. What does that gap imply?
A: That **transcription is far more widespread than protein production**. Most transcription produces introns that are discarded and noncoding RNAs that are never translated, so "transcribed" is not a synonym for "codes for a protein".

Q+: About 3.5% of the genome is highly conserved without coding for protein. Why is conservation without coding interesting?
A: Sequence is only conserved across species if **selection is acting on it**, which means it does something. Conserved noncoding DNA is therefore evidence of **function without a protein product**, and most of it is regulatory.

Q: Decondensed chromatin looks like beads on a string. What are the beads and what is the string?
A: The beads are **nucleosomes**, each a protein core with DNA wrapped around it. The string between them is **linker DNA**, naked double helix running from one nucleosome to the next.

![Chromatin isolated from an interphase nucleus appearing as a thick thread, and the same chromatin experimentally decondensed into beads on a string](assets/img/fig-4-21.png)

Q: A nucleosome core particle can be taken apart in two steps. What does each step separate, and what does that prove about the particle?
A: A **nuclease** digests the exposed **linker** DNA and releases individual core particles, which shows the linker is accessible and the wrapped DNA is not. **High salt** then splits a core particle into a **histone octamer** and the DNA that was wrapped around it, which shows the interaction is largely **electrostatic**.

![Chromatin digested by nuclease to release nucleosome core particles, which dissociate at high salt into a histone octamer and 147 nucleotide pairs of DNA, and further into H2A, H2B, H3 and H4](assets/img/fig-4-22.png)

Q+: The core particle holds 147 nucleotide pairs, and the repeating unit along chromatin is about 200. What accounts for the difference?
A: The **linker DNA** between one nucleosome and the next. Only the wrapped portion is protected from nuclease, so roughly a quarter of chromatin's DNA is exposed linker at any moment.

Q: The nucleosome core is called an octamer. Does that mean eight different histone proteins?
- [ ] Yes, eight distinct core histones
- [x] No, four kinds in two copies each
A: The core is **two H2A-H2B dimers flanking one (H3-H4)₂ tetramer**: four kinds of core histone, **H2A, H2B, H3 and H4**, present twice each. The symmetry matters, because it is what lets a remodeller swap one H2A-H2B dimer without dismantling the whole particle.

Q: Each core histone has a histone fold and an N-terminal tail. Which part does which job?
A: The **histone fold** is the structured part that does the **packing**: two folds interlock into a dimer, and the dimers assemble into the octamer. The **N-terminal tail** is unstructured and **protrudes** from the particle, doing nothing structural, which is precisely what makes it available to other proteins.

![The four core histones each drawn with an N-terminal tail and a histone fold, the fold itself, the H2A-H2B handshake dimer, and the assembled octamer with all eight tails protruding](assets/img/fig-4-24.png)

Q: Why do histones bind DNA so tightly in the first place?
A: DNA's backbone is strongly **negatively charged**. Histones are unusually rich in **lysine and arginine** and so carry a large **positive** charge. The interaction is largely **electrostatic**, and the neutralisation is what allows DNA to be bent tightly around the core without the adjacent turns of the wrap repelling each other.

Read more: [The histone fold, and the tails](#/chapter/ch04-dna-chromosomes-and-genomes/the-histone-fold-and-the-tails)
Tags: type/why, chromatin, core

---

Q+: Predict the effect on that grip of acetylating a tail lysine.
A: Acetylation **removes the lysine's positive charge**, so the tail's electrostatic attraction to the DNA backbone weakens and chromatin **loosens**. This is the direct physical route from a chemical mark to accessibility, and it is why acetylation is an activating mark.

Read more: [The histone fold, and the tails](#/chapter/ch04-dna-chromosomes-and-genomes/the-histone-fold-and-the-tails)
Tags: type/apply, chromatin, core

Q: Histone H1 is not part of the octamer. What is it and what does it do?
A: It is the **linker histone**, roughly one per nucleosome, and it binds where the DNA **enters and leaves** the core particle. By clamping those two points it fixes the angle at which DNA leaves the bead and seals an extra stretch of linker against the core.

![Histone H1 bound at the point where DNA enters and leaves the nucleosome, constraining the exit path of the DNA](assets/img/fig-4-30.png)

Q+: At which step of chromatin packing does H1 become necessary, and why there?
A: At the step from **beads-on-a-string to the 30 nm fibre**. Fixing the DNA's exit angle is what lets neighbouring nucleosomes pack against each other in a regular way; without it the string stays extended.

Read more: [Histone H1, and what it does](#/chapter/ch04-dna-chromosomes-and-genomes/histone-h1-and-what-it-does)

Q: Put the levels of chromatin packing in order, from naked DNA to the mitotic chromosome.
A: **Double helix** (2 nm), **beads-on-a-string** (11 nm), **chromatin fibre of associated nucleosomes** (30 nm, requiring histone H1), **chromatin fibre folded into loops** (700 nm), **entire mitotic chromosome** (1400 nm). Net result: a DNA molecule roughly **10,000-fold shorter** than its extended length.

![The levels of chromatin packing from the 2 nm double helix through beads-on-a-string and the 30 nm fibre to the 1400 nm mitotic chromosome, annotated with the point at which histone H1 is added and a bracket marking the higher orders as structurally unknown](assets/img/slide-7-15.png)

Q+: Which levels of that hierarchy are established structure, and which are models?
A: Everything up to and including **beads-on-a-string** is established. From the **30 nm fibre upward** the exact structure is **unknown**, which the lecture marks explicitly on the diagram. The textbook picture looks equally confident at every level, and it should not.

Q: Chromatin has to solve two opposite problems at once. What are they?
A: **Compaction**: a metre of DNA per cell has to fit in a nucleus a few micrometres across, and has to be condensed thousands of times further still to be segregated at mitosis. **Accessibility**: the same DNA has to be readable, base by base, by polymerases and repair enzymes. A structure that only compacted would be useless, which is why the packaging is regulated rather than fixed.

Read more: [From 2 nm to 1400 nm](#/chapter/ch04-dna-chromosomes-and-genomes/from-2-nm-to-1400-nm)
Tags: type/why, chromatin, core

Q: Why does a cell need a motor protein to expose DNA that is wrapped in a nucleosome?
A: Because the nucleosome is **very stable**: the histone-DNA contacts are numerous and largely electrostatic, so the particle does not come apart or slide spontaneously on any useful timescale. Moving it is mechanical work, and an **ATP-dependent chromatin remodelling complex** is the machine that does it.

Read more: [What a remodeller can do](#/chapter/ch04-dna-chromosomes-and-genomes/what-a-remodeller-can-do)

- [ ] Closing is spontaneous, since the compact state is the low-energy one
- [x] Closing costs ATP as well
A: Remodelling is **reversible but not free in either direction**. What is being moved either way is a tightly bound protein-DNA complex, so both opening and closing are catalysed and both are paid for.

Q+: What is a histone chaperone for?
A: Free histones are highly basic and would **stick to any DNA they met** or aggregate. A chaperone **holds them in a soluble, unbound state** and hands them over where they are needed, so eviction and deposition can be controlled rather than accidental.

Q: Promoters of active genes usually sit in a nucleosome-free region. Why does that arrangement matter, and how is it made?
A: The general transcription machinery needs **naked promoter DNA** to assemble on, so clearing the nucleosome is what makes a promoter usable at all. The clearing is done by a **remodeller**, by sliding or evicting the nucleosome, and putting one back is a way of shutting the gene down.

Q: A remodeller never lets go of the DNA while moving it. How does it shift DNA around the histone core?
A: It **anchors** on the histone core, then uses a **DNA translocase** to pull DNA in from one side. The DNA cannot slide smoothly against the core, so it buckles into a **bulge**, or loop, on the surface of the particle. Propagating that bulge around and out the far side moves DNA relative to the histones without releasing it: anchoring, uncoiling, bulge formation, bulge migration.

![The lecturer's composite of remodeller mechanism: anchoring, DNA uncoiling, bulge formation and bulge migration by a translocase with a DNA-binding domain, and the two outcomes of eviction with insertion elsewhere or sliding along the DNA](assets/img/slide-7-17.png)

Read more: [How the motor does it](#/chapter/ch04-dna-chromosomes-and-genomes/how-the-motor-does-it)
Tags: type/why, remodelling, core

Q+: Sliding and eviction are the two outcomes of that mechanism. What does each one achieve for a gene?
A: **Sliding** repositions the nucleosome on the same DNA, so sequence that was wrapped becomes exposed and something else becomes covered. **Eviction** removes the octamer altogether, which may then be deposited elsewhere, leaving a stretch of DNA fully accessible.

Q: Chd7 is a single very large polypeptide carrying a chromodomain pair, an SNF2 helicase domain, a SANT domain and two further domains. What is the architectural principle?
A: **Targeting and catalysis are separate modules on one chain.** The chromodomains **read** a histone mark and decide *where*, SANT **holds** histones, and the SNF2 helicase domain is the **motor** that moves nucleosomes. Decoupling the two is how the same chemistry is aimed at different parts of the genome by different members of the family.

Q+: CHARGE affects the eye, heart, trachea and esophagus, genitals and ears. Why would losing a general-purpose chromatin remodeller give such specific defects instead of killing the cell?

Q: Of all twenty amino acids, lysine carries most of the histone code. What makes it suitable?
A: Its side chain is a **long flexible arm ending in a positively charged amino group**, sitting out in solution on an unstructured tail where an enzyme can reach it. It is both **chemically reactive** and **physically accessible**, and its charge is directly involved in gripping DNA, so modifying it changes something real.

Q: Acetylation and methylation both land on lysine. What is the key chemical difference between them?
A: **Acetylation neutralises the lysine's positive charge**, so it directly loosens the histone's grip on DNA. **Methylation leaves the charge intact** and instead changes the **shape and hydrophobicity** of the surface, so its effect is entirely a matter of which reader protein binds it.

Q+: Acetylation and methylation are usually presented as competing reactions on the same lysine, so that writing one excludes the other. What recent finding complicates that?
A: That a lysine can carry **both at once**, as **acetyl-methyllysine**, and that this combined state marks chromatin at **active transcription start sites**. "Competing" is the clean textbook version; the real chemistry of a tail residue is richer than a binary switch.

Q: Besides lysine, which residue is commonly modified on histone tails, and what does the modification add?
A: **Serine**, by **phosphorylation** to phosphoserine. Unlike acetylation, which removes positive charge, phosphorylation **adds negative charge** to the tail.

Q: Why is the set of histone tail modifications called a code rather than a switch?
A: Because of the **combinatorial space**. Four types of modification, at dozens of positions, across eight tails per nucleosome, gives far more distinguishable states than on and off, and reader complexes bind **combinations** rather than single marks.

Q: Match each mark to what it means: H3K4me3, H3K9me3, H3K27me3.

Q+: Methylation of histone H3 activates at one position and silences at two others. What does that prove about how a mark works?
A: That the methyl group is **not acting chemically on the chromatin**. If it were, position would not change the outcome. A mark is a **binding site**, and what it means is decided entirely by **which reader protein recognises it**.

Q+: Phosphorylation of H3 serine 10 together with acetylation of H3 lysine 14 is associated with which state?
A: **Gene expression.** It is a second activating combination alongside H3K4me with H3K9ac, and a reminder that it is combinations, not single residues, that carry meaning.

Q: **Table 4–2.** Activating marks cover about 1% of the genome each; the silencing marks H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 cover tens of percent. What does that asymmetry say about how a genome is regulated?
A: That the **default state is off**. Being expressed is the exception that has to be specified:

| Mark | Chromatin | Expression | Extent |
|---|---|---|---|
| H3K4me3 | Highly accessible, open | ON | Rare, sharply localised |
| H3K9ac | Highly accessible, open | ON | Rare, sharply localised |
| H3K9me3 | Heterochromatin, constitutive or facultative | OFF | Broad domains |
| H3K27me3 | Facultative heterochromatin | OFF | Broad domains |

Q: A reader complex is a scaffold protein carrying several binding modules. Why build it that way rather than using a single module per mark?
A: Because it makes recognition **combinatorial**. Each module binds one mark weakly, so the complex binds stably only where **the right combination of marks is present at once**. A handful of chemically simple marks can then specify far more states than a handful, and the readout gains specificity the individual marks do not have.

![A reader complex built from several modules, each recognizing a different histone mark, binding only where the right combination is present and attracting further protein complexes](assets/img/fig-4-38.png)

---

Q+: Once a reader complex is bound, what happens next?
A: It **attracts further components**: complexes carrying catalytic activities and additional binding sites. The outcome, gene expression, gene silencing or something else, depends on **what gets recruited**, not on the reader itself.

Q: Trace the four steps by which a mark is nucleated at one place and then spreads along a chromosome.
A: A **gene regulatory protein** binds a specific **DNA sequence**; it recruits a **writer**, a histone-modifying enzyme, which marks a neighbouring nucleosome; a **code-reader protein** binds that mark; reader and writer associate into a **reader-writer complex**, which binds an existing mark and writes the same mark on the next nucleosome along.

![The slide showing a gene regulatory protein recruiting a histone modifying enzyme, the mark it writes being bound by a code-reader protein, and reader and writer together forming a complex that marks the next nucleosome along](assets/img/slide-7-29.png)

Read more: [Spreading](#/chapter/ch04-dna-chromosomes-and-genomes/spreading)
Tags: type/sequence, histone-code, core

---

Q+: Which single step in that chain is sequence-specific, and why does that matter?
A: Only the **first**: the gene regulatory protein binding its DNA sequence. Everything after it reads histones, not DNA. So **sequence decides where a domain starts**, and chromatin decides how far it goes, which is how a sequence-blind mechanism still ends up in the right place.

Read more: [Spreading](#/chapter/ch04-dna-chromosomes-and-genomes/spreading)
Tags: type/why, histone-code, core

---

Q+: Why does the spreading no longer need the transcription factor once the reader-writer complex has formed?
A: Because the **product of the reaction is what recruits the next round of it**. The complex binds the mark it has just written and writes the same mark one nucleosome further on, moving hand over hand. It is self-propagating, which is also why it has to be stopped from outside.

Read more: [Spreading](#/chapter/ch04-dna-chromosomes-and-genomes/spreading)
Tags: type/why, histone-code, core

---

Q+: A reader-writer complex can also recruit an ATP-dependent remodelling complex. What does that add?
A: The nucleosomes are **repositioned as they are marked**, so the chemical mark and the physical compaction advance together as a **spreading wave of chromatin condensation** rather than marks being laid down on an unchanged fibre.

![The slide showing the reader-writer complex joined by an ATP-dependent chromatin remodelling complex, with each round of ATP hydrolysis producing a spreading wave of chromatin condensation](assets/img/slide-7-30.png)

Q: Nothing in the reader-writer mechanism is self-limiting. What stops a heterochromatin domain from swallowing the whole chromosome?
A: **Barrier DNA sequences**, which recruit **barrier proteins** that block the advancing complex. Since the spreading machinery has no stop signal of its own, the stop signal has to be **encoded in the DNA**, and the barrier is what **defines the boundary** between a heterochromatin domain and the euchromatin next to it.

![A reader-writer complex recruited to a site, marking neighbouring nucleosomes and moving hand over hand until it reaches a barrier DNA sequence, and a reader-eraser complex removing the marks again](assets/img/fig-4-39.png)

Q+: Which of these are mechanisms by which a barrier protein blocks the spread?

- [x] Binding a group of nucleosomes tightly enough that the complex cannot reach them
- [x] Recruiting enzymes that erase the marks heterochromatin needs
- [ ] Cutting the DNA to physically separate the two domains
A: One barrier holds the fibre in place, one blocks access sterically, and one reverses the chemistry as fast as it arrives. Cutting the chromosome is not an option: a break would be repaired or lost.

Q: Every writer has an eraser. What does that imply about the chromatin state at any given point in the genome?
A: That it is a **steady-state balance between two opposing activities**, not a permanent record. A domain persists only as long as writing outpaces erasing, which is what makes chromatin states **reversible** and lets a cell change them when it is told to.

Q: A neuron, a cardiomyocyte and a dermal fibroblast carry identical genomes. Where does the difference between them live?
A: In **which parts of the sequence are accessible to be read**, meaning in the **chromatin**. Around three hundred human cell types share one genome, so cell identity cannot be a property of the sequence; it is a property of how that sequence is packaged and marked.

Q+: Why does a eukaryote need so much more of this machinery than a bacterium?
A: A much **larger genome**, mostly noncoding, has to be expressed differently in **hundreds of stable cell states** that persist through division. That needs a mechanism able to **address whole regions**, **hold a state through replication**, and be **set differently in each lineage**, which is more than sequence-specific repressors and activators alone can do.

Q: What was ENCODE set up to do, and what was its central conclusion?
A: To **map the functional elements and regions of the human genome**, by running a battery of genome-wide assays across many human cell types and tissues. Its conclusion: the resulting **map of functional elements is a "signature" of the identity of a cell**.

Q+: Which assay maps histone modifications, and which maps accessible chromatin?

Q+: What is the strong claim hidden in the word "signature"?

- [x] The variant may act in only one tissue, which the study does not identify

Q: **Exam-shaped question.** "Explain how chromatin allows one genome to specify several hundred different cell types, and why this makes chromatin the right place to look for the causes of common disease."
A: A checklist for marks:

| Point | Content |
|---|---|
| The problem | All cell types carry identical DNA, so identity cannot be encoded in the sequence |
| Packaging | DNA is wrapped in nucleosomes, octamers of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4, and folded into higher orders; wrapped DNA is not accessible |
| Accessibility is regulated | ATP-dependent remodellers slide, evict or exchange nucleosomes; promoters of active genes sit in nucleosome-free regions |
| Marking | Histone tails carry acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation and ubiquitylation; the combination is a code |
| The code is a binding site | H3K4me3 means active promoter, H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 mean silencing; the same chemistry at different positions means opposite things, so meaning comes from the reader |
| Domains | Sequence-specific factors nucleate marks; reader-writer complexes spread them; barrier sequences bound them; erasers keep the state reversible |
| Cell identity | Different lineages contain different regulatory proteins, so different regions are marked and accessible; the resulting map is a signature of the cell |
| Evidence | ENCODE mapped transcription, marks, factor binding, accessibility and 3D contacts across many cell types; chromatin states recover promoters, enhancers and insulators from marks alone |
| Clinical payoff | Most disease-associated variants are noncoding; active regulatory regions are a small fraction of the genome but carry the large majority of them |
| The method that follows | Accessibility across cell types identifies which variant is causal and which tissue it acts in |
