Q: The lecture defines a cell in five words. What are the two things a cell must contain?
A: **Information**, and the **machinery to interpret and reproduce it**. Everything else in the lecture hangs off that pairing: DNA is the information, proteins are the machinery, and the two make each other.
Tags: core

---

Q: How many cells are in a human body, and how many cell types, on the lecture's numbers?
A: About **3 × 10¹³ cells**, of roughly **325 cell types**.
Tags: numbers

---

Q+: Which cell type dominates that count, and by how much?
A: **Erythrocytes, 84%**: 25 × 10¹² of a 30 × 10¹² total. Platelets are 4.9%, bone marrow cells 2.5%, vascular endothelial cells 2.1% and lymphocytes 1.5%.
Tags: numbers

Q: The lecture states four universal features of life on Earth, one per slide. What is the first?
A: **All cells store their hereditary information in the form of double-stranded DNA molecules**, built as a sugar-phosphate backbone with hydrogen-bonded base pairs, built from **adenine, guanine, cytosine and thymine**.

![A nucleotide built from phosphate, sugar and base, and a stretch of double-strand DNA showing A-T and G-C base pairing](assets/img/fig-1-02.png)
Tags: universals

---

Q+: The second?
A: **All cells duplicate their hereditary information by templated polymerization.** The parent helix opens, each strand acts as a template, and the result is two identical daughter helices, which is **replication**.

![The two strands of a DNA double helix pulled apart, each serving as a template for a new complementary strand, producing two identical daughter double helices](assets/img/fig-1-03.png)
Tags: universals

---

Q+: The third?
A: **All cells transcribe portions of their DNA into RNA.** On the lecture's own diagram: **DNA synthesis = replication**, **RNA synthesis = transcription**, **protein synthesis = translation**.

![From DNA to protein: DNA replicates itself, is transcribed into RNA, and RNA is translated into protein](assets/img/fig-1-04.png)
Tags: universals

---

Q+: And the fourth?
A: **All cells use proteins as catalysts.** A protein's specific shape is dictated by its sequence, and that shape makes the catalytic site.
Tags: universals

---

Q: Which protein does the lecture use to show that a protein's shape makes its function, and what does it do on the slide?
A: **Lysozyme.** The slide shows its **catalytic site** as a notch in the folded structure, then the cycle: it binds a **polysaccharide chain**, cuts it, and releases the two products.
Tags: proteins

---

Q: "Life as an autocatalytic process": what are the two halves of the loop?
A: **DNA and RNA provide sequence information** used to build proteins, and **proteins provide the catalytic activity** needed to make more DNA, RNA and protein. Neither half works alone, and closing the loop is what makes a cell a **self-replicating collection of catalysts**.

![Life as an autocatalytic process: DNA and RNA provide sequence information used to build proteins, and proteins provide the catalytic activity needed to build DNA, RNA and more protein, closing the loop](assets/img/fig-1-05.png)
Tags: core, universals

---

Q: The cell in the lecture's cartoon takes in **{{food}}** and puts out **{{waste}}**, and its collection of catalysts has to reproduce **{{the entire collection}}** before the cell divides.
A: This is the slide's definition of the cell as a self-replicating collection of catalysts: the collection copies itself, not just the DNA.
Tags: core

Q: How does the lecture show that a plasma membrane forms by itself?
A: With phospholipids at an **oil–water interface**: they line up as a **monolayer** with heads in the water, and in bulk water they close into a **bilayer vesicle**, tails inward, heads facing water on both sides. **Nutrients and waste can pass.**

![A phospholipid has a hydrophilic head and hydrophobic tails, and in water these molecules spontaneously form a closed bilayer vesicle with heads facing the water on both sides](assets/img/fig-1-06.png)
Tags: membranes

![Electron micrograph of a cross section through the small bacterium Mycoplasma genitalium](assets/img/fig-1-08.png)

Q: Reconstruct the rest of the "Summary so far" slide. Which are on it?
- [x] An individual cell is the minimal self-reproducing unit of living matter
- [x] Transmission of information to progeny: DNA
- [x] The function of a protein is determined by its amino acid sequence, specified by the DNA sequence
- [x] The biochemistry of every living cell is fundamentally similar
- [ ] Every cell type has its own distinct biochemistry
A: The last one is the opposite of the lecture's point: the *biochemistry* is shared, and the differences between cells come from which parts of the shared genome are used.

Q: The lecture draws the tree of life twice. What is the **classical** tree based on?
A: **Evolutionary relationships inferred from development and morphology.** It splits the animals into **Deuterostomia** (chordates, echinoderms) and **Protostomia** (arthropods, nematodes, molluscs, annelids, flatworms), which diverged about **700 million years ago**, with sponges and cnidarians below the split.
Tags: evolution

---

Q+: And the modern tree?
A: **Genome and rRNA sequence comparison**, which gives the **three domains**: **Bacteria**, **Archaea** and **Eukaryotes**. Much of it comes from **genome sequencing of environmental samples** rather than from cultured organisms.

![A global tree of life based on genome comparisons, with branch lengths proportional to genome differences, showing bacteria, archaea and eukaryotes](assets/img/fig-1-09.png)
Tags: evolution

---

Q: Why does the lecturer call the archaea "intermediates"?
A: They **resemble eukaryotes in their genetics** and **bacteria in their metabolism**. On the tree, the eukaryotes emerge from within the archaea, next to the **Asgard** group.
Tags: evolution, core

---

Q: Orangutan, gorilla, chimpanzee and bonobo all have **{{48}}** chromosomes; humans have **{{46}}**. The difference is explained by a **{{fusion}}** that produced human chromosome 2.
A: Human chromosome 2 corresponds to chimpanzee chromosomes **12 and 13**: the banding of its **p arm** matches chimp 12 and the **q arm** matches chimp 13.
Tags: evolution, genomes

---

Q+: What two pieces of evidence on the slide show that human chromosome 2 is a fusion?
A: There is **telomeric DNA in the middle of the chromosome**, where two ancestral ends were joined, and the position of its **centromere matches that of chimp chromosome 12** (the second, inactivated centromere being lost).
Tags: evolution, genomes

---

Q: On the lecture's primate tree, when did the human line separate from the other great apes?
A: Orangutan ~**13 Mya**, gorilla ~**8 Mya**, chimpanzee and bonobo ~**6 Mya**; chimp and bonobo split from each other ~**3 Mya**.
Tags: evolution, numbers

Q: The lecture aligns the same stretch of rRNA from three organisms. Which, and what is the point?
A: **Human**, ***Methanococcus*** (an archaeon) and ***E. coli***. Most positions are still identical **after 3.5 billion years of evolution**, which is exactly why this gene is used to build the tree of life.

![A stretch of ribosomal RNA gene sequence aligned across a human, an archaeon and a bacterium, with most positions identical, showing how little this gene has changed since the last universal common ancestor](assets/img/fig-1-17.png)
Tags: evolution

Q: What is the quaternary structure of adult haemoglobin, as drawn on the globin slide?
A: **Two α-globin and two β-globin chains (α₂β₂)**, each carrying a **heme** group.

![Hemoglobin as a symmetrical assembly of two alpha-globin and two beta-globin subunits, each carrying a heme group](assets/img/fig-3-20.png)
Tags: globins

Q: Where do the human globin genes sit, and when did they arise?
A: Two clusters: **α1, α2 and ζ on chromosome 16**, and **ε, Gγ, Aγ, δ and β on chromosome 11**. On the slide's tree, **myoglobin** split from the haemoglobin line about **1,100 Mya**, the α and β clusters separated about **500 Mya**, and duplications inside the β cluster are much more recent, at roughly **200, 100, 40 and 30 Mya**.
Tags: globins, evolution

---

Q: In the fetal-to-adult haemoglobin switch, what decides which globin gene is expressed?
A: **Which gene the locus control region (LCR) loops onto.** In the fetal stage the LCR contacts **Gγ and Aγ** and the adult genes are repressed; in the adult stage it contacts **δ and β** and the fetal genes are repressed.
Tags: globins, regulation

---

Q+: What do the expression curves on that slide do, and when?
A: **ε** falls away during the embryonic stage; **γ** runs at nearly 100% through the fetal stage; **β** crosses over it **at around birth**; **δ** stays minor throughout. Fetal haemoglobin is **α₂γ₂**, adult is **α₂β₂**.
Tags: globins

Q: Which is which: paralogs and orthologs?
- [x] Paralogs arise by gene duplication within one genome
- [x] Orthologs arise by speciation, one copy in each species
- [ ] Paralogs arise by speciation
- [ ] Orthologs must have different functions from each other
A: Both are homologs, meaning genes with a common ancestor. α-globin and β-globin are paralogs; the human and mouse copies of a gene are orthologs, and orthologs usually keep the same job.

![Orthologs arise from speciation, paralogs arise from gene duplication within one genome](assets/img/fig-1-20.png)
Tags: evolution, core

---

Q: How many gene families are common to all three domains of life, and what are they used for?
A: **More than 200.** Because they are universal, they are how the function of a **newly identified gene** is deduced: find the family, infer the job.
Tags: genomes

---

Q+: Which two categories dominate that universal core, with what numbers?
A: **Translation: 63 families**, and **amino acid transport and metabolism: 43**, which is to say information processing and metabolism. By contrast **cell-cycle control** gets 2, **cell wall/membrane biogenesis** 2 and **signal-transduction mechanisms** just 1, because those were largely built separately in each domain.
Tags: genomes, numbers

Q: From Table 1–2 on the slide, match the genome sizes: *E. coli*, yeast, human.
A: ***E. coli*** **4.6 × 10⁶** nucleotide pairs (~4,300 genes); **yeast 12.5 × 10⁶** (~6,600 genes); **human 3,100 × 10⁶** (~20,000 genes).
Tags: genomes, numbers

---

Q+: Which organism on that table has the most protein-coding genes, and what is the lesson?
A: ***Arabidopsis thaliana***, with about **27,000** in a genome of 135 × 10⁶ nucleotide pairs: more genes than a human, in a genome 23 times smaller. **Genome size is not complexity, and gene count is not complexity either.** (*C. elegans* 20,000, *Drosophila* 14,000, zebrafish 26,000, mouse 20,000.)
Tags: genomes, core

Q: What does the log-scale genome size chart show about eukaryotes?
A: **Eukaryotic genomes are big, but the variation inside a group is larger than the difference between groups.** Protozoans run from the malarial parasite (~10⁷) to amoebae (>10¹¹); amphibians include both the frog and the newt, two orders of magnitude apart; mammals, birds and reptiles are a narrow band around 10⁹.

![Genome sizes compared on a log scale](assets/img/fig-1-30.png)
Tags: genomes

Q: Why does the lecture show an electron micrograph of a budding yeast cell?
A: Because one small round cell contains the whole eukaryotic inventory: **cell wall, Golgi stack, nucleus, mitochondrion, ribosomes** and **plasma membrane**. *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* is a eukaryote with everything present and nothing extra.

![Scanning electron micrograph of budding yeast cells beside an electron micrograph of a cross section showing the thick cell wall and intracellular organelles](assets/img/fig-1-39.png)
