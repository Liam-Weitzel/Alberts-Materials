Q: A cell has to contain two things to be a cell at all. What are they, and why does neither work alone?
A: **Information** and the **machinery to interpret and reproduce it**. Information with no machinery cannot be read or copied; machinery with no information cannot be rebuilt in the next generation. A cell is the smallest object that holds both.

Read more: [Cells](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/cells)
Tags: type/why, core

Q: A human body contains a vast number of cells but only a few hundred distinct cell types. Why is the number of types the more informative figure?
A: Because every one of those cells carries the **same genome**, so the types cannot differ in the information they hold. They differ in **which genes they express**, so a count of types is a count of the **distinct expression programmes** one genome can run.

Read more: [Cells](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/cells)
Tags: type/why, core

Q+: The cell count is dominated by one small cell type that has no nucleus at all. Which, and what does that do to the usefulness of a raw cell count?
A: **Erythrocytes**, well over three quarters of the total. A figure dominated by the smallest and simplest cell in the body tells you little about the body's biology, which is why cell number is a poor proxy for anything that matters.

Q+: Muscle is a large fraction of body weight but only 0.001% of the cell count. What does that tell you about what such a breakdown measures?
A: That it counts **cells, not mass**. A muscle fibre is enormous and multinucleate; an erythrocyte is tiny and has no nucleus at all. Cell number and tissue mass are different measures, and neither tells you about the amount of DNA.

Read more: [Cells](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/cells)

Q: All cells store hereditary information as {{double-stranded DNA}}, duplicate it by {{templated polymerization}}, transcribe parts of it into {{RNA}}, and use {{proteins}} as catalysts.
A: These are the four universal features of life on Earth, in the order they are usually taught.

Read more: [The universal features of life on Earth](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/the-universal-features-of-life-on-earth)

Q: Why does a double-stranded molecule make a better information store than a single-stranded one?
A: Because each strand **specifies the other**. That makes copying a matter of templated polymerization rather than of measurement, and it means a damaged strand can be rebuilt from its partner.

Read more: [Genetic information](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/genetic-information)
Tags: type/why, core

---

Q: DNA replication is described as "templated polymerization". What are the two daughter molecules like, and why?
A: Each is **one old strand paired with one new strand**, because the parent helix is opened and **both** strands act as templates at once. The two daughters are identical to each other and to the parent.

Read more: [Genetic information](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/genetic-information)
Tags: type/why, core

---

Q: Name the three processes that copy DNA, read it into RNA and turn RNA into protein, with the synthesis each performs.
A: **Replication** (DNA synthesis), **transcription** (RNA synthesis) and **translation** (protein synthesis).

Read more: [The universal features of life on Earth](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/the-universal-features-of-life-on-earth)

Q: What does lysozyme do to a polysaccharide chain, and what general principle does that stand for?
A: Lysozyme binds a **polysaccharide chain**, cuts it, and releases the two products, using a **catalytic site** formed by its fold. The principle: **a protein's specific shape is dictated by its sequence**, and the shape is what makes the catalysis possible.

![Lysozyme with its catalytic site marked, binding and cutting a polysaccharide chain](assets/img/slide-1a-17.png)

Read more: [Genetic information](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/genetic-information)
Tags: type/figure, core

Q: Life is described as an autocatalytic process, a loop with two arms. What does each arm carry?
A: **Sequence information**, from DNA and RNA to the proteins built from it; and **catalytic activity**, from proteins back to the synthesis of nucleotides, DNA, RNA and more protein. The loop is why a cell is a **self-replicating collection of catalysts** rather than a bag of molecules.

![Life as an autocatalytic process, with sequence information and catalytic activity forming a loop](assets/img/slide-1a-19.png)

Read more: [Life as an autocatalytic process](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/life-as-an-autocatalytic-process)
Tags: type/figure, type/why, core

---

Q+: Before a cell divides, the *entire collection* of catalysts has to be reproduced. Why is that a stronger requirement than copying the DNA?
A: Because the DNA is inert without the machinery that reads it. A daughter cell needs both halves of the loop, so replication of the genome is necessary but not sufficient: the collection of catalysts has to be doubled too.

Read more: [Life as an autocatalytic process](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/life-as-an-autocatalytic-process)
Tags: type/why

Q: In water, phospholipids form a closed bilayer vesicle rather than a monolayer. Why the difference at an oil-water interface?
A: At the **oil-water interface** each molecule can satisfy both ends at once: heads in the water, tails in the oil, so a **monolayer** suffices. In bulk water there is nowhere for the tails to go, so two layers pair tail-to-tail and the sheet **closes on itself** to leave no exposed edge.

![Phospholipids forming a monolayer at an oil-water interface and a bilayer vesicle in water](assets/img/slide-1a-20.png)

Read more: [Cells](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/cells)
Tags: type/why, type/figure, core

Q+: What has to be true of that membrane for the cell to live?
A: **Nutrients and waste materials have to be able to pass.** A perfectly sealed vesicle is as dead as no vesicle at all.

Read more: [Cells](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/cells)

Q: What is the significance of a minimal cell such as *Mycoplasma* or a synthetic stripped-down genome?
A: It puts a **floor under what it costs to be alive**. Running the chemistry of DNA, RNA and protein takes a few hundred genes at minimum, because below that the **autocatalytic loop** of information and catalysts can no longer close on itself.

Read more: [Mycoplasma](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/mycoplasma)

Q: Which of these statements about cells, DNA and proteins are correct?
- [x] An individual cell is the minimal self-reproducing unit of living matter
- [x] So the genome determines the biochemistry of a cell
- [x] The biochemistry of every living cell is fundamentally similar
- [ ] Each cell type runs its own distinct biochemistry
- [ ] RNA, not DNA, transmits information to progeny
A: The chain of reasoning runs: DNA transmits information, proteins are the catalysts, protein function follows from amino acid sequence, sequence is specified by DNA, therefore the genome determines the biochemistry.

Q: The tree of life can be drawn two ways. What is each version based on?
A: The **classical** tree is based on **evolutionary relationships inferred from development and morphology**. The **modern** tree is based on **genome and rRNA sequence comparison**.

Read more: [Two trees of life](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/two-trees-of-life)
Tags: type/discriminate, core

---

Q+: On what kind of evidence does the classical tree make its deepest split among the animals?
A: On **development and morphology**. The deepest division is **Deuterostomia** against **Protostomia**, a difference in how the early embryo develops, with sponges and cnidarians branching below it. No part of the tree comes from sequence.

![The classical animal tree, splitting Deuterostomia from Protostomia about 700 million years ago](assets/img/slide-1a-23.png)

Read more: [Two trees of life](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/two-trees-of-life)
Tags: type/figure, type/why

---

Q+: And what does the sequence-based tree give instead?
A: **Three domains: Bacteria, Archaea and Eukaryotes**, much of it built from **genome sequencing of environmental samples** rather than cultured organisms. Animals are one twig inside one domain rather than half the picture.

![The tree of life in three domains, with archaea between bacteria and eukaryotes](assets/img/slide-1a-26.png)

Read more: [Two trees of life](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/two-trees-of-life)
Tags: type/figure, core

Q: In what sense are the archaea "intermediates" between bacteria and eukaryotes?
A: Because they **resemble eukaryotes in their genetics** (the replication, transcription and translation machinery) and **bacteria in their metabolism**. On the tree the eukaryotes emerge from within the archaea, beside the **Asgard** group.

Read more: [Two trees of life](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/two-trees-of-life)

Q: Humans carry one fewer chromosome pair than the other great apes because human chromosome 2 is a {{fusion}} of two ancestral chromosomes. Chromosome number is therefore a {{poor}} guide to how much two genomes differ.
A: A single rearrangement changes the count without changing much of the content. Apes and humans differ by one pair, and that difference is almost entirely bookkeeping.

Read more: [Humans and the great apes](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/humans-and-the-great-apes)
Tags: evolution, core

---

Q+: What two pieces of evidence show that human chromosome 2 is the fused pair?
A: **Banding**: the **p arm** matches chimp chromosome **12** and the **q arm** matches chimp **13**. **Sequence and structure**: there is **telomeric DNA in the middle** of the chromosome, where two ends were joined, and the centromere position matches that of chimp 12.

![Human chromosome 2 compared with chimpanzee chromosomes 12 and 13](assets/img/slide-1a-25.png)

Read more: [Humans and the great apes](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/humans-and-the-great-apes)
Tags: type/figure, type/why, core

---

Q+: A fusion should leave two centromeres. Why does human chromosome 2 have one?
A: A chromosome with two active centromeres is pulled to both poles and breaks, so one centromere is **inactivated and degenerates**. The surviving centromere is the one matching chimp 12; the remains of the other are still detectable in the sequence.

Read more: [Humans and the great apes](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/humans-and-the-great-apes)
Tags: type/apply

Q: Why is ribosomal RNA the gene used to build the tree of life?
A: Because it is **universal** and **changes extremely slowly**: aligned across human, *Methanococcus* and *E. coli*, most positions are still identical **after 3.5 billion years**. A gene that changes quickly cannot be aligned across domains; one that never changed would carry no information about branching order.

![Aligned rRNA sequence from human, Methanococcus and E. coli](assets/img/slide-1a-29.png)

Read more: [Ribosomal RNA](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/ribosomal-rna)
Tags: type/why, type/figure, core

Q: Gene number differs far less between a bacterium and a human than complexity does. What question does that pose?
A: **Where do new genes come from?** A bacterium runs on a few thousand genes and a human on roughly twenty thousand, a gap far too small to account for the difference between them, so the real question is how a genome acquires new genes at all.

A: **Intragenic mutation**, **gene duplication**, **DNA segment shuffling**, and **horizontal DNA transfer**.

Q+: Which of the four can increase the *number* of genes in a genome, and which cannot?
- [x] Gene duplication
- [x] Horizontal DNA transfer
- [ ] Intragenic mutation
- [ ] DNA segment shuffling
A: Mutation rewrites an existing gene and shuffling rearranges existing pieces into new combinations; neither adds a gene. Duplication adds a copy, and horizontal transfer imports one from another organism.

Read more: [How new genes arise](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/how-new-genes-arise)
Tags: type/discriminate, core

---

Q+: Why is duplication such a productive route, when a duplicate is initially redundant?
A: Because redundancy **removes the selective constraint on one copy**. One copy keeps doing the original job while the other is free to accumulate mutations and acquire a modified or new function. This is the route the globin family illustrates.

Read more: [Gene duplication](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/gene-duplication)
Tags: type/why, core

Q: Adult haemoglobin is built from which chains, and what does each carry?
A: **Two α-globin and two β-globin chains**, α₂β₂, each carrying a **heme** group.

Read more: [Globin gene family](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/globin-gene-family)

Q: The human globin genes sit in two clusters on two different chromosomes. What does that arrangement record about their history?
A: A history of **repeated duplication**. An ancient duplication separated the α line from the β line, and the two copies ended up on different chromosomes; **further duplications within each cluster** produced its members, which is why the genes that duplicated most recently are still neighbours.

![The evolutionary history of the globin genes, with duplication times in millions of years](assets/img/slide-1a-35.png)

Read more: [The gene clusters and their timeline](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/the-gene-clusters-and-their-timeline)
Tags: type/figure, type/why, globins, core

Q+: Which pair would you expect to be most similar in sequence, and why: α and β, or δ and β?
A: **δ and β**, because theirs is the **more recent** duplication, while the α and β lines have been diverging since a far older one. **Sequence similarity tracks time since duplication**, so the family tree can be read off the sequences themselves.

Read more: [The gene clusters and their timeline](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/the-gene-clusters-and-their-timeline)

Q: What determines which globin gene is expressed before and after birth?
A: **Which gene the locus control region (LCR) loops onto.** In the fetal stage it contacts **Gγ and Aγ**, and the adult genes are repressed; in the adult stage it contacts **δ and β**, and the fetal genes are repressed.

![The fetal-to-adult haemoglobin switch, with the locus control region looping to different genes](assets/img/slide-1a-36.png)

Q+: Sickle cell disease is caused by a β-globin mutation. Why is the switch itself a drug target?

Q: Paralogs and orthologs are both homologs. What separates them?
- [x] Paralogs arise by duplication within one genome
- [x] Orthologs arise by speciation, leaving one copy per species
- [ ] Only orthologs share a common ancestor
- [ ] Paralogs always keep the same function
A: Homolog is the umbrella term: shared ancestry. Duplication gives paralogs, which are free to diverge in function (α-globin and β-globin). Speciation gives orthologs, which usually keep the same job (human and mouse copies of a gene).

Read more: [Homologous genes](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/homologous-genes)
Tags: type/discriminate, core

---

Q+: You find a new gene in zebrafish that resembles human *BRCA2*. Which comparison tells you whether it is likely to do the same job?
A: Whether it is an **ortholog** rather than a paralog. Orthologs, separated by speciation, generally retain the ancestral function; a paralog inside the same genome may have diverged. Inferring function from the family is exactly what the >200 universal gene families are used for.

Read more: [Orthologs](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/orthologs)
Tags: type/apply

Q: More than 200 gene families are common to all three domains. Which two categories are the largest, and what does that say about the last common ancestor?
A: **Translation** and **amino acid transport and metabolism**. The universal core is **information processing and metabolism**: whatever the common ancestor had, it already read its own genome and ran a full metabolism.

Q: Going from bacteria to yeast to human, how do genome size and gene number each change?
A: **Genome size grows enormously**, by roughly three orders of magnitude; **gene number grows only modestly**, by well under one. The two do not scale together, which is the first sign that gene count is not what separates a human from a yeast.

Q+: *Arabidopsis* has ~27,000 genes in 135 × 10⁶ base pairs; humans ~20,000 in 3,100 × 10⁶. What follows?

Q: How is a bacterial chromosome arranged, and what does that arrangement do for replication?
A: A **single circular chromosome** carrying **one origin of replication**, with the **terminus directly opposite it**. The two forks that leave the origin travel in opposite directions and meet at the terminus, so the whole chromosome is copied in the time it takes to travel half of it.

Read more: [Escherichia coli](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/escherichia-coli)
Tags: type/why, model-organisms, core

Q: Why is budding yeast the model organism of choice for a eukaryotic cell biologist?
A: Because it is a **eukaryote with everything present and nothing extra**: one electron micrograph shows a **cell wall, Golgi stack, nucleus, mitochondrion, ribosomes** and **plasma membrane** in one small cell that grows as fast as a bacterium.

Read more: [Yeast](#/chapter/ch01-cells-and-genomes/yeast)

Q: **Exam-shaped question.** "Explain how gene duplication generates new genes, using the globins as an example." Sketch a full answer.
A: A checklist for marks:

| Point | Content |
|---|---|
| Mechanism | Duplication produces two copies of one gene in one genome, which are **paralogs** |
| Why it matters | Redundancy releases one copy from selection, so it can accumulate mutations while the other keeps the original function |
| Evidence | The globins are a family whose members duplicated at different times: myoglobin split first, then the α and β lines, then the genes within each cluster |
| Structure | Two clusters on two different chromosomes, each holding several related genes, which is the fingerprint of repeated duplication |
| Function gained | Different oxygen-binding proteins for different tissues and life stages, for example fetal α₂γ₂ versus adult α₂β₂ |
| Wider point | Duplication is one of four routes to innovation, with mutation, segment shuffling and horizontal transfer |
