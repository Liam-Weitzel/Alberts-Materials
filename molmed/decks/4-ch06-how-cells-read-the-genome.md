Q: Trace the chain of reasoning that led from Mendel to the central dogma.
A: Heredity travels in **discrete units** (Mendel); those units ride on **chromosomes** (Boveri and Sutton, confirmed by Morgan in *Drosophila*); the hereditary material is **nucleic acid, not protein** (Avery, MacLeod and McCarty); and its **structure explains how it is copied** (Watson and Crick). What was left open was how it is **read**, which is what the central dogma answers.

Read more: [How DNA became the molecule of life](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/how-dna-became-the-molecule-of-life)
Tags: type/sequence, history, core

Q+: Which of those was the first evidence that DNA *carries information*, and why is it the pivot?
A: **Avery, MacLeod and McCarty.** Transformation of pneumococcus by material from heat-killed cells showed the carrier is **nucleic acid, not protein**. Watson and Crick then explained how such information could be **copied**; how it is **read** was still open, and that is what the rest of this topic answers.

Read more: [How DNA became the molecule of life](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/how-dna-became-the-molecule-of-life)

Q: State the central dogma, then the three ways in which it is not entirely true.
A: **DNA to RNA to protein.** The exceptions: **reverse transcription** (retroviruses such as **HIV**); **RNA-dependent RNA replication** (almost all RNA viruses **except** retroviruses, coronaviruses among them); and the fact that **not all RNA is translated**.

Read more: [The central dogma, and where it is not true](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/the-central-dogma-and-where-it-is-not-true)

Q: RNA differs from DNA in the sugar and in one base. Which, and what is the consequence of each?
A: **Ribose instead of deoxyribose**, adding a **2'-OH**, which makes RNA **chemically more reactive and therefore less stable**. **Uracil instead of thymine**, which changes nothing about base pairing.

Read more: [Two chemical differences from DNA](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/two-chemical-differences-from-dna)
Tags: type/discriminate, rna, core

---

Q+: True or false: the 2'-OH makes RNA more stable than DNA.
- [ ] True
- [x] False
A: It makes RNA **less** stable. A hydroxyl sitting next to the phosphodiester bond is a nucleophile in the wrong place, so RNA hydrolyses far more readily. That is why the genome is stored as DNA and only read out as RNA, and the same reactivity is used deliberately when the branch-point adenine attacks the 5' splice site in splicing.

Read more: [Two chemical differences from DNA](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/two-chemical-differences-from-dna)
Tags: type/misconception, rna, core

Q+: Which structural difference actually matters most, and what does it allow?
A: That **RNA is single-stranded**. A single strand folds back on itself: base pairs give **secondary structure**, further contacts give **tertiary structure**, and a shaped RNA can bind specifically and catalyse, as a **ribozyme**.

Read more: [Single-strandedness is the real difference](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/single-strandedness-is-the-real-difference)

Q: tRNA is the classic worked example of RNA folding. What does it look like in two and in three dimensions?
A: Drawn flat, a **cloverleaf** of base-paired stems. In three dimensions, an **L shape**, with the **anticodon** at one end and the **amino acid attachment site** at the other.

Q: What does each of the main non-coding RNA classes do: snRNA, snoRNA, miRNA and piRNA?
A: **snRNA:** nuclear processes including **splicing of pre-mRNA**. **snoRNA:** **process and chemically modify rRNA**. **miRNA:** **block translation** of specific mRNAs and cause their degradation. **piRNA:** bind piwi proteins and **protect the germ line from transposable elements**.

Q: What two kinds of evidence established the ribosome as the site of protein synthesis?
A: **Morphological**, from electron microscopy: cells that secrete protein at an enormous rate, such as pancreatic acinar cells, are **packed with ribosomes**, so the structure tracks the activity. **Biochemical**: a **cell-free system** fed **radiolabelled amino acids** put the label into protein on ribosomes, turning a correlation into a demonstration and turning up **tRNA** along the way.

Q+: Volkin and Astrachan saw RNA synthesis rise sharply after phage infection. Why was that not yet proof?
A: Because a **correlation** with phage protein synthesis is consistent with several roles for that RNA. It made RNA a **candidate** intermediate; showing that the new RNA **works on old ribosomes** is what settled it.

Q+: How did Brenner, Jacob and Meselson settle it?
A: *E. coli* grown in **heavy** medium has **heavy ribosomes**; after infection and a shift to **light** medium, the **new RNA is light** and is found on the **pre-existing heavy ribosomes**. So **ribosomes are general-purpose machines** and specificity comes from a **short-lived RNA intermediate**.

Q: Which of these distinguish transcription from replication?
- [x] The RNA detaches instead of staying paired to the template
- [x] The product covers one gene, not a chromosome
- [x] The error rate is higher
- [x] No primer is needed
- [ ] RNA polymerase is more processive than DNA polymerase
A: RNA polymerases are highly processive but **less so** than DNA polymerases, which carry sliding clamps. The other four are real differences, and the two enzyme families are **structurally distinct** despite doing chemically similar work.

Read more: [Similarities and differences with replication](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/similarities-and-differences-with-replication)

---

Q+: Why can the cell tolerate a higher error rate in transcription than in replication?
A: Because a transcript is **transient and made in many copies**, so a bad one is diluted and degraded, while a replication error is **inherited by every descendant** of that cell.

Read more: [Similarities and differences with replication](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/similarities-and-differences-with-replication)

Q: In bacteria, what makes RNA polymerase recognise a promoter, and what is the holoenzyme?
A: The **sigma factor**. Core enzyme plus sigma is the **holoenzyme**, and sigma is the subunit that reads the promoter.

Q: Put the bacterial transcription cycle in order.
A: **Sliding along DNA; closed complex at the promoter; open complex with the transcription bubble, including abortive initiation; promoter clearance and sigma release; elongation; hairpin formation; termination; reassociation with sigma.**

Q+: What is abortive initiation, and why does it happen?
A: The polymerase repeatedly makes **short transcripts and releases them without leaving the promoter**. It happens because **sigma still holds the enzyme on the promoter** while it tries to move forward; only once the transcript passes roughly ten nucleotides does the grip break.

Read more: [Transcription in prokaryotes](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/transcription-in-prokaryotes)
Tags: type/why, transcription, core

---

Q+: What terminates bacterial transcription, and where does that signal physically reside?
A: An **RNA hairpin**, a GC-rich stem followed by a U-rich tract, which destabilises the hybrid and pulls the transcript out. The signal is in the **RNA**, not in the DNA being read.

Read more: [Transcription in prokaryotes](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/transcription-in-prokaryotes)
Tags: type/why, transcription, core

Q: What does a bacterial promoter consist of, and what does the sigma factor actually read?
A: Two **short conserved sequence elements** upstream of the start site, named by their positions relative to it, separated by a **spacer of roughly fixed length**. Sigma reads **both elements at once**, so the **spacing matters as much as the sequences**: change the spacer and sigma can no longer touch both.

![The E. coli promoter consensus with its -35 and -10 elements and a sequence logo](assets/img/slide-4-23.png)

Read more: [The promoter, and why asymmetry matters](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/the-promoter-and-why-asymmetry-matters)
Tags: type/figure, type/why, transcription, core

---

Q+: One property of that consensus matters more than the letters themselves. Which, and why?
A: Its **asymmetry**. The consensus is **not a palindrome**, so sigma can dock only one way round. That fixes **which strand is the template** and therefore **which direction the polymerase travels**, which is why genes run both ways along a bacterial chromosome depending on their own promoter.

Read more: [The promoter, and why asymmetry matters](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/the-promoter-and-why-asymmetry-matters)
Tags: type/why, transcription, core

Q: Eukaryotes divide transcription between several RNA polymerases where bacteria use one. How is the work split, and why split it at all?
A: **Pol I** makes the bulk **rRNA**, **Pol II** makes all **protein-coding** genes and most regulatory RNAs, **Pol III** makes **tRNA and other small RNAs**. Splitting the job lets each enzyme carry its **own promoter recognition and its own regulation**, so ribosome production can be tuned independently of gene expression.

Read more: [Three polymerases](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/three-polymerases)
Tags: transcription, core

Q: Match each core promoter element to the general transcription factor that reads it.
A: The principle first: **each element is read by a specific general transcription factor**, which is how an otherwise generic machine finds a start site at all. **BRE** is read by **TFIIB**; **TATA** by **TBP**, the subunit of TFIID; **INR** and **DPE** by **TFIID** itself.

Q+: Assembly starts with TFIID. What is it made of, and what happens to the DNA when its TBP subunit binds?
A: **TBP plus a set of TAFs.** TBP sits over the DNA as a **saddle**, binds through the **minor groove** and **kinks the helix sharply**. That bend is not incidental: it is the **geometry the remaining factors dock onto**.

Q: TFIIH has two enzymatic activities. Name them and say what each achieves.
A: An **ATP-dependent helicase**, which **opens the double helix** at the start site, doing by hand what sigma does by itself in bacteria; and a **kinase**, which **phosphorylates the CTD**.

Q+: What is the CTD, and what are its two phosphorylation marks for?
A: The **C-terminal domain** of the largest Pol II subunit, a long tail built from **many tandem repeats of one short amino acid sequence**. **Serine 5 phosphorylation** marks **initiation** and recruits the **capping** enzymes; **serine 2 phosphorylation** rises during **elongation** and recruits the **splicing** and **3'-end processing** machinery.

Read more: [The pre-initiation complex and the CTD](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/the-pre-initiation-complex-and-the-ctd)
Tags: transcription, core

---

Q+: Why is it useful that the tail carries two different marks rather than one?
A: Because the marks encode **where the polymerase is along the gene**, so the RNA is processed **in the right order**: capping while the transcript is 25 nucleotides long, splicing and polyadenylation later. The enzymes ride the polymerase and are handed to the RNA at the right moment.

Read more: [The pre-initiation complex and the CTD](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/the-pre-initiation-complex-and-the-ctd)
Tags: type/why, core

Q: A pre-initiation complex is assembled but transcription does not start. What else is needed?
A: **Activators** bound at **enhancers**, **Mediator** coupling them to the polymerase, and **chromatin remodelling and histone-modifying complexes**. A distant enhancer reaches the promoter by **DNA looping**.

![An activator at a distant enhancer with Mediator, remodellers and modifying enzymes](assets/img/slide-4-33.png)

---

Q+: What is the difference between a general and a sequence-specific transcription factor?
A: A **general** factor (TFIID, TFIIA, TFIIB, TFIIF, TFIIE, TFIIH) is needed at **essentially all Pol II promoters**, reads **core promoter elements**, and carries **no information about which gene should be on**. A **sequence-specific** factor binds a **particular enhancer or silencer**, is present only in some cells or conditions, and is **where the regulatory information lives**.

Read more: [General transcription factors](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/general-transcription-factors)
Tags: type/discriminate, transcription, core

---

Q: As transcription starts, what leaves the promoter and what joins the polymerase?
A: **Leaves:** most general transcription factors, as the polymerase clears the promoter, though **TFIID and Mediator can stay** to allow rapid re-initiation. **Joins:** **elongation factors** for the length of the gene, **ATP-dependent chromatin remodelling complexes**, and the **RNA-processing factors** riding the phosphorylated CTD.

Q: A polymerase tracking along DNA makes {{positive}} supercoils ahead of it and {{negative}} supercoils behind it.
A: Helix opening is **hindered ahead** and **facilitated behind**. On a long transcription unit the tension slows the polymerase, and **topoisomerases** relieve it.

Q+: Why does a polymerase supercoil the DNA at all, rather than just spinning the helix?
A: Because chromosomal DNA has **fixed ends** and is anchored, so it **cannot rotate freely**. Only DNA with a free end can spin the strain away.

Q: Name the three capping enzymes in order and what each does.
A: **RNA triphosphatase** removes a phosphate; **guanylyltransferase** adds **GMP** in a reversed **5'-to-5'** linkage; **methyltransferase** methylates the guanine. A further **2'-O-methyltransferase** gives cap-1.

![The capping reactions performed by RNA triphosphatase, guanylyltransferase and methyltransferase](assets/img/slide-4-43.png)

---

Q+: Why does the reversed 5'-to-5' linkage matter?
A: Because it leaves **no free 5' end** for an exonuclease to grip, so the cap **protects the transcript**. It is also the handle for the cap-binding complex and later for **eIF4E**, and cap methylation is how a cell marks an RNA as **its own**.

Read more: [The 5′ cap](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/the-5-cap)
Tags: type/why, processing, core

Q: Why splice at all, when bacteria manage without it?
A: Because a gene in pieces can be **recombined in different ways**. **Alternative splicing** lets one coding region make several proteins, as α-tropomyosin does in striated muscle, smooth muscle, fibroblasts and brain.

![Alternative splicing of the rat alpha-tropomyosin gene into tissue-specific mRNAs](assets/img/slide-4-46.png)

Read more: [Splicing](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/splicing)

---

Q+: Splicing consumes a lot of ATP for a reaction whose chemistry needs none. What is the ATP buying?
A: **Accuracy.** The chemistry is two **transesterifications**, which trade one phosphodiester bond for another. The ATP pays for the **rearrangements that check and re-check the splice sites**, and for recycling the snRNPs.

Read more: [Splicing](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/splicing)
Tags: type/why, splicing, core

---

Q: What does the spliceosome have to recognise on an intron, and which atom actually does the chemistry?
A: Three signals: the **5' splice site**, the **branch point** and the **3' splice site**, the last preceded by a **polypyrimidine tract**. Two of them mark **where to cut**; the branch point supplies the **reagent**, the **2'-OH of its adenine**, which is the nucleophile that opens the reaction.

![The consensus sequences at the 5' splice site, branch point and 3' splice site](assets/img/slide-4-47.png)

Read more: [Splicing](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/splicing)
Tags: type/figure, splicing, core

---

Q+: Describe the two transesterification reactions.
A: First, the **2'-OH of the branch-point adenine attacks the 5' splice site**, cutting it and forming the **lariat**, leaving exon 1 with a free **3'-OH**. Second, that **3'-OH attacks the 3' splice site**, joining the exons and **releasing the lariat**.

Read more: [The chemistry: two transesterifications](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/the-chemistry-two-transesterifications)
Tags: type/sequence, splicing, core

---

Q+: Those consensus sequences are short and degenerate. Is that a defect?
A: **No, it is the precondition for alternative splicing.** Strong, unambiguous sites would give one product per gene. Weak sites let regulatory proteins tip the choice from tissue to tissue, at the cost of needing a large machine to check them.

Read more: [Splicing](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/splicing)
Tags: type/why, splicing, core

Q: Which snRNAs make up the spliceosome, and what is a snRNP?
A: **U1, U2, U4, U5 and U6**. A **snRNP** is one of those short RNAs together with the set of proteins bound to it, and the spliceosome is **assembled from them afresh on every intron** rather than arriving ready-made.

![The spliceosome cycle, from U1 binding the 5' splice site to release of the spliced RNA](assets/img/slide-4-50.png)

Read more: [The spliceosome](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/the-spliceosome)

---

Q+: Put the assembly in order.
A: **U1** pairs with the 5' splice site while **BBP** and **U2AF** mark the branch point and 3' site; **U2** displaces them and pairs with the branch point, **bulging the adenine out**; the **U4/U6 and U5 tri-snRNP** enters, with **U4 and U6 paired to each other**; rearrangement **breaks U4/U6** so that **U6 displaces U1** at the 5' splice site and **U4 leaves**; the active site forms.

Read more: [The spliceosome](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/the-spliceosome)
Tags: type/sequence, splicing, core

---

Q+: Why does U6 arrive base-paired to U4 rather than ready to work?
A: Because U6 carries part of the **catalytic core**, and an active site assembled too early would cut the wrong place. Pairing with U4 keeps it **inactive until the site has been checked**, and releasing it is one of the ATP-driven proofreading steps.

Read more: [The spliceosome](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/the-spliceosome)

Q: What happens to intron and exon lengths across species, and what does the machinery do about it?
A: **Introns vary enormously**, from mostly under 100 nucleotides in worm and fly to tens of thousands in humans; **exons stay short and uniform**. So the machinery marks the **exon**, not the intron: **SR proteins** bind along exons and recruit U1 and U2AF to the flanking sites, while **hnRNPs** coat the introns.

Q: What makes an mRNA export-competent, and what is stripped off before it leaves?
A: Its **protein coat**: **cap-binding complex**, **poly-A-binding proteins**, **SR proteins** and **exon junction complexes**. **hnRNPs and other nucleus-restricted proteins are stripped off**, so an unspliced transcript still wearing them is not exported.

Q+: In the cytosol, what is exchanged, and what does the first round of translation do?
A: The **cap-binding complex is replaced by eIF4E and eIF4G**, and the **first round of translation strips the exon junction complexes**. An EJC left **downstream of a stop codon** means the stop came too early, and triggers **nonsense-mediated decay**.

Read more: [What happens to the mRNA, and to the polymerase?](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/what-happens-to-the-mrna-and-to-the-polymerase)

Q+: Why is NMD a sensible use of the EJC rather than an accident?
A: Because the EJC marks **where introns were**, and a normal stop codon lies in the **last exon**, past every junction. A stop with junctions still downstream therefore signals a **premature termination codon**, and destroying the message is cheaper than making a truncated protein.

Q: What is the nucleolus, and what is made there besides rRNA?
A: The site where **rRNA is transcribed and processed and ribosomal subunits assembled**, and it is **not membrane-bounded**: it forms around the tandem rRNA genes that **Pol I** transcribes at high density. Also made there: **U6 snRNP**, **telomerase**, and some **tRNA** processing.

Read more: [The nucleolus](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/the-nucleolus)
Tags: nucleolus, core

---

Q+: What happens to it at mitosis, and why?
A: It **disperses and reassembles afterwards**, because there is **no rRNA transcription** while the chromosomes are condensed. A compartment held together by the work going on inside it cannot outlive that work.

Read more: [The nucleolus](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/the-nucleolus)
Tags: type/why, nucleolus, core

Q: How is the rRNA precursor processed, and what happens to the rest of it?
A: A **single long precursor** is **chemically modified** first, then **cleaved and trimmed** into **18S, 5.8S and 28S**, **discarding nearly half of its own sequence**. The fourth rRNA, **5S**, is made separately by **Pol III**, so one transcript yields three of the four and the last comes from elsewhere.

Q+: Which two modifications, and what targets them to the right nucleotide?
A: **2'-O-methylation** of the ribose and **conversion of uridine to pseudouridine**. They are targeted by **snoRNAs** in **snoRNPs**, which **base-pair with the precursor** at the site to be modified and position the enzyme: the RNA supplies the address, the protein does the chemistry.

Q: Besides the nucleolus, which nuclear bodies are there, and what is each for?

Q+: What is a transcription factory, and what holds one together?
A: A focus where **many polymerases work together** rather than transcription being spread evenly through the nucleoplasm. **Many weak, multivalent interactions** between the CTD tails and the factors they carry **condense** them into one body, and **genes on different chromosomes can be drawn into the same factory**.

Q: Trace a miRNA from gene to RISC, naming the enzyme at each step.
A: **Pol II** transcribes a capped, polyadenylated **pri-miRNA** that folds into hairpins; **Drosha** **crops** the hairpin in the nucleus; **exportin 5** exports it; **Dicer** **dices** off the loop, leaving a duplex of about **22 nucleotides**; one strand is loaded with **Argonaute** into **RISC**.

Q+: What decides whether RISC slices the target or merely represses it?
A: **How well the miRNA matches**, usually in the **3' UTR**. An **extensive match** leads to **slicing** by Argonaute and rapid degradation; a **less extensive match** leads to **translational repression**, deadenylation and eventual decay.

Q+: RISC and RITS are loaded with similar small RNAs. What is the difference in what they do?
A: **RISC** acts **post-transcriptionally** in the cytosol, cleaving or repressing the target mRNA. **RITS** acts in the **nucleus** on the **nascent transcript**, recruiting **histone and DNA methylation** to give **transcriptional repression** and heterochromatin. The second uses an RNA as an address for a heritable chromatin mark.

Q: Which of these summary statements about transcription and RNA processing are correct?
- [x] Transcription is always 5' to 3' and RNA polymerases are structurally distinct from DNA polymerases
- [x] TFIIH and the Pol II CTD coordinate RNA processing
- [x] Splicing increases protein diversity, enabled by weak consensus sites
- [x] Introns are removed as a lariat by the spliceosome, and it consumes energy
- [ ] The spliceosome is made of protein alone
A: The spliceosome's active site is made of **RNA**, U6 and U2 holding two magnesium ions, so it is a **ribozyme** with a large protein exoskeleton. The other four are correct, and belong with mRNA as a short-lived intermediate, the prokaryote and eukaryote polymerase inventories, the elongation factors, the 3' processing factors, nuclear organisation, and non-coding RNA regulation.

Q: An mRNA vaccine has the same parts as a cellular mRNA. Which parts are design variables, and what does each change?
A: The **5' and 3' UTRs**, which change the **in-cell half-life** and the **ribosome load**; and the **codon choice**, which changes both the **total protein output** and the **secondary structure the mRNA folds into**, since the coding sequence also base-pairs with itself.

Q+: Synonymous codons encode the same protein. Why does the choice change how much protein you get?
A: Because the **mRNA is a physical molecule as well as a message**: codon choice alters its **folded structure and therefore its stability and half-life**, and it alters how easily ribosomes move along it. Measured half-lives differed **several-fold** between codon-optimised versions of the very same open reading frame.

Q+: Pseudouridine is used in these vaccines. Which part of normal cellular RNA biology does that connect to?
A: The **nucleolus**: pseudouridine and **2'-O-methylation** are the modifications cells put on their **own** rRNA and mRNA, and are part of how the innate immune system tells **self from non-self**. Building them into a synthetic message makes it a medicine rather than an inflammatory stimulus, and also stabilises it. Reference: Leppek et al., *Nat. Commun.* 2022.

Q: **Exam-shaped question.** "Describe how a eukaryotic mRNA is made and processed, and explain how the polymerase coordinates the processing steps."
A: A checklist for marks:

| Point | Content |
|---|---|
| Initiation | General transcription factors assemble on the core promoter: TFIID with TBP on the TATA box, then TFIIB, TFIIA, Pol II with TFIIF, TFIIE, TFIIH |
| Activation | Activators at enhancers, Mediator, chromatin remodelling and histone modification; the enhancer reaches the promoter by DNA looping |
| The coordinator | TFIIH opens the helix and phosphorylates the CTD, whose many heptad repeats act as a moving platform |
| Capping | Ser5 phosphorylation recruits the capping enzymes as soon as the transcript emerges: triphosphatase, guanylyltransferase with a 5'-to-5' linkage, methyltransferase |
| Splicing | Ser2 phosphorylation recruits the splicing machinery; introns are removed as lariats by the spliceosome, co-transcriptionally, with exon definition by SR proteins |
| 3' end | CPSF reads AAUAAA, CstF the GU-rich element, cleavage at the CA, then poly-A polymerase adds the tail |
| Export and quality control | The mRNP coat licenses export; hnRNPs are stripped; EJCs downstream of a stop codon trigger nonsense-mediated decay |
| The principle | Processing is coupled to transcription through the CTD, so it happens in the right order and to every transcript |
