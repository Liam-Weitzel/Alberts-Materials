Q: Which discoveries does the lecture's opening timeline run through?
A: **Mendel** and the principles of heredity; **Boveri and Sutton** and the chromosome theory; **Morgan** confirming it in *Drosophila*; **Avery, MacLeod and McCarty** showing that nucleic acid is the transforming principle; **Watson and Crick** and the double helix; and the **central dogma**.
Tags: history, core

---

Q: State the central dogma, and the three ways the lecture marks it "not entirely true".
A: **DNA to RNA to protein**, by transcription then translation. The qualifications: **reverse transcription** by retroviruses such as **HIV**; **RNA-dependent RNA replication** by almost all RNA viruses **except the retroviruses**, coronaviruses among them; and the fact that **not all RNA is translated**.
Tags: core

---

Q: Chemically, how does RNA differ from DNA, and what follows from each difference?
A: **Ribose instead of deoxyribose**, adding a **2'-OH**, which makes RNA **more reactive and therefore less stable**; and **uracil instead of thymine**. Neither change affects base pairing.
Tags: rna, core

---

Q+: What is the structural difference, and why does it matter more?
A: **RNA is single-stranded**, so it folds back on itself: conventional base pairs give **secondary structure**, and further folding gives **tertiary structure**. That is what lets an RNA bind specifically and catalyse, as a **ribozyme**.

![An RNA molecule folded through conventional base pairs, the same structure folded further by nonconventional pairs, and the structure of a real self-splicing RNA](assets/img/fig-6-07.png)
Tags: rna, core

---

Q: Which RNA does the lecture use as its worked example of folding, and what does it look like in two and three dimensions?
A: **tRNA**: a **cloverleaf** of base-paired stems when drawn flat, folding into an **L shape** in three dimensions, with the **anticodon** at one end and the **amino acid attachment site** at the other.
Tags: rna

---

Q: Table 6-1. What do snRNA, snoRNA and piRNA do?
A: **snRNA**: small nuclear RNAs, various nuclear processes including **splicing of pre-mRNA**. **snoRNA**: small nucleolar RNAs, which **process and chemically modify rRNAs**. **piRNA**: piwi-interacting RNAs, which bind piwi proteins and **protect the germ line from transposable elements**.
Tags: rna, core

---

Q: Who established that ribosomes are the site of protein synthesis, and with what evidence?
A: **George Palade** (Nobel Prize 1974), by electron microscopy of **acinar cells of the rat pancreas**, which secrete enzymes at a huge rate and are packed with ribosomes.
Tags: history, core

---

Q+: Who made it biochemical, and what did they find along the way?
A: **Paul Zamecnik and Mahlon Hoagland**, with a **cell-free system** and **radiolabelled amino acids**. They discovered **tRNA**, the adaptor.
Tags: history

---

Q: "You are the scientist." Which result would best reveal the messenger molecule?
- [ ] The candidate should be very close to the DNA
- [ ] If the candidate is removed, protein levels remain unchanged
- [ ] If the candidate is removed, protein levels increase
- [x] An increase in protein levels should correspond to an increase in the candidate, and vice versa
A: A messenger has to be **quantitatively coupled** to the protein it specifies. Proximity is suggestive but not evidence, and the other two options have the sign backwards.
Tags: history, quiz, core

---

Q: What did Volkin and Astrachan see after infecting bacteria with bacteriophage?
A: **RNA synthesis rose sharply** at the moment the cell switched to making phage proteins, suggesting **RNA as the intermediate** between DNA and protein synthesis.
Tags: history, core

---

Q+: How did Brenner and colleagues settle it with isotopes?
A: *E. coli* was grown in **heavy** medium so its **ribosomes were heavy**, then infected and shifted to **light** medium. The **newly made RNA was light** and was found on the **pre-existing heavy ribosomes**: ribosomes are general-purpose machines, and the specificity comes from a **short-lived RNA intermediate**.
Tags: history, core

---

Q: In which direction is RNA synthesized, and what does that mean for the template?
A: RNA is synthesized **5' to 3'**, reading the **template strand 3' to 5'**, so the transcript matches the **non-template strand** with U in place of T.
Tags: transcription, core

---

Q: Which features are labelled on the cutaway of RNA polymerase?
A: The **Mg²⁺ ion at the active site**, the **ribonucleoside triphosphate uptake channel**, the **short DNA-RNA helix**, the **direction of transcription**, and the **transcription bubble** with DNA unwinding ahead and rewinding behind.

![Cutaway view of RNA polymerase with the magnesium ion at the active site, the short DNA-RNA helix, the ribonucleoside triphosphate uptake channel and the direction of transcription](assets/img/fig-6-09.png)
Tags: transcription, core

---

Q: How does transcription differ from replication, on the lecture's comparison?
- [x] The RNA detaches instead of staying paired to the template
- [x] The product is one gene long, not a chromosome
- [x] RNA polymerase has a higher error rate
- [ ] RNA polymerase is more processive than DNA polymerase
- [ ] RNA polymerase needs a primer to start
A: RNA polymerases are highly processive but **less so** than DNA polymerases, and they can **start a chain from scratch**, which a proofreading DNA polymerase cannot. The enzymes are structurally distinct.
Tags: transcription, core

---

Q: In bacteria, what makes RNA polymerase recognise a promoter?
A: The **sigma factor**, which joins the core enzyme to form the **holoenzyme** and is the subunit that reads the promoter.
Tags: transcription, core

---

Q: Put the bacterial transcription cycle in order.
A: Holoenzyme **slides along DNA**; **closed complex** at the promoter; **open complex** with a transcription bubble and **abortive initiation**; **promoter clearance and sigma release**; **elongation**; formation of the **terminator hairpin**; **termination**; **reassociation with sigma**.

![The transcription cycle of bacterial RNA polymerase drawn as eight steps, from holoenzyme assembly and promoter binding through abortive initiation, sigma release, elongation and termination](assets/img/fig-6-11.png)
Tags: transcription, core

---

Q+: What is abortive initiation?
A: The polymerase repeatedly makes **short transcripts and releases them without leaving the promoter**, until the transcript is long enough for it to break its grip and commit. The lecture plays a five-minute animation of it.
Tags: transcription, core

---

Q: What terminates transcription in bacteria, and where does the signal live?
A: An **RNA hairpin**, a GC-rich stem followed by a U-rich tract, which destabilises the hybrid and pulls the transcript out of the polymerase. The signal is in the **RNA**, not in the DNA being read.
Tags: transcription, core

---

Q: Give the bacterial promoter consensus.
A: **TTGACA at -35** and **TATAAT at -10**, separated by **15 to 19 nucleotides**, most often **17**.

![The consensus sequence for E. coli promoters with its -35 and -10 hexamers, the distribution of spacings between them, and the same information drawn as a sequence logo](assets/img/fig-6-12.png)
Tags: transcription, numbers, core

---

Q+: What is the point the lecturer writes in red about that consensus?
A: Its **asymmetry**. The sequence is not a palindrome, so sigma can only dock one way round, which sets **which strand is the template** and **which direction the polymerase travels**.
Tags: transcription, core

---

Q: How many RNA polymerases do eukaryotes have, and what does each transcribe?
A: **Three** (plants have two more). **Pol I**: 5.8S, 18S and 28S rRNA genes. **Pol II**: all protein-coding genes, plus snoRNA, miRNA, siRNA, lncRNA and most snRNA genes. **Pol III**: tRNA genes, 5S rRNA, some snRNA and other small RNAs.
Tags: transcription, core

---

Q: Name the general transcription factors, and say which one starts the assembly.
A: **TFIID, TFIIA, TFIIB, TFIIF, TFIIE, TFIIH.** Assembly starts with **TFIID**, which contains **TBP** plus about eleven **TAFs**, and TBP binds the **TATA box**.
Tags: transcription, core

---

Q: Match each core promoter element to the factor that reads it.
A: **BRE: TFIIB.** **TATA: TBP, the subunit of TFIID.** **INR: TFIID.** **DPE: TFIID.** The take-home message on the slide is that **each element is bound by a specific general transcription factor**.

![The consensus sequences around a eukaryotic polymerase II start point, with BRE and TATA upstream, INR at the start point and DPE downstream, and the factor that recognizes each](assets/img/fig-6-16.png)
Tags: transcription, core

---

Q: What does TBP do to the DNA when it binds?
A: It sits over the helix as a **saddle** of two similar domains, binds through the **minor groove**, and **kinks the helix sharply**, bending it by about 80 degrees.
Tags: transcription

---

Q: TFIIH has two enzymatic activities. What are they, and what does each accomplish?
A: An **ATP-dependent helicase**, which **opens the double helix** at the start site, and a **kinase**, which **phosphorylates the C-terminal domain** of the polymerase.
Tags: transcription, core

---

Q: What is the CTD, and how big is it?
A: The **C-terminal domain** of the largest Pol II subunit: **52 tandem repeats of a seven amino acid sequence** in humans. It works as a **moving platform** carrying the RNA-processing enzymes.
Tags: transcription, numbers, core

---

Q+: What do the two phosphorylation marks on it mean?
A: **Serine 5 phosphorylation** is the **initiation** mark, added by TFIIH, and it recruits the **capping** enzymes; it falls away as the polymerase moves on. **Serine 2 phosphorylation** rises during **elongation** and recruits the **splicing** and **3'-end processing** machinery.
Tags: transcription, core

---

Q: What has to be added to a pre-initiation complex to actually start transcription?
A: **Activators** bound at **enhancers**, **Mediator** coupling them to the polymerase, and **chromatin remodelling and histone-modifying complexes**. The DNA in between **loops out** so that a distant enhancer can touch the promoter.

![An activator protein bound at a distant enhancer, with Mediator, general transcription factors, RNA polymerase, chromatin remodelling complexes and histone-modifying enzymes assembling at the promoter](assets/img/fig-6-18.png)
Tags: transcription, core

---

Q: What is the difference between a general and a sequence-specific transcription factor?
A: A **general** factor is needed at **essentially all Pol II promoters**, recognises **core promoter elements**, and carries no information about which gene should be on. A **sequence-specific** factor binds a **particular enhancer or silencer sequence**, is present in some cells and conditions and not others, and is where the regulatory information lives.
Tags: transcription, core

---

Q: When transcription starts, what leaves the promoter and what arrives?
A: Most **general transcription factors are released** as the polymerase clears the promoter, while **TFIID and Mediator can stay** to allow re-initiation. **Elongation factors** bind and stay for the length of the gene, **chromatin remodelling complexes** travel with the polymerase, and the **RNA-processing factors** ride the phosphorylated CTD.
Tags: transcription, core

---

Q: A transcribing polymerase generates **{{positive}}** supercoils ahead of it and **{{negative}}** supercoils behind it.
A: Helix opening is **hindered** ahead and **facilitated** behind. On a long transcription unit the tension slows the polymerase, and **topoisomerases** relieve it.

![DNA with a free end rotating as the helix is opened, DNA with fixed ends forming a supercoil instead, and a protein tracking along DNA generating positive supercoils ahead and negative supercoils behind](assets/img/fig-6-20.png)
Tags: transcription, core

---

Q: Which three enzymes build the 5' cap, in order?
A: **RNA triphosphatase**, which removes a phosphate; **guanylyltransferase**, which adds GMP in a reversed **5' to 5'** linkage; and **methyltransferase**, which methylates the guanine. A further **2'-O-methyltransferase** gives cap-1.

![The capping reactions, from removal of a phosphate through addition of GMP in a reverse linkage to methylation of the guanosine](assets/img/fig-6-24.png)
Tags: processing, core

---

Q+: What is the take-home message of the capping slides?
A: That there are **different cap types**, and the difference is not cosmetic: cap methylation is how a cell marks an RNA as its own.
Tags: processing, core

---

Q: Why does splicing exist, given that bacteria manage without it?
A: Because a gene in pieces can be **recombined in different ways**: **alternative splicing** lets one coding region produce several proteins, which is an advantage both for making new proteins from existing parts and for regulation.
Tags: splicing, core

---

Q+: What does it cost?
A: **A lot of ATP**, circled in red on the slide, plus **several hundred proteins and five RNAs**, for a reaction whose chemistry needs no energy at all. The ATP buys **accuracy**.
Tags: splicing, core

---

Q: What are the three sequence signals of an intron, and which nucleotide does the chemistry?
A: The **5' splice site (donor)**, which begins **GU**; the **branch point**, whose **adenine** is the nucleophile; and the **3' splice site (acceptor)**, which ends **AG**, preceded by the **polypyrimidine tract**.
Tags: splicing, core

---

Q: Describe the two transesterification reactions of splicing.
A: First the **2'-OH of the branch-point adenine attacks the 5' splice site**, cutting it and forming the **lariat**, and leaving exon 1 with a free **3'-OH**. Then that **3'-OH attacks the 3' splice site**, joining the exons and **releasing the lariat**.

![The branch-point adenine attacking the 5' splice site to form a lariat, then the freed exon end attacking the next exon, with the phosphate rearrangements drawn in detail](assets/img/fig-6-26.png)
Tags: splicing, core

---

Q: Which snRNAs make up the spliceosome, and how big are they?
A: **U1, U2, U4, U5 and U6**, each **under 200 nucleotides** and each complexed with **at least seven proteins** to form a snRNP.
Tags: splicing, core

---

Q+: Put the assembly in order.
A: **U1** base-pairs with the 5' splice site while **BBP** and **U2AF** mark the branch point and 3' site; **U2** displaces them and pairs with the branch point, bulging the adenine out; the **U4/U6 and U5 triple snRNP** enters, with **U4 and U6 base-paired to each other**; rearrangement **breaks the U4/U6 pairing** so that **U6 displaces U1** at the 5' splice site and **U4 leaves**; the active site forms.

![The stepwise spliceosome cycle from U1 binding the 5' splice site through U2 displacing BBP, entry of the U4/U6+U5 triple snRNP, active site formation, lariat formation and release of the spliced RNA](assets/img/fig-6-29.png)
Tags: splicing, core

---

Q: What are exon and intron lengths like across species, and what does the difference imply?
A: **Introns vary enormously**, from mostly under 100 nucleotides in worm and fly to tens of thousands in humans, while **exons are short and remarkably uniform**. So the machinery marks the **exon**, not the intron, which is exon definition.

![Size distributions of exons and of introns in the human, worm and fly genomes, with exon length far more uniform](assets/img/fig-6-31.png)
Tags: splicing, core

---

Q+: Which proteins mark which?
A: **SR proteins** bind along the **exons** and guide the snRNPs to the boundaries; **hnRNP** proteins coat the **introns**. Splicing happens **co-transcriptionally**, and several introns are removed **simultaneously**.
Tags: splicing, core

---

Q: Which three signals direct cleavage and polyadenylation, and which factor reads each?
A: **AAUAAA**, read by **CPSF**; the **CA** cleavage site 10 to 30 nucleotides downstream, cut by the **cleavage factors**; and a **GU-rich or U-rich element** within about 30 nucleotides further on, read by **CstF**.

![The AAUAAA hexamer, the CA cleavage site and the GU-rich element downstream, the signals that direct cleavage and polyadenylation](assets/img/fig-6-36.png)
Tags: processing, core

---

Q+: What happens after the cut, and how is the length set?
A: **Poly-A polymerase** adds roughly **200 adenines** without a template, and **poly-A-binding proteins** coat the tail as it grows; when enough have bound, the polymerase lets go.
Tags: processing, core

---

Q: What decides whether an mRNA may leave the nucleus?
A: Its **protein coat**. An export-ready mRNP carries the **cap-binding complex**, **poly-A-binding proteins**, **SR proteins** and **exon junction complexes**, while **hnRNPs and other nucleus-restricted proteins are stripped off**. An unspliced transcript still wearing them is not exported.
Tags: export, core

---

Q+: What happens to the coat in the cytosol?
A: The nuclear proteins are **exchanged for cytosolic ones**: the cap-binding complex is replaced by **eIF4E and eIF4G**, and the **first round of translation strips the exon junction complexes**. An EJC left **downstream of a stop codon** triggers **nonsense-mediated decay**.
Tags: export, core

---

Q: What is the nucleolus?
A: The site where **ribosomal RNA is transcribed and processed and ribosomal subunits are assembled**, and it is **not membrane-bounded**: it forms around the tandemly repeated rRNA genes, which **Pol I** transcribes at extraordinary density.
Tags: nucleolus, core

---

Q+: What else is made there, and what happens to it during the cell cycle?
A: Not only rRNA: also **U6 snRNP**, **telomerase** and some **tRNA** processing. It **disperses at mitosis and reassembles afterwards**, and its size follows demand.
Tags: nucleolus, core

---

Q: How is the rRNA precursor processed?
A: The **35S precursor**, about **13,000 nucleotides**, is first **chemically modified**, then **cleaved and trimmed** into **18S, 5.8S and 28S** rRNA, discarding nearly half the sequence. **5S rRNA is made elsewhere**, by Pol III.

![The 35S precursor rRNA of 13,000 nucleotides being chemically modified, then cleaved and trimmed into 18S, 5.8S and 28S rRNAs with nearly half the sequence discarded](assets/img/fig-6-42.png)
Tags: nucleolus, core

---

Q+: Which two chemical modifications, and what targets them?
A: **2'-O-methylation** of the ribose and **isomerisation of uridine to pseudouridine**. They are targeted by **snoRNAs** in **snoRNPs**, which **base-pair with the precursor** at the site to be modified and position the enzyme.
Tags: nucleolus, core

---

Q: Besides the nucleolus, which nuclear bodies does the lecture name, and what are they for?
A: **Cajal bodies**, for snRNA and snoRNA maturation and snRNP recycling; and **interchromatin granule clusters**, or **speckles**, for snRNP storage. The rest is **bulk chromatin**.
Tags: nucleolus, core

---

Q: What is a transcription factory?
A: A focus where **many polymerases work together** rather than transcription being spread evenly through the nucleoplasm. Many weak, multivalent interactions between the polymerase tails and the factors they carry **condense** them into one body, and **genes on different chromosomes can be drawn into the same factory**.
Tags: nucleolus, core

---

Q: Which polymerase transcribes miRNA genes, and what does the primary transcript look like?
A: **Pol II**, so the transcript has a **5' cap and a poly-A tail** like an mRNA, but it folds into **hairpins**.
Tags: ncrna, core

---

Q+: Name the steps and the enzyme for each.
A: **Cropping** by **Drosha** in the nucleus; **export** by **exportin 5**; **dicing** by **Dicer** in the cytosol, leaving a duplex of about 22 nucleotides; and **loading** of one strand into **RISC**, with **Argonaute**.
Tags: ncrna, core

---

Q: What decides whether RISC slices an mRNA or just represses it?
A: **How well the miRNA matches the target**, usually in the **3' UTR**. An **extensive match** leads to **slicing**, cleavage by Argonaute and rapid degradation; a **less extensive match** leads to **translational repression**, deadenylation and eventual degradation.
Tags: ncrna, core

---

Q: What is the difference between RISC and RITS?
A: Both are loaded with small RNAs made by Dicer. **RISC** acts **post-transcriptionally**, cleaving or repressing the target mRNA. **RITS** acts in the **nucleus** on the **nascent transcript**, recruiting **histone and DNA methylation** to give **transcriptional repression** and heterochromatin.
Tags: ncrna, core

---

Q: Recite the lecture's take-home messages.
A: **RNA is the information-carrying molecule in gene expression.** **Ribosomes are the site of protein synthesis.** **mRNA is a short-lived DNA-to-protein intermediate.** **Transcription is always 5' to 3'.** **Prokaryotes use one polymerase plus a sigma factor; eukaryotes use Pol I, II and III plus general transcription factors.** **TFIIH and the CTD coordinate RNA processing.** **Splicing increases protein diversity.** **Introns are removed as a lariat by the spliceosome, at an energy cost.** **CPSF, CstF, the cleavage factors and PAP do 3' processing.** **Non-coding RNAs regulate gene expression.**
Tags: core

---

Q: In the mRNA vaccine example, how many deaths were averted in the first year of COVID-19 vaccination?
A: **14.4 million** on the basis of reported COVID deaths, and about **19.8 million** using excess-mortality estimates.
Tags: vaccines, numbers

---

Q: Which parts of a synthetic mRNA are design variables, and what do they change?
A: The **5' and 3' UTRs**, which change the **in-cell half-life** and the **ribosome load**; and the **codon choice**, which changes both **total protein output** and the **secondary structure** the mRNA folds into, because the coding sequence also base-pairs with itself.
Tags: vaccines, core

---

Q+: What did the structure and stability measurements show?
A: Differently codon-optimised versions of the same open reading frame have measurably different **half-lives**, from about **0.36 to 1.14 hours** in the in-solution degradation assay, with **pseudouridine** substitution adding further stability. Reference: **Leppek et al., *Nat. Commun.* 2022**.
Tags: vaccines, numbers
