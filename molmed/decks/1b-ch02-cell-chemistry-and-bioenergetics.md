Q: Treat the cell as a living industrial city. What goes in, and what are the three things that come out?
A: In: **resources and raw materials**. Out: **energy production**, **construction and maintenance**, and **waste**. The whole chapter is the chemistry of those three arrows.

Q: Sharing electrons gives a **{{covalent}}** bond, which is strong and builds macromolecules. Transferring an electron gives an **{{ionic}}** bond, which is easily reversible and lets molecules interact.
A: The two are not just strong and weak versions of each other: the reversible one is what makes interaction, recognition and regulation possible at all.

Q: What happens to the strength of each bond type when you move it from vacuum into water?
A: **Covalent and van der Waals are unaffected.** The **ionic bond collapses**, losing the great majority of its strength, and the **hydrogen bond loses most of its own**. Water attacks precisely the bonds that depend on separated charge, and the in-water values are the only ones a cell ever sees.

![Table 2-1, covalent and noncovalent bond lengths and strengths in vacuum and in water](assets/img/slide-1b-06.png)

Q+: Why do the ionic and hydrogen bonds lose most of their strength in water, while the covalent one does not?
A: Because water is **polar and mobile**: its molecules crowd around a charge or a donor and satisfy it themselves, so forming the bond gains you only the **difference** over what water was already providing. A covalent bond is a shared electron pair, and water has nothing to offer against it.

Read more: [Effect of water on chemical interactions](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/effect-of-water-on-chemical-interactions)
Tags: type/why, core

---

Q+: An ionic bond in water is worth about 3 kcal/mol, roughly thermal energy. How does a cell get specific recognition out of interactions that weak?
A: By using **many at once**. Two surfaces that fit closely can make dozens of hydrogen bonds, salt bridges and van der Waals contacts simultaneously, and the total is both strong and **reversible**, which a covalent bond would not be.

Read more: [Effect of water on chemical interactions](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/effect-of-water-on-chemical-interactions)

Q: Which combinations make a hydrogen bond in biology?
- [x] O-H to O
- [x] N-H to O
- [x] O-H to N
- [x] N-H to N
- [ ] C-H to O
A: The donor is an electronegative atom carrying a hydrogen, the acceptor an electronegative atom with a lone pair. Carbon is not electronegative enough, which is exactly why hydrocarbon chains are hydrophobic.

Q: Define the van der Waals force and the hydrophobic force.
A: **Van der Waals:** a weak attraction between **transient charges** in atoms. **Hydrophobic force:** the **aggregation of hydrophobic molecules caused by their forced exclusion from a polar environment**.

Read more: [Van der Waals and the hydrophobic force](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/van-der-waals-and-the-hydrophobic-force)
Tags: type/discriminate, bonds

---

Q+: On that definition, is the hydrophobic force an attraction between the nonpolar molecules?
A: **No.** Nothing pulls them together; **water pushes them out**, because ordering water around a nonpolar surface is entropically expensive. It is a property of the solvent, not a bond, which is why Table 2-1 gives it no length and no strength.

Read more: [Hydrophobic interactions](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/hydrophobic-interactions)

Q: Name the four families of small organic molecules and what each builds.
A: **Sugars** into polysaccharides, glycogen and starch; **fatty acids** into fats and membrane lipids; **amino acids** into proteins; **nucleotides** into nucleic acids.

Q: Condensation is energetically {{unfavourable}} and hydrolysis is energetically {{favourable}}, so building a macromolecule {{costs energy::what the cell must supply}}.
A: That asymmetry is why the rest of this topic exists: every polymer in the cell is uphill of its subunits.

Q: Subunits become macromolecules by one kind of bond, and macromolecules become assemblies by another. Which, and what is the example?
A: **Covalent** bonds build macromolecules; **noncovalent** bonds assemble them. The example is the **ribosome**, held together entirely by noncovalent interactions between its RNAs and proteins.

Read more: [Two levels of assembly](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/two-levels-of-assembly)
Tags: type/discriminate, core

---

Q+: Why does it matter that the second step is noncovalent?
A: Because it is **reversible**. A ribosome can assemble, work and come apart; a covalently welded one could not. The same weak forces also **drive protein folding and enzymatic activity**.

Read more: [Two levels of assembly](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/two-levels-of-assembly)
Tags: type/why, core

Q: Do cells defy the second law of thermodynamics?
A: **No.** They order their own molecules using energy from food, but they **release heat**, which increases the motion of everything around them. **Heat is energy in its most disordered form**, and the increase outside exceeds the decrease inside.

![Cells take energy from food to order molecules but release heat, increasing disorder overall](assets/img/slide-1b-12.png)

Read more: [Second law of thermodynamics](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/second-law-of-thermodynamics)
Tags: type/figure, type/misconception, core

---

Q+: A child's bedroom is the standard illustration of the second law. Which direction is free, and what does the other one cost?
A: Disorder increases by a **"spontaneous" reaction as time elapses**, and the reverse takes **organised effort requiring energy input**. Order is never free.

![A room becoming disordered spontaneously, and ordered again by organised effort](assets/img/slide-1b-11.png)

Read more: [Second law of thermodynamics](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/second-law-of-thermodynamics)
Tags: type/figure

---

Q: State the first law of thermodynamics, and give two examples of energy being transformed from one form into another.
A: **Energy is not created or destroyed, only transformed, so total energy is conserved.** Examples: a raised brick (potential to kinetic to heat on impact), and a chlorophyll molecule (light to high-energy electrons to chemical-bond energy in photosynthesis).

Read more: [First law of thermodynamics](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/first-law-of-thermodynamics)
Tags: thermodynamics

---

Q: What are the two things catabolism hands to anabolism, and what is lost on the way?
A: **Useful forms of energy** and **the many building blocks for biosynthesis**. **Heat is lost.** Order increases at the anabolic end, and the whole thing is **metabolism, made possible by enzymes**.

![Catabolic pathways feeding useful energy and building blocks into anabolic pathways](assets/img/slide-1b-13.png)

Read more: [Metabolism](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/metabolism)
Tags: type/figure, core

---

Q: Write out both halves of the carbon cycle as equations.
A: **Photosynthesis:** CO₂ + H₂O gives O₂ + sugars, driven by sunlight. **Cellular respiration:** sugars + O₂ gives H₂O + CO₂, releasing useful chemical-bond energy. Respiration is an **oxidation**.

Read more: [Carbon cycles through the biosphere](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/carbon-cycles-through-the-biosphere)
Tags: redox, core

---

Q: Give three equivalent definitions of oxidation.
A: **Removal of electrons**; **H atoms replaced by O atoms**; **a decreased number of C-H bonds**. Reading the ladder downward, **methane, methanol, formaldehyde, formic acid, carbon dioxide**, each step does all three at once.

![The oxidation ladder from methane to carbon dioxide, with oxidation and reduction arrows](assets/img/slide-1b-17.png)

Read more: [The oxidation ladder](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/the-oxidation-ladder)
Tags: type/figure, redox, core

---

Q+: Why must oxidation and reduction always happen together?
A: **Conservation of electrons.** An electron removed from one molecule has to be accepted by another, so every oxidation is somebody else's reduction.

Read more: [Reduction](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/reduction)
Tags: type/why, core

---

Q+: Which end of that ladder has more energy available to a cell, and why does that explain fat?
A: The **reduced** end. Methane and the hydrocarbon tail of a fatty acid are full of C-H bonds, and oxidising them all the way to CO₂ releases a great deal of energy. That is why fat, whose carbons are almost fully reduced, stores more energy per gram than carbohydrate.

Read more: [The oxidation ladder](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/the-oxidation-ladder)
Tags: type/apply, core

Q: What is $G$, and what does the sign of $\Delta G$ mean in terms of order?
A: $G$ is the energy available **to do work**, for example to drive a reaction. A **reduction of $G$** ($\Delta G < 0$) goes with a **reduction of order** and the reaction is favourable; an **increase of $G$** ($\Delta G > 0$) goes with an **increase of order** and energy must be supplied.

Read more: [Gibbs free energy](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/gibbs-free-energy)
Tags: thermodynamics, core

---

Q: A reaction has $\Delta G < 0$ but does not happen. What is missing, and what supplies it?
A: The **activation energy**, the barrier to the transition state. It comes from **the heat of a lighted match, or simply a collision between molecules**, and in cells it is lowered by enzymes rather than supplied as heat.

![A favourable reaction still has an activation energy barrier to cross](assets/img/slide-1b-20.png)

Read more: [Activation energy and enzymes](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/activation-energy-and-enzymes)
Tags: type/why, type/figure, core

---

Q+: Comparing the uncatalysed and catalysed energy diagrams, which levels change and which do not?
A: The **barrier** drops, from a to d. The **reactant level (b)** and the **product level (c)** are identical in both, so **$\Delta G$ is unchanged**: an enzyme changes the rate, never the equilibrium.

Read more: [Enzymes](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/enzymes)

Q: A reaction profile shows a reduction in free energy G. Which of these could it represent?
![Quiz slide asking which reactions could show a reduction in free energy](assets/img/slide-1b-21.png)
- [ ] Addition of an amino acid during the synthesis of β-globin
- [ ] Photosynthesis in *aloe vera*
- [x] Hydrolysis of glycogen to glucose
- [x] A reaction that releases heat
A: Adding an amino acid to a chain is a **condensation**, uphill. Photosynthesis is driven **by light** to a higher-energy product, also uphill. Hydrolysis is downhill, and a reaction that releases heat is giving up energy to its surroundings.

Q+: Why does such a reaction count as "spontaneous"?
- [x] It requires no energy input to occur
- [ ] It happens very quickly
- [ ] It cannot be stopped or slowed down
- [ ] It can also occur without enzymes
A: Spontaneity is **thermodynamic, not kinetic**. A spontaneous reaction may be immeasurably slow and is usually regulated; that it can proceed without an enzyme is a consequence, not the definition.

Q: How does a cell drive an unfavourable reaction, and what carries the energy between the two halves?
A: By **coupling** it to a favourable one so that **$\Delta G_{total} < 0$**. The coupling is done by an **activated carrier molecule**, loaded by catabolism and unloaded in anabolism, so the two reactions never have to meet.

Read more: [Reaction coupling](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/reaction-coupling)
Tags: coupling, core

---

Q: Which bonds hold ATP's energy, and what are the signs of ΔG° in each direction?
A: The two **phosphoanhydride** bonds. **Making ATP from ADP and Pᵢ has $\Delta G° > 0$** and is paid for by sunlight or food; **hydrolysis has $\Delta G° < 0$** and drives unfavourable reactions.

Read more: [ATP](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/atp)
Tags: carriers, core

---

Q: ATP joins A-OH to B-H in two steps. Name them and give the net equation.
A: **Activation:** ATP transfers a phosphate to A-OH, giving the high-energy intermediate **A-O-P** plus ADP. **Condensation:** A-O-P reacts with B-H to give **A-B**, releasing Pᵢ. Net: **A-OH + B-H + ATP gives A-B + ADP + Pᵢ**.

Read more: [Reaction coupling](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/reaction-coupling)
Tags: carriers, core

---

Q+: Which reaction is the worked example, and which route does not work?
A: **Glutamic acid + ammonia to glutamine.** The **direct** condensation does not go at all without the ATP-driven activation step.

Read more: [Reaction coupling](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/reaction-coupling)
Tags: carriers

---

Q+: Why activate the acid rather than simply "pushing harder" on the direct reaction?
A: Because the direct condensation is **thermodynamically uphill**, and no amount of catalysis changes that. Phosphorylating the carboxyl makes a **new, high-energy intermediate** whose reaction with ammonia is downhill, so the coupled route has a negative overall ΔG.

Read more: [Reaction coupling](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/reaction-coupling)
Tags: type/why, core

Q: What does NADPH carry, and what happens to the two molecules in one turn of the NADP⁺/NADPH cycle?
A: It carries **two electrons and one proton** (a hydride). **Molecule 1 is oxidised** as NADP⁺ takes them up; the **NADPH then reduces molecule 2**. The example is the last step of cholesterol synthesis, **7-dehydrocholesterol to cholesterol**.

Read more: [NADH and NADPH](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/nadh-and-nadph)
Tags: carriers

Q: Acetyl-CoA carries what, on what kind of bond?
A: An activated **two-carbon acetyl group** on a **high-energy thioester bond** to the sulfur of coenzyme A. It supplies the two carbons for building larger molecules, and it is the entry point to the citric acid cycle.

Read more: [Acetyl-CoA](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/acetyl-coa)
Tags: carriers, core

Q: Succinate dehydrogenase converts succinate to fumarate, and FAD becomes FADH₂. Which statements are true?

Q: What does glycolysis hand on to the rest of metabolism, and under what conditions does it run?
A: A **small net gain of ATP** made by substrate-level phosphorylation, plus **reduced NADH** and **two pyruvate** per glucose. It runs in the **cytoplasm** and needs **no oxygen**. Its real value is the pyruvate and the reduced carrier it passes on, not the ATP.

![The ten steps of glycolysis in three phases](assets/img/slide-1b-31.png)

Read more: [Glycolysis](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/glycolysis)
Tags: type/figure, type/why, core

---

Q+: What are the three phases of those ten steps?
A: **Energy investment** (steps 1 to 3, two ATP spent, ending at fructose 1,6-bisphosphate); **cleavage** of the six-carbon sugar into **two three-carbon sugars** (steps 4 to 5); **energy generation** (steps 6 to 10), each three-carbon unit yielding one NADH and two ATP.

Read more: [Glycolysis](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/glycolysis)
Tags: type/sequence

---

Q: Without oxygen, why must pyruvate be reduced at all? What would happen if it were not?
A: To **regenerate NAD⁺**. Step 6 of glycolysis needs NAD⁺ as the electron acceptor, and the cell's pool is small, so with NADH accumulating **glycolysis itself stops** and no ATP is made. Fermentation exists to recycle the carrier, not to extract more energy.

Read more: [Pyruvate without oxygen](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/pyruvate-without-oxygen)
Tags: type/why, core

---

Q+: What does a muscle cell make, and what does yeast make?
A: Muscle: **lactate**. Yeast: **ethanol plus CO₂**, by first decarboxylating pyruvate. Same purpose, different waste product.

Read more: [Pyruvate without oxygen](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/pyruvate-without-oxygen)

Q: With oxygen present, what is the reaction that gets pyruvate into the citric acid cycle?
A: **Pyruvate + CoA-SH + NAD⁺ gives acetyl CoA + NADH + CO₂**, catalysed by pyruvate dehydrogenase in the mitochondrion. One of the three carbons leaves immediately as CO₂.

Read more: [Pyruvate with oxygen](#/chapter/ch02-cell-chemistry-and-bioenergetics/pyruvate-with-oxygen)

Q: What does one turn of the citric acid cycle yield, and what is regenerated?
A: Mostly **reduced electron carriers**, NADH and FADH₂, together with a single nucleotide triphosphate and the **two carbons leaving as CO₂**. **Oxaloacetate** is regenerated to accept the next acetyl group, which is what makes it a cycle rather than a pathway.

Q+: Follow the carbon count around the cycle.
A: **4C oxaloacetate + 2C acetyl gives 6C citrate**; 6C to **5C** losing one CO₂; 5C to **4C** losing the second; then 4C round to oxaloacetate. Two carbons in, two carbons out as CO₂.

Q+: The cycle produces only one GTP per turn. So why is it the centre of energy metabolism?
A: Because its real product is **reduced carriers**: 3 NADH and 1 FADH₂ per turn, which feed the electron transport chain where the bulk of the ATP is made. The cycle is an electron-harvesting device, not an ATP-making one.

Q: Where do the electrons from NADH and FADH₂ finally go, and how does that make ATP?
A: Through complexes **I to IV**, with **Q** and **cytochrome c**, to **O₂**, which is reduced to **water**. The complexes **pump H⁺** across the inner membrane, and the H⁺ flowing back through **ATP synthase** makes ATP. Electron transport plus chemiosmosis is **oxidative phosphorylation**.

Q: How are fatty acids oxidised, and what comes out of each turn?
A: In the **fatty acid cycle** in mitochondria: **two carbons removed per cycle** as **acetyl CoA**, plus **one FADH₂ and one NADH**, repeating until the chain is consumed. The acetyl CoA enters the citric acid cycle.

A: Stored **triacylglycerol** is **hydrolysed** to fatty acids and glycerol; the fatty acids enter the **bloodstream**; a **muscle cell** takes them up; they are **oxidised in its mitochondria** to CO₂, yielding ATP.

Q+: Why does the body keep both a one-day store and a one-month store rather than only the larger one?
A: Because they do different jobs. **Glycogen** is quickly mobilised, water-soluble and can supply glucose to tissues that need it, including in the absence of oxygen. **Fat** is far denser per gram, since its carbons are more reduced, but it is slower to mobilise and cannot be converted to glucose.

Q: **Exam-shaped question.** "Explain how a cell carries out an energetically unfavourable biosynthetic reaction, using a named example."
A: A checklist for marks:

| Point | Content |
|---|---|
| The problem | Condensation reactions have $\Delta G > 0$, so polymers are uphill of their subunits |
| The principle | Coupling: pair the unfavourable reaction with a favourable one so $\Delta G_{total} < 0$ |
| The courier | Activated carriers, loaded by catabolism, carrying energy in high-energy bonds |
| Worked example | Glutamic acid + NH₃ to glutamine: ATP first makes the high-energy intermediate A-O-P, which then condenses with ammonia |
| Net | A-OH + B-H + ATP gives A-B + ADP + Pᵢ |
| Where the energy came from | Food oxidation or sunlight, via ATP, NADH, NADPH, FADH₂, acetyl CoA |
