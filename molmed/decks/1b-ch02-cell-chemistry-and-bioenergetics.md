Q: The lecture opens with an analogy for the cell. What is it, and what are the three outputs?
A: **The cell as a living industrial city.** Resources and raw materials come in; what comes out is **energy production**, **construction and maintenance**, and **waste**.
Tags: core

---

Q: Two atoms can share electrons or transfer one. Which gives which bond, and what is each good for?
A: **Sharing of electrons gives a covalent bond**, which is strong and is what **(macro)molecules are built from**. **Transfer of an electron gives an ionic bond**, a positive and a negative ion, which is **easily reversible and therefore allows molecules to interact**.
Tags: bonds, core

---

Q: A hydrogen bond needs a donor and an acceptor. Which four combinations does the slide list?
A: **O-H···O**, **O-H···N**, **N-H···O** and **N-H···N**. The donor carries the hydrogen; the acceptor is the electronegative atom it points at.
Tags: bonds

---

Q+: Why do those weak interactions matter so much?
A: Because there are **very large numbers of them**. They are what **keeps water a liquid at room temperature**, and why **polar and charged molecules dissolve easily in water**, which is what "hydrophilic" means.
Tags: bonds

---

Q: How does the lecture define the two weak interactions that involve no charge at all?
A: **Van der Waals forces** are weak attractions between **transient charges** in atoms. The **hydrophobic force** is the **aggregation of hydrophobic molecules caused by their forced exclusion from a polar environment**.
Tags: bonds

---

Q: In Table 2-1 the lecturer circles one column in red. Which, and why does it matter?
A: The **strength in water** column. A covalent bond is **377 kJ/mol in vacuum and in water alike**, but an **ionic bond falls from 335 to 12.6 kJ/mol** and a **hydrogen bond from 16.7 to 4.2**. Water screens charges, so inside a cell the noncovalent interactions are individually feeble and only work in numbers.
Tags: bonds, numbers, core

Q: Put the four bond types in order of length, from shortest to longest.
- [x] covalent 0.15 nm, ionic 0.25 nm, hydrogen 0.30 nm, van der Waals 0.35 nm
- [ ] hydrogen 0.15 nm, covalent 0.25 nm, ionic 0.30 nm, van der Waals 0.35 nm
- [ ] van der Waals 0.15 nm, hydrogen 0.25 nm, ionic 0.30 nm, covalent 0.35 nm
A: The order of length is also the order of strength: the shortest bond is the strongest. Table 2-1 adds the hydrophobic force at the bottom with no entry, because it is not a bond.

Q: Name the four families of small organic molecules and the larger molecule each builds.
A: **Sugars** to polysaccharides, glycogen and starch; **fatty acids** to fats and membrane lipids; **amino acids** to proteins; **nucleotides** to nucleic acids.

![The four main families of small organic molecules in cells: sugars, fatty acids, amino acids and nucleotides, with the macromolecules each forms](assets/img/fig-2-06.png)

Q: Condensation is energetically **{{unfavourable}}** and hydrolysis is energetically **{{favourable}}**, which is why building a macromolecule **{{requires energy}}**.
A: The two are the same reaction run in opposite directions: condensation joins two subunits and releases a water molecule, hydrolysis uses a water molecule to break the bond.

![Condensation joins two subunits with the loss of a water molecule, while hydrolysis breaks the bond by adding water back](assets/img/fig-2-09.png)
Tags: molecules, core

---

Q: The lecture separates two levels of assembly. What are they, and which bonds does each use?
A: **Subunits to macromolecules by covalent bonds**, and **macromolecules to macromolecular assemblies by noncovalent bonds**. The example of the second is the **ribosome**, about **30 nm** across, built from RNA molecules and globular proteins without a single new covalent bond.
Tags: core, molecules

---

Q+: What does the lecturer conclude from that second step?
A: That **noncovalent forces drive protein folding and enzymatic activity**. The interactions that looked negligible in Table 2-1 are the ones that build and run the cell, because they are reversible.
Tags: core

---

Q: "Do cells defy the second law of thermodynamics?"
A: **No.** Cells take energy from food to order their own molecules, but they **release heat**, which increases the motion of everything around them, so **disorder increases overall**. **Heat is energy in its most disordered form.**

![A cell taking in energy and releasing heat, ordering its own molecules while increasing the disorder of the surrounding sea of matter](assets/img/fig-2-16.png)
Tags: thermodynamics, core

---

Q: What does the first law of thermodynamics say, in the lecture's wording?
A: **Energy is not created or destroyed**, only transformed, so **total energy is always conserved**. The examples are a falling brick (potential to kinetic to heat) and a chlorophyll molecule (light to high-energy electrons to chemical-bond energy).
Tags: thermodynamics

---

Q: Write the two halves of the carbon cycle as the slide writes them.
A: **Photosynthesis: CO₂ + H₂O gives O₂ + sugars**, driven by sunlight, in plants, algae and some bacteria. **Cellular respiration: sugars + O₂ gives H₂O + CO₂**, in most living organisms, releasing useful chemical-bond energy. Respiration is an **oxidation**.
Tags: redox, core

---

Q: Define oxidation three ways, as the lecture does.
A: **Removal of electrons**; **H atoms replaced by O atoms**; a **decreased number of C-H bonds**. Reduction is the reverse, and the two **always occur simultaneously**, because the number of electrons is conserved.
Tags: redox, core

---

Q+: Put the lecture's one-carbon ladder in order from most reduced to most oxidised.
A: **Methane, methanol, formaldehyde, formic acid, carbon dioxide.** Reading down is oxidation, reading up is reduction; each step trades a C-H bond for a bond to oxygen.

![Formation of a polar covalent bond in which one atom gains a greater share of the electrons, and the ladder from methane through methanol, formaldehyde and formic acid to carbon dioxide](assets/img/fig-2-20.png)
Tags: redox

---

Q: What is G, and what do the two signs of ΔG mean on the lecture's slide?
A: **G is the energy available "to do work"**, for example to drive a chemical reaction. A **reduction of G (ΔG < 0)** goes with a **reduction of order** and the reaction is favourable; an **increase of G (ΔG > 0)** goes with an **increase of order** and the reaction needs energy put in.
Tags: thermodynamics, core

---

Q: A reaction has ΔG < 0. Why might it still not happen?
A: Because of the **activation energy**: the barrier the reactants must get over first. Something has to supply it, such as **the heat of a lighted match, or simply a collision between molecules**.
Tags: enzymes, core

---

Q+: What exactly does an enzyme change on that energy diagram, and what does it leave alone?
A: It **lowers the activation energy**, so the barrier drops from a to d. The **reactant level and the product level are identical** in the catalysed and uncatalysed diagrams, so **ΔG is unchanged**.

![Energy diagram showing the activation energy barrier between a reactant and a lower-energy product, and how a catalyst lowers that barrier](assets/img/fig-2-21.png)
Tags: enzymes, core

---

Q: Quiz slide. A graph shows a reaction with a reduction in free energy G. Which could it represent?
- [ ] Addition of an amino acid during the synthesis of β-globin
- [ ] Photosynthesis in *aloe vera*
- [x] Hydrolysis of glycogen to glucose
- [x] A reaction that releases heat
A: The two biosynthetic options are energetically uphill: adding an amino acid to a chain is a condensation, and photosynthesis drives a molecule to a higher-energy product using light. Hydrolysis and a heat-releasing reaction are both downhill.
Tags: quiz, thermodynamics

---

Q: Quiz slide. That reaction is called "spontaneous" because:
- [x] It requires no energy input to occur
- [ ] It happens very quickly
- [ ] It cannot be stopped or slowed down
- [ ] It can also occur without enzymes
A: "Spontaneous" is a statement about thermodynamics, not about rate. A spontaneous reaction can be immeasurably slow, and it can certainly be regulated. It is true that it can occur without an enzyme, but that is a consequence, not the definition.
Tags: quiz, thermodynamics

---

Q: How does a cell drive an energetically unfavourable reaction?
A: By **coupling** it to a favourable one so that **ΔG_total < 0**. In the cell the coupling is done by a courier: **catabolism** loads an **activated carrier molecule** with energy in energy-rich covalent bonds, and the carrier drives **anabolism**. The two halves never have to meet.

![An energetically unfavourable reaction driven by being coupled to a favourable one, so that the net free-energy change is negative](assets/img/fig-2-29.png)
Tags: coupling, core

---

Q: Which bonds in ATP carry the energy, and where does that energy come from?
A: The two **phosphoanhydride bonds**. Making ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate has **ΔG° > 0** and is paid for by **sunlight or the breakdown of food**; hydrolysing it has **ΔG° < 0** and the energy released **drives energetically unfavourable reactions**.

![ATP synthesis by phosphorylation of ADP and its hydrolysis back to ADP and inorganic phosphate, showing the phosphoanhydride bonds](assets/img/fig-2-33.png)
Tags: carriers, core

---

Q: ATP joins two molecules in two steps. What are they?
A: **Activation:** ATP transfers a phosphate to A-OH, giving a **high-energy intermediate A-O-P** and ADP. **Condensation:** the intermediate reacts with **B-H** to give **A-B**, releasing inorganic phosphate. Net result: **A-OH + B-H + ATP gives A-B + ADP + Pᵢ**.
Tags: carriers, core

---

Q+: Which reaction does the lecture use to show it?
A: **Glutamic acid plus ammonia to glutamine.** The direct reaction is crossed out in red on the slide, because it does not go without the ATP-driven activation step.
Tags: carriers

---

Q: What does NADPH carry, and what happens to the two molecules in the slide's cycle?
A: It carries **two electrons and one H⁺**, a hydride. **Molecule 1 is oxidised** as NADP⁺ takes them up, and the **NADPH then reduces molecule 2**. The biological example is the last step of cholesterol synthesis: **7-dehydrocholesterol plus NADPH gives cholesterol**.
Tags: carriers

---

Q: What does acetyl-CoA carry, and on what kind of bond?
A: An **activated two-carbon acetyl group**, attached to the sulfur of coenzyme A by a **high-energy thioester bond**. It provides the two carbons used in the biosynthesis of larger molecules.

![The structure of acetyl CoA, with the sulfur atom forming a thioester bond to acetate](assets/img/fig-2-38.png)
Tags: carriers

---

Q: Table 2-3 lists six activated carriers. Which group does each carry?
A: **ATP**: phosphate. **NADH, NADPH, FADH₂**: electrons and hydrogens. **Acetyl CoA**: acetyl group. **Carboxylated biotin**: carboxyl group. **S-adenosylmethionine**: methyl group. **Uridine diphosphate glucose**: glucose.
Tags: carriers, core

---

Q+: Which three of those are the ones people forget, and what are they for?
A: **Biotin** for carboxylation reactions, **S-adenosylmethionine** for every methylation in the cell, and **UDP-glucose** for building polysaccharides and glycogen.
Tags: carriers

---

Q: Quiz slide. Succinate dehydrogenase converts succinate to fumarate and FAD becomes FADH₂. Which statements are true?
- [ ] It is an oxidation reaction since both succinate and FAD are oxidised
- [x] Succinate is oxidised while FAD is reduced
- [x] The reaction has negative ΔG
- [ ] The reaction has positive ΔG
- [ ] FAD can later be regenerated by reducing FADH₂
A: Both partners cannot be oxidised, since electrons are conserved: two hydrogens leave succinate, giving a C=C double bond, and FAD takes them up. The last option is false as written, because regenerating FAD from FADH₂ means **oxidising** it, which is what the electron transport chain does.
Tags: quiz, redox

---

Q: Give the ATP and NADH bookkeeping for glycolysis.
A: **2 ATP consumed**, **4 ATP and 2 NADH generated**, so the **net gain is 2 ATP** and 2 NADH per glucose, plus **2 pyruvate**. It happens in the **cytoplasm**.

![Outline of the ten steps of glycolysis, showing the energy-investment phase, the cleavage of a six-carbon sugar into two three-carbon sugars, and the energy-generation phase](assets/img/fig-2-46.png)
Tags: metabolism, numbers, core

---

Q+: What are the three blocks of the ten steps?
A: An **energy-investment** phase (two ATP spent, ending at fructose 1,6-bisphosphate), the **cleavage of the six-carbon sugar into two three-carbon sugars** (glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate), and an **energy-generation** phase, where each three-carbon unit yields one NADH and two ATP.
Tags: metabolism

---

Q: Without oxygen, what happens to pyruvate, and what is the point of it?
A: It is **reduced to lactate** in a vigorously active muscle cell, or in yeast **decarboxylated and reduced to ethanol plus CO₂**. Either way the point is to **regenerate NAD⁺**, without which glycolysis itself stops.

![Fermentation in a vigorously active muscle cell producing lactate, and fermentation in yeast producing ethanol and carbon dioxide](assets/img/fig-2-50.png)
Tags: metabolism, core

---

Q: With oxygen present, pyruvate enters the mitochondrion. What is the reaction there?
A: **Pyruvate + CoA-SH + NAD⁺ gives acetyl CoA + NADH + CO₂.** One of the three carbons leaves as carbon dioxide.
Tags: metabolism

---

Q: What does one turn of the citric acid cycle produce?
A: **3 NADH, 1 GTP, 1 FADH₂ and 2 CO₂**, with **oxaloacetate regenerated** to accept the next acetyl group.

![Overview of the citric acid cycle, with acetyl CoA joining oxaloacetate to form citrate and the cycle regenerating oxaloacetate](assets/img/fig-2-58.png)
Tags: metabolism, numbers, core

---

Q+: Follow the carbons round the cycle.
A: **4C oxaloacetate + 2C acetyl group gives 6C citrate**, 6C to **5C** losing one CO₂, 5C to **4C** losing the second, then 4C through to oxaloacetate again. Amino acids and fatty acids can also enter, as acetyl CoA or as other intermediates.
Tags: metabolism

---

Q: The lecture flags oxidative phosphorylation as "the next episodes". What is the outline it shows?
A: NADH and FADH₂ give their electrons to a chain of **protein complexes I to IV**, with **Q** and **cytochrome c**, which **pump H⁺** across the inner mitochondrial membrane. The H⁺ flows back through **ATP synthase**, making ATP. Electron transport plus **chemiosmosis** is oxidative phosphorylation, and O₂ is the final acceptor, reduced to water.
Tags: metabolism

---

Q: How are fatty acids oxidised, and what comes out per cycle?
A: In the **fatty acid cycle in mitochondria**: **two carbons are removed per cycle** as **acetyl CoA**, plus **one FADH₂ and one NADH**, and the cycle repeats until the fatty acid is completely oxidised. The acetyl CoA enters the citric acid cycle.
Tags: metabolism, core

---

Q: How long does each energy store last, on the lecture's slides?
A: **Glycogen: about one day** of normal activity. **Fat: about one month.**
Tags: storage, numbers, core

---

Q+: How is each mobilised?
A: **Glycogen phosphorylase** releases **glucose 1-phosphate**, which becomes **glucose 6-phosphate** and enters glycolysis. **Stored fat** is **hydrolysed to fatty acids and glycerol**; the fatty acids travel in the **bloodstream**, are taken up by a muscle cell, and are **oxidised in its mitochondria** to CO₂ and ATP.
Tags: storage

---

Q: Besides ATP, what else do glycolysis and the citric acid cycle provide?
A: **Precursors for biosynthesis.** From glycolysis: glucose 6-phosphate to nucleotides, fructose 6-phosphate to amino sugars and glycolipids, dihydroxyacetone phosphate to lipids, 3-phosphoglycerate to serine, phosphoenolpyruvate to amino acids and pyrimidines, pyruvate to alanine. From the cycle: citrate to cholesterol and fatty acids, α-ketoglutarate to glutamate and purines, succinyl CoA to heme, oxaloacetate to aspartate and pyrimidines.

![Glycolysis and the citric acid cycle shown feeding branches that lead to nucleotides, amino acids, lipids, sugars and other biosynthetic products](assets/img/fig-2-60.png)
Tags: metabolism, core

---

Q: The lecture ends on a KEGG metabolic map. What is it there to show?
A: That the pathways just covered are a **thin thread through a few thousand reactions**: glucose 6-phosphate, pyruvate, acetyl CoA and the citric acid cycle are highlighted in red on a page of grey. It is called "a more realistic, still simplified, view".
Tags: metabolism

---

Q: Recite the lecture's closing key points.
A: **Biomolecules to polymers costs energy** (ΔG > 0). **Enzymes lower the activation energy and energy carriers store the energy derived from food.** **Food: sugars to glycolysis in the cytoplasm, fat to the fatty acid cycle in mitochondria.** **In mitochondria: acetyl CoA to the citric acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation.** **Intermediates from glycolysis and the citric acid cycle are also precursors for biosynthesis.**
Tags: core
