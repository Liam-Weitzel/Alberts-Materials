Q: Part II of this topic covers the second half of the pathway from gene to protein. Which steps are those, and what do they have in common?
A: **Export, translation, folding, and the degradation of both the mRNA and the protein.** What they have in common is that **every one of them is a regulatory step**: the cell controls output at each, not only at transcription.

Read more: [Export-ready mRNA and its transport](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/export-ready-mrna-and-its-transport)
Tags: type/why, core

Q: An mRNA leaves the nucleus as a particle rather than as a bare strand. What is it wearing, and what is the coat for?
A: A coat of proteins laid down during processing: **hnRNPs**, **SR proteins**, **exon junction complexes**, **poly-A-binding proteins**, the **cap-binding complex** and a **nuclear export receptor**. The coat is a **record of what has been done to the transcript**, so it is what export and quality control read.

![An export-ready mRNA passing through the nuclear pore, with some proteins travelling with it, others staying behind, and cytosolic versions replacing the nuclear ones](assets/img/fig-6-40.png)

Read more: [Export-ready mRNA and its transport](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/export-ready-mrna-and-its-transport)
Tags: type/figure, core

---

Q+: Which proteins are stripped off at the nuclear pore, and why does that amount to a checkpoint?
A: The **nucleus-restricted** ones, hnRNPs above all. They are only removed once processing is complete, so a transcript still wearing them **is not exported**. Nothing has to inspect the sequence: the coat itself reports whether the message is finished.
Read more: [Export-ready mRNA and its transport](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/export-ready-mrna-and-its-transport)
Tags: type/why, core

---

Q+: What takes over the 5' cap once the mRNA reaches the cytosol?
A: The **cap-binding complex** is exchanged for the **initiation factors for protein synthesis**, **eIF4E** on the cap itself with **eIF4G** behind it. That exchange is what converts an exported message into a translatable one.
Read more: [Export-ready mRNA and its transport](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/export-ready-mrna-and-its-transport)
Tags: core

---

Q: A translatable mRNA is circular rather than linear. What does closing the loop buy the cell?
A: Three things:

| Benefit | How the loop delivers it |
|---|---|
| **Quality control** | The loop needs **both** an intact cap and an intact poly-A tail to close |
| **Efficiency** | A ribosome terminating at the 3' end is released beside the 5' end and can re-initiate |
| **Stability** | Both ends are covered by protein, and both ends are where exonucleases attack |

Read more: [Export-ready mRNA and its transport](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/export-ready-mrna-and-its-transport)
Tags: core

---

Q+: Why is circularisation a quality check rather than merely a convenience?
A: Because **eIF4G at the cap has to reach the poly-A-binding proteins at the tail**. A truncated, uncapped or deadenylated message physically **cannot close the loop**, so it is never translated efficiently. One structural requirement tests the integrity of **both ends at once**.
Read more: [Export-ready mRNA and its transport](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/export-ready-mrna-and-its-transport)
Tags: type/why, core

Q: Some ribosomes sit on the endoplasmic reticulum and some float free in the cytosol. What decides which is which?
- [x] What the ribosome happens to be translating
- [ ] The ribosome is built differently for each location
- [ ] Free ribosomes are inactive reserves
- [ ] ER ribosomes are permanently anchored for life
A: The ribosome is **not committed in advance**. Proteins destined for **secretion or the membrane system** are made on the ER, proteins that stay in the **cytosol** are made free. The signal is in the nascent chain, which is chapter 12's subject.

Q: Compare the bacterial and eukaryotic ribosome. What is the same, what differs, and why do the S values not add up?
A: **Same plan**: two subunits, one large and one small, that join only on a message. **The eukaryotic ribosome is larger at every level**: bigger overall, longer rRNAs and more proteins. The **S values are sedimentation coefficients**, which depend on **shape as well as mass**, so they are **not additive**: a 50S plus a 30S gives a 70S.

![Bacterial 70S and eukaryotic 80S ribosomes broken down into their subunits, rRNAs and protein counts](assets/img/fig-6-65.png)

Read more: [Two sizes, one machine](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/two-sizes-one-machine)
Tags: type/figure, type/discriminate, ribosome, core

Q+: Why is that size difference worth knowing rather than being a piece of trivia?
A: Because it is **what antibiotics exploit**. A bacterial ribosome differs enough from a eukaryotic one that a compound can jam one and leave the other running, and that is the whole basis of ribosome-targeting antibiotics.
Read more: [Antibiotics and the ribosome](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/antibiotics-and-the-ribosome)

Q: A ribosome is built from rRNA and ribosomal proteins. Which of the two provides the structure, and which does the chemistry?
A: **The RNA does both.** rRNA gives the **overall structure** and performs the **catalysis**; it is roughly **two thirds of the mass**. The proteins sit mainly on the **surface and in the crevices**, stabilising the fold and improving efficiency rather than carrying the function.

![The bacterial large subunit with rRNA in blue and its proteins in green, occupying the surface and crevices](assets/img/fig-6-72.png)

Read more: [Mostly RNA, and the RNA does the work](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/mostly-rna-and-the-rna-does-the-work)
Tags: type/figure, ribosome, core

---

Q+: What does that make the ribosome, and which other machine from this chapter is the same kind of thing?
A: A **ribozyme**: the peptidyl transferase centre is made of RNA. The **spliceosome** is the other one, with a catalytic core of U6 and U2 RNA. Both are RNA machines wearing a protein exoskeleton, which is the strongest surviving evidence for an **RNA world**.
Read more: [Mostly RNA, and the RNA does the work](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/mostly-rna-and-the-rna-does-the-work)
Tags: type/why, ribosome, core

Q+: A common assumption is that the ribosomal proteins do the catalysis and the rRNA is scaffolding. Why is that backwards?
A: Because the **catalytic centre contains no protein at all**. Removing or mutating rRNA at the active site abolishes activity; the proteins are peripheral. Evolution built the machine out of RNA first and added protein afterwards, which is the opposite of the intuitive order.
Read more: [Mostly RNA, and the RNA does the work](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/mostly-rna-and-the-rna-does-the-work)
Tags: type/misconception, core

Q: Colour a bacterial and a eukaryotic ribosome by how conserved each region is. What pattern appears, and what does it say about the machine's history?
A: A **universally conserved core**, which is the part that does the chemistry, with **eukaryote-specific extensions built on top of it**. The machine was **solved once, very early**, and has been decorated since rather than redesigned.

![Aligned rRNA sequence from human, Methanococcus and E. coli beside bacterial and eukaryotic ribosome structures coloured by which parts are universally conserved](assets/img/slide-5-09.png)

Q: A genome carries many copies of the rRNA gene but usually one copy of a protein-coding gene. Why the difference?
A: Because a cell needs an **enormous number of ribosomes**, and **one gene transcribed at the maximum possible rate cannot supply them**. For rRNA the product is the RNA itself, so output cannot be amplified by translating each transcript many times. The only remaining route is **amplification by gene copy number**.
Read more: [Why a cell needs so many rRNA genes](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/why-a-cell-needs-so-many-rrna-genes)
Tags: type/why, nucleolus, core

Q: What is the nucleolus, physically, and what is it built around?
A: A **condensate**, not a membrane-bounded organelle. It **nucleates at the rDNA loci** themselves, and **rDNA loci from different chromosomes are drawn together into one nucleolus**, which then organises into concentric phases that work as a **ribosome assembly line**.
Read more: [The nucleolus is built by the genes it transcribes](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/the-nucleolus-is-built-by-the-genes-it-transcribes)
Tags: nucleolus, core

---

Q+: Why does the nucleolus disperse at mitosis and reappear afterwards?
A: Because **rRNA transcription stops** while the chromosomes are condensed, and the nucleolus is **held together by the activity going on inside it**. A compartment maintained by a process cannot outlive that process, which is exactly what distinguishes a condensate from a membrane-bounded organelle.
Read more: [The nucleolus is built by the genes it transcribes](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/the-nucleolus-is-built-by-the-genes-it-transcribes)
Tags: type/why, nucleolus, core

---

Q: Trace a ribosomal subunit from gene to working ribosome, and say where each stage happens.
A: **rRNA is transcribed and processed in the nucleolus**; **ribosomal proteins are made in the cytoplasm and imported**; the two are assembled into **immature subunits in the nucleolus**; the **large and small subunits are exported separately**; each is **completed by additional assembly in the cytoplasm**; and the two **join only on an mRNA**, at initiation.

![The nucleolus taking in rRNA gene transcripts and ribosomal proteins from the cytoplasm, processing and assembling immature subunits that are exported through nuclear pores](assets/img/fig-6-49.png)

Read more: [Where the subunits are actually finished](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/where-the-subunits-are-actually-finished)
Tags: type/figure, type/sequence, nucleolus, core

---

Q+: Assembly is often said to happen either in the nucleolus or in the cytoplasm. Why is neither answer right on its own?
A: Because it **begins in the nucleolus and finishes in the cytoplasm**. Immature subunits leave the nucleus and undergo further assembly steps once out. Treating it as one location misses that export happens **mid-assembly**, which is itself a checkpoint against exporting a defective subunit.
Read more: [Where the subunits are actually finished](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/where-the-subunits-are-actually-finished)
Tags: type/misconception, nucleolus, core

---

Q+: Ribosomal proteins are made by ribosomes in the cytoplasm and then carried back into the nucleus. What does that circularity imply?
A: That a cell **cannot build a ribosome from scratch**: it needs working ribosomes to make the parts of the next ones. Ribosomes come only from ribosomes, which is the same autocatalytic loop that makes a cell the smallest thing that can reproduce itself.
Read more: [Where the subunits are actually finished](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/where-the-subunits-are-actually-finished)
Tags: type/why, core

---

Q+: Besides ribosomal subunits, what else is assembled in the nucleolus?
A: **Telomerase**, and some tRNA processing happens there too. The nucleolus is better thought of as a general **ribonucleoprotein assembly site** than as a ribosome-only factory.
Read more: [Where the subunits are actually finished](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/where-the-subunits-are-actually-finished)
Tags: nucleolus

Q: Translating a message needs the ribosome and one further component. What is it, and what problem does it solve?
A: **tRNA.** Nothing about a codon physically resembles an amino acid, so the two cannot recognise each other directly. A tRNA is an **adaptor**: one end reads the codon, the other carries the amino acid.
Read more: [tRNA and the genetic code](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/trna-and-the-genetic-code)
Tags: type/why, trna, core

---

Q: What does a tRNA look like flat and folded, and why does the folded shape matter?
A: Flat, a **cloverleaf** of base-paired stems and loops. Folded, an **L**, with the **anticodon** at one end and the **amino acid on the 3' acceptor stem** at the other. The two jobs sit at **opposite ends**, far enough apart that the codon can be read and the amino acid handled independently.

![A phenylalanine tRNA drawn as a cloverleaf with its base-paired stems, as the folded L-shaped structure from x-ray diffraction, and as its linear sequence with modified bases marked](assets/img/fig-6-54.png)

Read more: [The shape of a tRNA](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/the-shape-of-a-trna)
Tags: type/figure, trna, core

Q: Three nucleotides per codon and four nucleotides to choose from gives 64 codons for 20 amino acids. Does that mean some triplets are never used?
- [ ] Yes, 44 triplets are unused
- [x] No, the code is redundant
- [ ] No, some codons specify two amino acids at once
A: **Every triplet is used.** Most amino acids are specified by **more than one codon**, and the rest are **stop codons**. The shape of the table to carry away: **AUG is both the start codon and the methionine codon**, and there are **three stops**.
Read more: [The code is redundant, not partly unused](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/the-code-is-redundant-not-partly-unused)
Tags: type/misconception, code, core

---

Q+: By what two mechanisms is that redundancy achieved?
A: **More than one tRNA exists for many amino acids**, and **some tRNAs base-pair with more than one codon**. The second is necessary because a cell has **far fewer tRNA species than sense codons**, so tRNAs must be reading several codons each.
Read more: [The code is redundant, not partly unused](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/the-code-is-redundant-not-partly-unused)
Tags: code, core

---

Q: Synonymous codons almost always differ in the third position rather than the first or second. What explains that?
A: The **wobble position**. When a tRNA pairs with a codon, the **third codon base** pairs with the first anticodon base under **relaxed, non-standard geometry**, so that position tolerates mismatches the other two do not. Redundancy is concentrated where the pairing is loosest.

![The wobble rules, listing which anticodon bases can pair with each codon base at the third position, separately for bacteria and eukaryotes](assets/img/fig-6-55.png)

Read more: [Wobble, and inosine](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/wobble-and-inosine)
Tags: type/figure, type/why, code, core

---

Q: What is inosine, where does it come from, and why does it matter for decoding?
A: A **modified nucleotide made by deamination of adenosine** in an already-made tRNA. It matters because it is **permissive**: an inosine in the **wobble position of the anticodon** can pair with **U, C or A**, so **one tRNA covers three codons**. It is a decoding device, not a structural one.
Read more: [Wobble, and inosine](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/wobble-and-inosine)
Tags: type/why, code, core

---

Q+: A common slip is to say inosine changes the structure of the tRNA. What does it actually change?
A: **Which codons that tRNA can read.** It sits in the anticodon and alters **base-pairing specificity**, broadening it. The fold of the tRNA is unaffected.
Read more: [Wobble, and inosine](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/wobble-and-inosine)
Tags: type/misconception, code

Q: The genetic code is called universal. Where does it break down, and what does that tell you?
A: In **mitochondria**, whose codes differ slightly from the standard one and differ from each other between lineages. A codon that is a **stop** in the universal code can specify an amino acid, and the reverse also happens. The code is a **frozen accident that is not completely frozen**: a genome small and isolated enough can drift away from it.

![Table 14-3, some differences between the universal code and mitochondrial genetic codes in mammals, invertebrates, yeasts and plants](assets/img/slide-5-21.png)

Q: The genetic code is said to be read by two adaptors rather than one. What are they, and what does each decide?
A: The **aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase**, which decides **which amino acid is attached to which tRNA**; and the **tRNA**, whose anticodon decides **which codon it reads**. Two independent decisions have to be right for the correct amino acid to arrive.

![An aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase charging a tRNA with tryptophan, and the charged tRNA then base-pairing with the UGG codon on mRNA](assets/img/fig-6-60.png)

Read more: [Two adaptors, not one](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/two-adaptors-not-one)
Tags: type/figure, trna, core

---

Q+: Which of those two does the ribosome check, and what follows for a mischarged tRNA?
A: Only the **second**: the ribosome verifies **codon-anticodon pairing** and takes the attached amino acid **on trust**. A tRNA carrying the wrong amino acid is therefore **undetectable** at the ribosome and its amino acid is incorporated. All the accuracy of the first step has to come from the synthetase.
Read more: [Two adaptors, not one](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/two-adaptors-not-one)
Tags: type/apply, trna, core

---

Q+: Is there a separate synthetase for each amino acid, or for each codon?
- [x] One per amino acid
- [ ] One per codon
- [ ] One per tRNA
A: **One per amino acid.** Several tRNAs can carry the same amino acid, and one synthetase serves them all, recognising them by features spread over the molecule, in the **anticodon** and in the **acceptor stem**.
Read more: [Two adaptors, not one](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/two-adaptors-not-one)
Tags: type/discriminate, trna, core

---

Q+: What does charging a tRNA cost, and what is the product?
A: **ATP, hydrolysed to AMP and two phosphates.** The amino acid ends up joined to the 3' end of the tRNA by a **high-energy bond**, and that stored energy is what pays for the peptide bond later.
Read more: [Two adaptors, not one](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/two-adaptors-not-one)
Tags: trna, core

Q: Some amino acids are chemically almost identical, so a synthetase sometimes attaches the wrong one. How does it recover?
A: By **editing**. The enzyme has **two sites**, a **synthesis site** and a separate **editing site**. If the wrong amino acid has been attached, the tRNA's 3' end is **moved into the editing site** and the amino acid is **hydrolysed off**.

![Hydrolytic editing by an aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase compared with the exonucleolytic proofreading of DNA polymerase, both showing separate synthesis and editing sites](assets/img/fig-6-61.png)

Read more: [Editing](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/editing)
Tags: type/figure, trna, core

---

Q+: Which enzyme from an earlier topic uses the same architecture, and what is the general principle?
A: **DNA polymerase**, with its proofreading exonuclease. The principle: **a second active site that undoes the work of the first**. A single binding step can only discriminate as well as the binding energies allow, so accuracy beyond that has to be bought by **checking again after the fact**.
Read more: [Editing](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/editing)
Tags: type/why, core

Q: Proteins and nucleic acids are both built by condensation, but the energy is carried differently. What is the difference?
A: In **direct-monomer activation**, used for DNA, RNA and polysaccharides, **each incoming monomer carries the high-energy bond for its own addition**. In **polymer-end activation**, used for proteins, the **high-energy bond sits at the growing end of the chain**, and each incoming monomer supplies the energy for the **next** addition.

![Polymer-end activation compared with direct-monomer activation for the condensation reactions that build biological polymers](assets/img/fig-2-44.png)

Read more: [Where the energy for the peptide bond comes from](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/where-the-energy-for-the-peptide-bond-comes-from)
Tags: type/figure, type/discriminate, core

---

Q+: Apply that to translation. What holds the activated end, and how does it move?
A: The growing chain is held as a **peptidyl-tRNA**, which is the activated species. The incoming **aminoacyl-tRNA** attacks it, the chain is transferred onto the new amino acid, and **the new tRNA now carries the activated end**. The energy was paid earlier, by the synthetase, when that tRNA was charged.
Read more: [Where the energy for the peptide bond comes from](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/where-the-energy-for-the-peptide-bond-comes-from)
Tags: type/apply, core

Q: A ribosome holds three tRNAs at once. What is each site for, and why does each span both subunits?
A: **A** holds the incoming **aminoacyl-tRNA**, **P** holds the **peptidyl-tRNA** carrying the growing chain, **E** is the **exit** site for the spent tRNA. Each spans both subunits because the **mRNA-binding site is in the small subunit** and the **peptidyl transferase centre is in the large one**, so a tRNA is read at one end and has its amino acid handled at the other.

![A bacterial ribosome with tRNAs bound in the E, P and A sites, shown from several angles and as the schematic used in later figures](assets/img/fig-6-66.png)

Read more: [The ribosome has three tRNA sites](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/the-ribosome-has-three-trna-sites)
Tags: type/figure, ribosome, core

---

Q+: Why do the two subunits exist as separate particles at all, rather than as one machine?
A: Because each has a **different job** and they have to be brought together **onto a message**, at initiation, and taken apart again at termination. Keeping them separate is what makes initiation a **controllable step**, and it is the step almost all translational regulation acts on.
Read more: [The ribosome has three tRNA sites](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/the-ribosome-has-three-trna-sites)

Q: Put one round of elongation in order, from an empty A site back to an empty A site.
A: **A charged tRNA binds the A site**, selected by codon-anticodon pairing; a **peptide bond forms**, transferring the chain from the P-site tRNA onto the A-site amino acid; the **large subunit translocates** into hybrid positions; the **small subunit translocates**, carrying the mRNA on by **exactly one codon** and **ejecting the empty tRNA from the E site**.

![The translation cycle drawn as four steps: aminoacyl-tRNA binding to the A site, peptide bond formation, large subunit translocation into hybrid states, and small subunit translocation carrying the mRNA three nucleotides](assets/img/fig-6-68.png)

Read more: [Elongation](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/elongation)
Tags: type/figure, type/sequence, translation, core

---

Q+: After the peptide bond forms but before translocation, which tRNA carries the chain?
A: The one in the **A site**. The transfer moves the chain **onto the incoming amino acid**, so the P-site tRNA is left **empty** and the A-site tRNA becomes the new peptidyl-tRNA. Translocation then slides it into the P site, which is why the chain never has to be handed backwards.
Read more: [Elongation](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/elongation)
Tags: type/apply, translation, core

Q: Translation runs at a few amino acids per second, so a typical protein takes on the order of a minute. What two consequences follow?
A: That **elongation is slow relative to almost everything else a cell does**, so regulation acts on **initiation** rather than on elongation; and that a very long protein occupies a ribosome for a very long time, which is part of why a cell runs **many ribosomes on one message** instead of waiting.
Read more: [Speed](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/speed)
Tags: type/why, translation, core

Q: An incoming tRNA arrives bound to a GTP-loaded elongation factor rather than on its own. What does that buy, and how?
A: **Accuracy**, through **two separate proofreading steps**, both of which work by **delay**. Before **GTP hydrolysis**, only a correctly paired tRNA is held long enough to trigger it, so mismatched ones dissociate first. After hydrolysis, the factor leaves **GDP-bound** and there is a **second pause** before the amino acid is committed, during which mismatched tRNAs again fall off preferentially.

![The detailed translation cycle with EF-Tu delivering aminoacyl-tRNA and hydrolysing GTP at the first proofreading step, and EF-G driving translocation](assets/img/fig-6-69.png)

Read more: [Elongation factors 1: speed and accuracy](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/elongation-factors-1-speed-and-accuracy)
Tags: type/figure, translation, core

---

Q+: State the principle both proofreading steps share, in one sentence.
A: **A wrong codon-anticodon pairing is not impossible, it is short-lived**, so the ribosome does not reject it, it simply **waits long enough that most wrong ones let go**. Accuracy is bought with time rather than with a better binding site.
Read more: [Elongation factors 1: speed and accuracy](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/elongation-factors-1-speed-and-accuracy)
Tags: type/why, translation, core

---

Q+: Why are speed and accuracy properties of the same step rather than independent ones?
A: Because the **delay that lets wrong tRNAs dissociate is also what limits the rate**. Speeding elongation up would mean waiting less, and waiting less means committing more errors. The factor sets where on that trade-off the ribosome sits.
Read more: [Elongation factors 1: speed and accuracy](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/elongation-factors-1-speed-and-accuracy)
Tags: type/why, translation, core

---

Q+: What actually checks the codon-anticodon pairing, and what does that add to our view of the ribosome?
A: The **rRNA of the small subunit**, which hydrogen-bonds to the codon-anticodon helix and verifies that its **geometry** is correct. So the RNA does not only catalyse peptide bond formation, it also **does the decoding**, which reinforces the ribosome as an RNA machine.
Read more: [Elongation factors 1: speed and accuracy](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/elongation-factors-1-speed-and-accuracy)
Tags: type/why, ribosome, core

Q: A second GTP-bound elongation factor drives translocation. What exactly does it move, and why is a factor needed when the step could happen on its own?
A: It moves the **small subunit along with the mRNA** by one codon and ejects the spent tRNA. Translocation would happen unaided, but **slowly and reversibly**. Coupling it to **irreversible GTP hydrolysis** makes it **fast and directional**, the same trick every other molecular motor uses.
Read more: [Elongation factors 2: efficiency](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/elongation-factors-2-efficiency)
Tags: type/why, translation, core

Q: Put eukaryotic initiation in order, from a free small subunit to the first peptide bond.
A: An **initiator tRNA charged with methionine** is loaded with **eIF2 and GTP** into the **P site of the small subunit**; the small subunit is brought to the **5' cap**, held by **eIF4E and eIF4G**; it **scans along the message spending ATP** until it finds the **first AUG**; **eIF2 hydrolyses GTP and the initiation factors dissociate**; the **large subunit joins**; the first **aminoacyl-tRNA enters the A site** and the **first peptide bond** forms.

![The eukaryotic initiation pathway, with Met-tRNAi loaded into the small subunit, the cap bound by eIF4E and eIF4G, scanning for the first AUG, and the large subunit joining](assets/img/fig-6-74.png)

Q+: Which site does the initiator tRNA enter, and why is that worth noticing?
A: The **P site**, which is the **only time a tRNA enters there directly**. Every other tRNA arrives at the **A site**. It has to, because the P site is where the peptidyl-tRNA belongs and the initiator is carrying what will become the first residue of the chain.

Q+: Scanning finds the first AUG, but an AUG can occur by chance. What marks the real start codon?
A: The **Kozak sequence**, the preferred context of bases around the AUG. A scanning ribosome reads the **surroundings** as well as the triplet, so a poor-context AUG can be skipped and a strong one recognised.

Q: Bacteria do not scan from the cap. How does a bacterial ribosome find a start codon instead?
A: Through a **Shine-Dalgarno sequence**, a **ribosome-binding site** just upstream of each AUG that **base-pairs directly with the small subunit's rRNA** and places the ribosome straight onto the start codon.

Q+: What does that difference allow bacteria to do that eukaryotes cannot?
A: Put **several ribosome-binding sites on one mRNA** and so **encode several proteins from one message**. A ribosome can be placed anywhere along it rather than having to enter at the 5' end. Eukaryotic scanning from the cap makes that impossible, which is why eukaryotic mRNAs are essentially one message per protein.

Q: Cap-dependent scanning is not the only way a eukaryotic ribosome can start. What is the alternative, and why would a cell or a virus want one?
A: An **internal ribosome entry site (IRES)**, a **structured region of the mRNA** that recruits **eIF4G directly**, with **no cap and no eIF4E**. It is a **bypass**: it keeps working when cap-dependent initiation is shut down, which is useful to a stressed cell and extremely useful to a virus that wants to be translated while shutting the host down.

Q: There is no tRNA that reads a stop codon. What occupies the A site instead, and what does it do there?
A: A **release factor**, which is a **protein**. It cannot carry an amino acid, so when the peptidyl transferase centre performs its usual chemistry it uses **water as the nucleophile** instead. The chain is therefore **hydrolysed off the tRNA** rather than extended, leaving a free **carboxyl terminus**, and the protein is released.

![Release factor binding an A site bearing a stop codon, water added instead of an amino acid, and the ribosome dissociating into subunits](assets/img/fig-6-76.png)

Q+: How does a protein manage to occupy a site built for a tRNA?
A: By **molecular mimicry**: the release factor has evolved a **shape close enough to a tRNA's** to fit the A site. It is the same principle as an insect shaped like a dead leaf, an unrelated object built to fit a slot it has no right to, and it is worth remembering because mimicry recurs throughout molecular biology.

![A release factor shaped like a tRNA beside a moth mimicking a dead leaf](assets/img/slide-5-49.png)

Q+: What happens to the ribosome once the protein has been released?
A: It **dissociates into its two subunits**, which return to the cytosolic pool and are **reused on other messages**. The ribosome is not dedicated to one transcript, which is what the heavy and light isotope experiment from part I established.

Q: A single mRNA usually carries many ribosomes at once. What is that called, and what holds the arrangement together?
A: A **polyribosome**. Nothing holds it together except the **mRNA itself**, closed into a loop by **eIF4E and eIF4G** at the cap and the **poly-A-binding proteins** at the tail. The message is its own scaffold.

![A series of ribosomes translating one mRNA simultaneously, and an electron micrograph of a polyribosome](assets/img/fig-6-77.png)

Q+: Why is it efficient that the same loop both proves the message is intact and organises the polyribosome?
A: Because a terminating ribosome is released **next to the start codon**, so it can **re-initiate on the same message** rather than diffusing away. One structural feature delivers the integrity check, the ribosome recycling and the packing density at once.

Q: Why are so many antibiotics aimed at the ribosome, and where do they come from?
A: Because **bacterial and eukaryotic ribosomes differ enough** that a compound can **jam one and leave the other running**, which is selective toxicity. They come from **coevolution**: organisms such as fungi have competed with bacteria for hundreds of millions of years and evolved molecules that poison them, and we borrowed those molecules.
Read more: [Antibiotics and the ribosome](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/antibiotics-and-the-ribosome)

Q+: Mapped onto the ribosome, where do the binding sites of these antibiotics fall?
A: Where the work happens: around the **decoding centre and the mRNA channel** on the small subunit, and around the **peptidyl transferase centre and the exit tunnel** on the large one. Nothing useful is achieved by binding the periphery.

Q: Inhibitors of protein synthesis split into three groups by what they act on. What are the groups, and what is each good for?
A: Those acting **only on bacteria**, which are the useful antibiotics; those acting on **bacteria and eukaryotes**, which poison the patient too and so are laboratory tools rather than drugs; and those acting on **eukaryotes but not bacteria**, which are toxins and research reagents.

Q+: An antibacterial antibiotic can still harm a human cell. Through what, and why?
A: Through the **mitochondrion**. Mitochondrial ribosomes **resemble bacterial ones**, because the organelle descends from a bacterium, so a drug selective for bacterial ribosomes can also hit the ones inside our own cells. Endosymbiosis turns up as a side-effect profile.

Q: Puromycin resembles the business end of a charged tRNA. What does the ribosome do with it, and what goes wrong?
A: The ribosome accepts it into the **A site** and **transfers the growing chain onto it**, exactly as onto a real aminoacyl-tRNA. But the chain is now joined through a bond the ribosome **cannot break again**, so it **cannot be passed on**. The peptidyl-puromycin **falls out of the ribosome** and translation ends with a **truncated protein**.

![Puromycin compared with a tyrosyl-tRNA, showing the amide bond in place of the ester bond, and its effect on translation elongation](assets/img/slide-5-57.png)

Read more: [Puromycin](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/puromycin)
Tags: type/figure, antibiotics, core

---

Q+: Which single chemical difference between puromycin and a real aminoacyl-tRNA causes all of that?
A: The **bond to the amino acid**. In a real aminoacyl-tRNA it is an **ester**, which the peptidyl transferase centre can break to pass the chain on. In puromycin it is an **amide**, that is, a **peptide bond**, which is stable. The chain goes on and cannot come off.
Read more: [Puromycin](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/puromycin)
Tags: type/why, antibiotics, core

---

Q+: Why does puromycin act on bacteria and eukaryotes alike, when most ribosome antibiotics do not?
A: Because it **does not exploit a difference between the two ribosomes**. It abuses a reaction they **both** perform, in a site they both have. That makes it useless as a drug and very useful as a reagent, since the truncated chains it releases carry a puromycin tag.
Read more: [Puromycin](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/puromycin)
Tags: type/apply, antibiotics, core

Q: The cell checks an mRNA twice before trusting it. What do both checks have in common?
A: Both use the **first round of translation as the test**. Rather than inspecting the message directly, the cell lets a ribosome try it and reads the **outcome**: whether initiation can happen at all, and where the ribosome stops.
Read more: [Quality control](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/quality-control)
Tags: type/why, quality-control, core

Q: How does requiring a cap and a poly-A tail protect the cell, given that neither is translated?
A: Because **only a message with both intact can circularise**, and **only a circularised message initiates efficiently**. A transcript that was truncated, degraded or never finished **fails the structural requirement** and is not translated. The check costs nothing extra: it is a side effect of how initiation works.
Read more: [Cap and poly-A](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/cap-and-poly-a)

Q: Nonsense-mediated decay uses a mark left by splicing. What is the mark, when is it removed, and what rule does the cell apply?
A: The mark is an **exon junction complex**, deposited where an intron was removed and **stripped off by the first ribosome to pass over it**. The rule: on a good message the **normal stop codon lies in the last exon**, past every junction, so **all EJCs are gone by the time a ribosome terminates**. A stop reached while **EJCs remain downstream** means the stop came too early.

![A correctly spliced mRNA whose exon junction complexes are all stripped off before the stop codon, beside an aberrantly spliced one where a premature stop is reached while EJCs remain, triggering degradation](assets/img/fig-6-80.png)

Q+: What is the commonest way a premature stop codon arises, and what happens to the message?
A: **Abnormal splicing**, typically a **retained intron**, which is read in frame and almost always contains an in-frame stop. The stalled ribosome with an EJC still downstream recruits **Upf proteins**, which **trigger degradation of the whole mRNA**.
Read more: [Nonsense-mediated decay](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/nonsense-mediated-decay)
Tags: quality-control, core

Q+: The cell has no idea what the encoded protein should look like. Why is nonsense-mediated decay a clever way around that?
A: Because it **never inspects the protein**. It inspects the **message**, using a mark that records the **splicing history**, and treats a stop codon in the wrong place **relative to that mark** as evidence of an error. Destroying the message is also cheaper than making a truncated protein, which may be not merely useless but actively harmful.

Q: When does a polypeptide start to fold, and where is the folding actually completed?
A: **While it is still being synthesized.** The N-terminal part emerges from the exit tunnel and **folds into its domain** while the C-terminal part is still being made, so a multi-domain protein folds **domain by domain, N to C**. The tunnel is too narrow for much more than a helix, so real folding happens **outside** the ribosome and is **completed only after release**.
Read more: [Folding starts before synthesis finishes](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/folding-starts-before-synthesis-finishes)
Tags: folding, core

Q: Heat shock proteins are named after the conditions that revealed them. Why is that name misleading?
A: Because they are **not only stress proteins**. Heat denatures proteins, so heat-stressed cells make more of the machinery that handles unfolded chains, which is how they were found. That machinery is **working constantly** in an unstressed cell, on every protein being made.
Read more: [Chaperones](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/chaperones)

Q: HSP70 and HSP60 are both chaperones. What does each act on, and what does each do?
A: **HSP70** acts at the **ribosome exit tunnel**, on a chain **as it emerges**. It binds **exposed hydrophobic stretches**, which signal incomplete folding, and **cycles of ATP-driven binding and release** give the chain repeated chances to fold without aggregating. **HSP60** acts on a protein that is **already misfolded**, capturing it in a **barrel**, closing a **cap** over it, and letting it refold **in isolation** at the cost of ATP.

![A misfolded protein captured at the hydrophobic opening of a barrel-shaped chaperonin, enclosed by the GroES cap for folding in isolation, then released, with the crystal structure of GroEL-GroES](assets/img/fig-6-84.png)

---

Q+: State the contrast between the two in one sentence.
A: **HSP70 works on a chain as it appears and prevents trouble; HSP60 encloses a chain already in trouble and gives it a private second chance.** Both spend ATP, and neither contributes any information about the final structure.
Read more: [Chaperones](#/chapter/ch06-how-cells-read-the-genome/chaperones)
Tags: type/discriminate, folding, core

Q+: Why does enclosing a protein in a chamber help it fold, when the sequence already contains all the information?
A: Because the problem is not information, it is **competition**. In a crowded cytosol an exposed hydrophobic surface is as likely to find **another molecule** as to find its own core. Folding in isolation removes the competing partners, so the chain can only fold onto itself.

Q: The proteasome has to destroy proteins without destroying the wrong ones. How is it built to make that safe?
A: Its **protease active sites face an internal chamber** inside a **central cylinder**, so nothing can be digested by accident. Access is controlled by a **cap** that **recognises the polyubiquitin chain** and **removes the ubiquitin for recycling**, and by an **unfoldase ring** that spends ATP **pulling the substrate apart**, since a folded protein will not fit through.

Q+: Why does the proteasome need an unfoldase at all, when it already has proteases?
A: Because the active sites are **buried inside a narrow cylinder**, which is exactly what makes them safe. A **folded protein cannot reach them**, so it has to be **pulled into an extended chain** and threaded in. The safety feature and the requirement for ATP are the same design decision.

Q: How is a protein marked for destruction, and where does the specificity live?
A: Ubiquitin is attached to the **epsilon-amino group of a lysine side chain** by a **ubiquitin ligase (E3)** working with an **E2**, and further ubiquitins are added to the first to give a **polyubiquitin chain**. The specificity lives in the **E3**, which recognises the **degradation signal** on the target. There are very many E3s, which is how one destruction machine serves thousands of substrates.

Q+: Why does the proteasome read a chain of ubiquitins rather than a single one?
A: Because a **single ubiquitin is used as a signal for other things**, so it cannot mean destruction on its own. Requiring a **chain** makes the signal unambiguous, and it makes it **gradual**: the mark has to be built up, so it can be reversed before the point of no return.

Q: Protein degradation is not only for clearing up mistakes. What else is it for, and how is it controlled?
A: It is a **scheduled part of signalling and of the cell cycle**. Control acts on the **degradation signal**, not on the proteasome: the signal is **exposed or created** when the protein should go, by **phosphorylation**, by **dissociation of a partner that was masking it**, or by **cleavage creating a destabilizing N-terminus**.

Q+: Why regulate by exposing a hidden signal rather than by making a new ubiquitin ligase when one is needed?
A: Because exposing a signal is **fast**, needing no transcription or translation, and because destruction is **irreversible**. Both properties are exactly what a cell wants at a point of no return such as a cell-cycle transition, where a decision has to be made quickly and must not be undone.

Q: **Exam-shaped question.** "The ribosome achieves both high speed and high accuracy in translation. Explain how, and explain where the accuracy actually comes from."
A: A checklist for marks:

| Point | Content |
|---|---|
| Two adaptors | The synthetase chooses which amino acid goes on which tRNA; the tRNA anticodon chooses which codon is read. The ribosome checks only the second |
| Consequence | A mischarged tRNA is undetectable at the ribosome, so the synthetase carries the whole burden of the first decision |
| Synthetase accuracy | A separate editing site hydrolyses off an incorrectly attached amino acid, the same two-site architecture as DNA polymerase proofreading |
| Ribosome accuracy | rRNA of the small subunit checks the geometry of the codon-anticodon helix, so the decoding is done by RNA |
| Proofreading step 1 | An elongation factor holds the tRNA in the A site; only a correct pair lasts long enough to trigger GTP hydrolysis |
| Proofreading step 2 | After hydrolysis the factor leaves GDP-bound, and a second pause lets remaining mismatches dissociate |
| The principle | A wrong pairing is short-lived rather than impossible, so accuracy is bought by waiting |
| The trade-off | The same delay limits the rate, which is why speed and accuracy are set by one step and why elongation is slow enough that regulation acts on initiation |
