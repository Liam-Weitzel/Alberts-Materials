Q: A plasma membrane has to separate a cell from its surroundings. Why does it also have to be fluid, rather than a rigid shell?
A: Because a rigid boundary could do nothing but separate. **Fluidity is what allows deformation, vesicle budding and fusion, and the bringing together of two proteins that have to meet.** Every process at the membrane, from endocytosis to signalling, needs components to move within it.

Read more: [The plasma membrane](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/the-plasma-membrane)
Tags: type/why, membranes, core

Q: Which of these are processes the plasma membrane is directly involved in?
- [x] Cell-cell and cell-substrate adhesion
- [x] Ion conductivity
- [x] Cell signalling
- [x] Endocytosis and exocytosis
- [ ] Transcription of the genes encoding its own proteins
A: All four of the first group. The membrane is a **working surface**, not only a barrier: it adheres, conducts, signals and traffics. Transcription happens in the nucleus, and the membrane proteins reach the surface afterwards by the secretory pathway.
Read more: [The plasma membrane](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/the-plasma-membrane)

Q: What does it mean for a phospholipid to be **amphipathic**, and which part of the molecule is which?
A: It has **both a water-loving and a water-hating end**: a **polar hydrophilic head group**, built from choline on a phosphate on a glycerol, and **two nonpolar hydrocarbon tails**. Every structural property of a membrane follows from that one fact.
Read more: [Phospholipids, and why they are amphipathic](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/phospholipids-and-why-they-are-amphipathic)
Tags: membranes, core

---

Q: True or false: all lipids are amphipathic, which is why lipids form membranes.
- [ ] True
- [x] False
A: **The membrane lipids are amphipathic; lipids in general are not.** A triacylglycerol has no polar head at all, so it cannot line up against water and forms a **fat droplet** rather than a bilayer. Amphipathicity is a property of the lipids the cell chooses for membranes, not of the class.
Read more: [Phospholipids, and why they are amphipathic](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/phospholipids-and-why-they-are-amphipathic)
Tags: type/misconception, membranes, core

Q: Amphipathic molecules in water assemble into either a micelle or a bilayer. What decides which one?
A: **The shape of the molecule, set by how many tails it has.** One tail gives a cone, which packs into a **micelle**; two tails give a cylinder, which packs into a **bilayer**. Phospholipids have two, so they give bilayers.

![Cone-shaped amphiphilic molecules packing into a spherical micelle and cylinder-shaped ones packing into a bilayer, with cross sections of a micelle and a lipid bilayer in water](assets/img/fig-10-07.png)

Read more: [Why a bilayer assembles by itself](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/why-a-bilayer-assembles-by-itself)
Tags: type/why, membranes, core

Q+: Why does a bilayer close into a sealed compartment instead of staying a flat sheet?
A: Because a sheet has **edges**, and an edge exposes hydrocarbon tails to water. Curling up and sealing removes the edges, so the closed form is the energetically favourable one. That is why a membrane has no ends, and why a punctured membrane **reseals**.
Read more: [Why a bilayer assembles by itself](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/why-a-bilayer-assembles-by-itself)
Tags: type/why, membranes, core

Q: A lipid in a bilayer can move in four ways: {{lateral diffusion}} within its own monolayer, {{rotation}} about its long axis, {{flexion}} of its tails, and {{flip-flop::the rare one}} across to the other monolayer.
A: Three of the four happen constantly. The fourth is **rare**.

![A computer simulation of a phosphatidylcholine bilayer showing disordered head groups and tangled tails with water on both sides, and a diagram of lipid motions: lateral diffusion, flexion, rotation, and the rare flip-flop](assets/img/fig-10-10.png)

---

Q+: Why is flip-flop rare when lateral diffusion is constant, given that both are just a lipid moving?
A: Because of **where the head group ends up**. Moving sideways keeps the polar head in water the whole way and costs nothing. Flipping over drags that **polar head through the hydrophobic core**, which is energetically expensive, so it essentially does not happen without an enzyme.
Read more: [The bilayer is a two-dimensional fluid](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/the-bilayer-is-a-two-dimensional-fluid)
Tags: type/why, membranes, core

---

Q+: What does that combination of fast sideways motion and forbidden crossing make the bilayer?
A: A **two-dimensional fluid**: freely mobile within each monolayer, and effectively **sealed between them**. It is also what allows the two halves of a membrane to have different compositions and keep them.
Read more: [The bilayer is a two-dimensional fluid](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/the-bilayer-is-a-two-dimensional-fluid)
Tags: type/why, membranes, core

Q: What makes a glycolipid a glycolipid, structurally?
A: **Sugars attached in place of the phosphate-linked group.** The tails are unchanged, so it still sits in the bilayer the same way, but the head is now a carbohydrate and faces the outside of the cell.
Read more: [Glycolipids and sterols](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/glycolipids-and-sterols)
Tags: membranes

Q: Cholesterol has a small polar head, a rigid steroid ring structure and a short nonpolar tail. Which part explains its effect on the bilayer, and what is that effect?
A: **The rigid ring structure.** It is flat and stiff, so it sits packed against the first part of the neighbouring hydrocarbon chains and **stops them moving freely**. More cholesterol therefore makes the bilayer **more rigid**.

![The structure of cholesterol as a formula, a space-filling model and a schematic, with a small polar hydroxyl head, a rigid planar steroid ring structure and a short nonpolar hydrocarbon tail](assets/img/fig-10-04.png)

Read more: [Glycolipids and sterols](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/glycolipids-and-sterols)
Tags: type/why, membranes, core

Q: A membrane region becomes richer in glycosphingolipids. Does it become more fluid or less?
- [ ] More fluid, because glycolipids disrupt the packing of phospholipids
- [x] Less fluid, because their long saturated tails pack tightly
- [ ] Unchanged, because only cholesterol affects fluidity
A: **Less fluid.** Glycosphingolipids have **long, largely saturated tails**, and saturated chains pack against one another closely. That is exactly why they cluster with cholesterol rather than with anything else, which is what a lipid raft is.
Read more: [Glycolipids and sterols](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/glycolipids-and-sterols)
Tags: type/misconception, membranes, core

Q: Which changes to a bilayer's lipids actually raise its fluidity?
- [x] Shorter hydrocarbon tails
- [x] More *cis*-double bonds in the tails
- [ ] Longer saturated tails
- [ ] More cholesterol
A: A ***cis*-double bond puts a kink in the chain**, so neighbouring chains cannot pack neatly, and shorter tails have less surface to interact over. Both loosen the packing. Long saturated tails and cholesterol do the opposite.
Read more: [Glycolipids and sterols](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/glycolipids-and-sterols)
Tags: type/discriminate, membranes

Q: The fluid mosaic model makes two claims in one sentence. What are they?
A: That a membrane is a **fluid structure**, and that it carries a **mosaic of various proteins embedded in it**. The first claim is what the heterokaryon and FRAP experiments prove; the second is what freeze-fracture shows.
Read more: [The fluid mosaic model](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/the-fluid-mosaic-model)
Tags: membranes, core

---

Q: What is a lipid raft made of, and what does one do?
A: A cluster of **cholesterol and glycosphingolipids** within the membrane. It **moves freely as a unit**, carrying the proteins embedded in it, which brings those proteins **into proximity with one another** and excludes the rest.

![A model of a raft domain in which cholesterol, glycolipids, GPI-anchored proteins and transmembrane glycoproteins cluster together in a thicker patch of bilayer](assets/img/fig-10-13.png)

Read more: [Lipid rafts](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/lipid-rafts)
Tags: membranes, core

---

Q+: Why does clustering a few lipids together amount to a way of regulating cell signalling?
A: Because a cascade needs its components **to meet**. A raft concentrates a chosen subset of proteins into one drifting patch and keeps the others out, so lipid composition becomes a way of **organising which proteins encounter each other**, without having to build a protein scaffold. Whether rafts are stable structures or something far smaller and more fleeting has been argued over since the idea appeared: [[munro-2003-lipid-rafts]].
Read more: [Lipid rafts](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/lipid-rafts)
Tags: type/why, membranes, core

Q: A mouse cell and a human cell are fused into a single heterokaryon, and their surface proteins are labelled with two different fluorescent antibodies. What is observed over the next two hours, and what does it establish?
A: The two labels start in **separate halves** and **intermix** until the whole surface carries both: about half the doubly stained cells have mixed by 25 minutes, essentially all by 40. It establishes that **membrane proteins diffuse freely in the plane of the membrane**, with no transport machinery involved.

![Slide 9: the Frye and Edidin heterokaryon experiment, with mouse and human cells fused, fluorescence images of the two labels and of a heterokaryon, and a bar chart of mosaic against non-mosaic double-stained cells over incubation time at 37 degrees](assets/img/slide-6-09.png)

Read more: [Frye and Edidin, 1970](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/frye-and-edidin-1970)
Tags: type/figure, methods, membranes, core

---

Q+: If membrane proteins were instead pinned to a rigid scaffold beneath the membrane, what would the same experiment have looked like?
A: The two labels would have **stayed in their own halves indefinitely**, however long the cells were incubated. The fact that homogenisation happens on a timescale of tens of minutes, at 37 °C and with nothing added, is what rules that model out.
Read more: [Frye and Edidin, 1970](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/frye-and-edidin-1970)

Q+: A FRAP experiment recovers quickly but plateaus at only 60% of the pre-bleach intensity. What does that tell you about the protein?
A: That roughly **40% of it is immobilised**. The population is **not homogeneous**: one pool diffuses normally and another is anchored, probably to the cytoskeleton or to something outside the cell. A protein can be fluid and fixed at the same time, in different pools.
Read more: [Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP)](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/fluorescence-recovery-after-photobleaching-frap)

Q: A frozen membrane hit with a knife splits along a particular plane. Which plane, and why that one?
A: **The middle of the bilayer**, where the two sets of hydrocarbon tails meet. That plane is held together only by **weak hydrophobic association between tails**, with no covalent bonds and no hydrogen bonding across it, so it is the weakest surface in the whole structure.
Read more: [Freeze-fracture](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/freeze-fracture)
Tags: type/why, methods, membranes, core

---

Q+: The two exposed faces are then imaged by electron microscopy and are covered in particles. What are the particles, and what does it mean that the two faces do not carry equal numbers?
A: They are the **transmembrane proteins**, each staying with one monolayer as the membrane splits. Their presence proves the proteins are genuinely **embedded in** the bilayer rather than lying on it. The unequal split shows the **two halves of a membrane are not equivalent**: the cytoplasmic half retains far more.

Q: Strip away the category names. There are only three physical ways a protein can be held at a membrane. What are they?
A: Either **part of the protein itself is hydrophobic**, or **something hydrophobic has been covalently attached to it**, or it is **holding onto another protein that is already there**. Every category in the classification is a variation on one of those three.
Read more: [How a protein can be attached to a membrane](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/how-a-protein-can-be-attached-to-a-membrane)

Q: Distinguish a transmembrane protein, a monotopic protein and a membrane-associated protein by their relationship to the bilayer.
A: A **transmembrane** protein crosses the bilayer and is exposed on **both** faces. A **monotopic** protein has an α-helix lying **within one monolayer** and does not cross. A **membrane-associated** protein **never touches the bilayer at all**: it is held there by binding another membrane protein.

Q+: In this course's classification, what does "integral" mean, and how does that differ from the textbook's use of the word?
A: In the course's sense, **integral** means embedded in the bilayer but **exposed on one face only**, as a category separate from transmembrane. The textbook uses it far more broadly, for **anything that needs a detergent to release it**, transmembrane proteins included, with **peripheral** for everything else. Answer the exam in the course's sense.

Q: A protein comes off a membrane cleanly when the salt concentration is raised, with no detergent needed. What kind of attachment did it have, and what kind did it not have?
A: A **non-covalent, electrostatic** one: a positively charged patch on its surface held to the negatively charged lipid head groups, as with cytochrome *c*. It was **not transmembrane**, because releasing a transmembrane protein means disrupting the bilayer itself with detergent.
Read more: [Lipid-associated and GPI-anchored proteins](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/lipid-associated-and-gpi-anchored-proteins)

Q: Why is the α-helix the default architecture for a protein segment crossing a bilayer?
A: Because the **peptide backbone is polar** and has nowhere to put its hydrogen bonds inside the hydrocarbon core. Rolling into a helix **satisfies every backbone hydrogen bond internally**, leaving only the side chains facing the lipid, so those side chains can all be hydrophobic.
Read more: [Transmembrane proteins](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/transmembrane-proteins)
Tags: type/why, membrane-proteins, core

---

Q+: Porins are β-barrels rather than helical bundles. What does that architecture give them that a bundle of helices does not?
A: A **water-filled channel down the middle**. Closing a β-sheet into a cylinder solves the backbone hydrogen-bonding problem the same way a helix does, and it leaves a hole. For a porin the architecture and the function are the same fact.
Read more: [Transmembrane proteins](#/chapter/ch10-membrane-structure/transmembrane-proteins)
Tags: type/why, membrane-proteins, core

Q: A protein with no hydrophobic segment of its own can still be held at a membrane. Which mechanisms allow that?
- [x] A positively charged surface patch attracted to the lipid head groups
- [x] A lipid chain covalently attached to the protein
- [x] A binding site for one particular lipid head group

Q+: A GPI anchor is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol. Which part of it actually enters the bilayer?
A: The **lipid**, not the sugars. Carbohydrate is hydrophilic and cannot enter a hydrocarbon core, which is the same reason a glycolipid still needs fatty tails. The sugars sit in the anchor's linker, outside the membrane.

Q+: Of the four attachments, which can a cell switch on and off quickly, and why?
A: The **electrostatic** and **non-specific hydrophobic** ones, because they are weak and reversible: changing the protein's surface charge by **phosphorylation** is enough to release it. A **covalently attached lipid** cannot be removed on that timescale, so it is a commitment rather than a switch.

Q: In the link from collagen outside a cell to actin inside it, running through fibronectin, an integrin dimer and adaptor proteins, which component is the transmembrane one and what does the whole arrangement achieve?
A: **The integrin dimer** is the only transmembrane protein; fibronectin and the adaptors are **membrane-associated**, held there by binding it. The arrangement gives **mechanical continuity across the membrane**, so a pull on the matrix is felt by the cytoskeleton and a pull by the cytoskeleton is felt by the matrix.

Q: Five functions were listed for membrane proteins: enzymatic activity, signal transduction, adhesion, intercellular communication and docking for cytoskeletal proteins. How many of those does an integrin perform, and what does that tell you about the list?

Q: In TGFβ/BMP signalling, what does ligand binding change about the receptors, and what kind of proteins are those receptors?
A: The receptors are **transmembrane proteins with their own catalytic activity**, kinases in their own right. Ligand binding **increases the affinity of type I and type II receptors for further type I or type II receptors**, so they **oligomerise**, and the assembled complex **phosphorylates itself**.

Q+: When does SARA arrive at the receptors, and what does it do?
A: SARA is a **membrane-associated protein already sitting beside the receptors before any ligand arrives**. It is not recruited by the phosphorylation. Its job is to **present the R-Smad** to the activated receptor complex so that the R-Smad can be phosphorylated.

Q+: What complex do the phosphorylated R-Smads then form, and where does it go?
A: **Two phosphorylated R-Smads plus one Smad4**, a trimer, which **moves into the nucleus**. Note that it is three subunits and not four: R-Smad and Smad4 are not present in equal numbers.

Q+: Once in the nucleus, does the Smad complex regulate transcription by itself?
A: No. It sits on the promoter **together with a separate partner transcription factor**. That is what allows one pathway to give **different outputs in different cell types**: the Smads are the same everywhere, the partner available is not.

Q: TGFβ/BMP signalling is a signal transduction pathway, yet it belongs in an account of membrane structure rather than being a digression away from it. Why?

The receptors are **transmembrane** proteins, SARA and Smurf1/2 are **membrane-associated** proteins controlling events near the membrane, and lipid **rafts** hold the receptors and SARA close enough for any of it to happen quickly. Q: Protein traffic in a eukaryotic cell uses four modes: protein translocation, gated transport, vesicular transport and engulfment. Which one is nuclear transport, and what physically distinguishes it from the others?
A: **Gated transport.** The nucleus and the cytosol are **topologically continuous** through the pore, so a cargo crosses **already folded**, in one piece, with its binding partners still attached.

Q+: A protein entering a mitochondrion has to be unfolded first, but a protein entering a nucleus does not. Why the difference?
A: Because mitochondrial import is **translocation across a membrane**, through a narrow protein channel that only a threaded chain fits. Nuclear import passes through an **aqueous gate in a continuous space**, wide enough for a folded protein and its partners, so nothing has to come apart.
Read more: [Where this sits among the sorting routes](#/chapter/ch12-intracellular-organization-and-protein-sorting/where-this-sits-among-the-sorting-routes)

Q: The nuclear envelope is two membranes rather than one. Name the layers a molecule passes on its way in, and say what the nuclear pore complex spans.
A: The **outer nuclear membrane**, continuous with the ER, the **perinuclear space**, and the **inner nuclear membrane**, which is lined on the nuclear side by the **nuclear lamina**. The NPC **punctures both membranes at once**, giving a single continuous channel.
Read more: [Why the nucleus needs a gate](#/chapter/ch12-intracellular-organization-and-protein-sorting/why-the-nucleus-needs-a-gate)

Q: Molecules up to about 40 kDa cross the nuclear envelope freely and larger ones need a receptor. Is there a 40 kDa hole in the pore?
- [ ] Yes, the central channel is that wide and anything larger is excluded
- [x] No, the channel is filled with a mesh that slows things down in proportion to their size
A: The channel is filled by the **FG repeats** of the nucleoporins, intrinsically disordered chains forming a **tangled, gel-like mesh**. A mesh is not a wall: the limit is **soft**, and the larger a molecule is, the slower it gets, rather than being stopped at a threshold.

Q+: If the FG mesh blocks large molecules, how does a transport receptor carrying a large cargo get through it?
A: It **binds the FG repeats**. Rather than being excluded by the mesh, the receptor interacts with it and **locally dissolves it**, opening a path and closing it again behind. Being able to bind FG repeats is what makes a protein a transport receptor. The model is testable, and was tested on a barrier rebuilt from FG domains alone: [[hlsmann-2012-permeability-reconstituted-nuclear]].

Q: The nuclear pore complex has a cytoplasmic ring and a nuclear ring, each anchoring filaments that extend into their compartment. What are those filaments for?
A: **Docking.** They are where transport factors and their cargo attach before translocation, so the pore captures its traffic from the surrounding volume rather than waiting for a direct hit on the channel mouth.

Q: Transport through the nuclear pore is described as active and energy-dependent. Where does the energy actually go, given that the pore is not a pump?
A: Not into moving anything. Cargo **diffuses through the pore in both directions**; the pore does no work. The energy maintains the **Ran-GTP gradient across the envelope**, and it is that asymmetry, not a motor, that makes net movement go one way.

Q+: Is protein import handled by one pathway and protein export by another?
A: No. **One Ran-GTP regulated route runs in both directions.** The **NLS** is the signal read on the way in and the **NES** on the way out, and the **same Ran gradient** powers both. The genuinely separate pathway is the **mRNP** one, and it carries mRNA rather than protein.

Q+: Why is a coat of proteins a better export credential than the RNA sequence itself?

Q: Walk through Ran-GTP regulated nuclear import. What recognises the cargo, and where?
A: An **importin**, a nuclear import receptor, recognises the **nuclear localisation signal (NLS)** on the cargo, **in the cytosol**. The importin-cargo complex then binds the **nucleoporins** of the NPC and is carried through into the nucleus.

![Slide 24: the five steps of Ran-GTP regulated nuclear import, with importin binding NLS cargo in the cytosol, translocation through the pore, RanGTP displacing the cargo in the nucleus, and RanGAP recycling the importin in the cytosol](assets/img/slide-6-24.png)

---

Q+: Once the complex is inside the nucleus, what releases the cargo?
A: **Ran-GTP binds the importin**, and that binding **displaces the cargo**. The cargo is set free in the nucleus, which is the only compartment where Ran-GTP is abundant.
Read more: [Nuclear import](#/chapter/ch12-intracellular-organization-and-protein-sorting/nuclear-import)

Q+: The Ran-GTP-importin complex then leaves the nucleus. What breaks it up, and where?
A: **RanGAP**, the GTPase-activating protein, **in the cytosol**. It drives hydrolysis of **Ran-GTP to Ran-GDP**, which releases the importin to pick up another cargo. Ran does not hydrolyse its own GTP at any useful rate; it needs the GAP.
Read more: [Nuclear import](#/chapter/ch12-intracellular-organization-and-protein-sorting/nuclear-import)

Q: An exportin cannot pick up its cargo on its own. What else does it need, and why does that requirement make sense?
A: It needs **Ran-GTP**, which is required for cargo binding. Since **Ran-GTP exists only in the nucleus**, an exportin can only load **inside the nucleus**, which is exactly where an exported protein has to be collected from.

![Slide 25: the five steps of Ran-GTP regulated nuclear export, with RanGTP required for exportin to bind NES cargo in the nucleus, translocation, RanGAP disassembling the complex in the cytosol, and RanGEF reloading Ran in the nucleus](assets/img/slide-6-25.png)

Q+: Once the exportin-cargo-Ran-GTP complex is out in the cytoplasm, what happens to it?
A: **RanGAP hydrolyses Ran-GTP to Ran-GDP**, the complex **falls apart** and the cargo is released. The exportin and Ran-GDP then return to the nucleus, where **RanGEF converts Ran-GDP back to Ran-GTP**.
Read more: [Nuclear export](#/chapter/ch12-intracellular-organization-and-protein-sorting/nuclear-export)

Q: Ran-GTP releases cargo from an importin but is required for an exportin to bind cargo. Why does that single inversion produce two opposite directions of traffic?
A: Because cargo is released **wherever the receptor's grip is broken**. Ran-GTP breaks the importin's grip, and Ran-GTP is in the **nucleus**, so import cargo lands inside. Ran-GTP makes the exportin's grip possible, so losing Ran-GTP breaks it, and that happens in the **cytosol**, so export cargo lands outside.
Read more: [One gradient, two directions](#/chapter/ch12-intracellular-organization-and-protein-sorting/one-gradient-two-directions)
Tags: type/discriminate, nuclear-transport, core

---

Q+: What creates and maintains the Ran-GTP gradient in the first place?
A: The **spatial separation of two enzymes**. **RanGEF is in the nucleus**, loading Ran with GTP; **RanGAP is in the cytosol**, hydrolysing it. Ran is therefore GTP-bound inside and GDP-bound outside, and the GTP that RanGEF spends is what holds the gradient against diffusion.

![Ran GAP in the cytosol and Ran GEF bound to chromatin in the nucleus, creating a Ran-GDP cytosol and a Ran-GTP nucleus, and Ran-GTP causing an import receptor to release its cargo](assets/img/fig-12-60.png)

Read more: [One gradient, two directions](#/chapter/ch12-intracellular-organization-and-protein-sorting/one-gradient-two-directions)

---

Q+: Directionality is often attributed to the pore. Where does it actually come from?
A: Neither from the pore nor from the receptor. Both are **symmetrical**: cargo diffuses through the pore either way, and the receptor works the same in both compartments. Direction comes entirely from **where the two Ran enzymes sit**.

Q: RanGAP is selectively inhibited in the cytosol of a cell. Predict what happens to nuclear import and to nuclear export.
A: **Both stall.** Import fails because importins never release Ran-GTP and so are never recycled to collect new cargo. Export fails because the export complex is only disassembled by that hydrolysis, so cargo is never released outside. One enzyme sits in both cycles, so losing it breaks the traffic in both directions.

Q: A protein carries both an NLS and an NES. Where is it found?

- [ ] In the cytoplasm, because the export signal wins
- [x] Shuttling continuously between the two
A: It is **not confused; it is moving in both directions all the time**. Its steady-state distribution is set by **which signal is currently being read**, so masking one accumulates it on the other side. That is the mechanism behind a large part of signal transduction.

Q+: How does a cell use a shuttling protein to hold a transcription factor out of the nucleus until a signal arrives?
A: By **masking the NLS**, so only the NES is read and the protein accumulates in the cytoplasm. When the signal arrives the NLS is exposed, or the NES masked, and the same protein accumulates in the nucleus. Nothing has to be synthesised, so the response is **fast**.

A: Because of **what the consensus specifies**. A classical NLS is a **cluster of particular basic residues**, which stands out. Q: Nuclear/cytoplasmic fractionation lyses the cells in a **hypotonic** buffer first and the nuclear pellet in a **hypertonic** one afterwards. Why opposite buffers?
A: The **hypotonic** step makes cells swell so the **plasma membrane bursts while the nuclei stay intact**, which is what makes the separation possible. The **hypertonic** step is needed afterwards because **nuclear proteins are bound to chromatin**, and it takes **high salt** to compete them off.

Q: **Exam-shaped question.** "A nuclear pore does no work on its cargo, and both importins and exportins can bind and release their cargo in either compartment. Explain how nuclear transport is nevertheless directional, and what the cell pays for it."
A: A checklist for marks:

| Point | Content |
|---|---|
| The pore is passive | Cargo diffuses through the NPC in both directions; the FG repeat mesh slows large molecules but pushes nothing |
| The signals | An NLS is read by importins, an NES by exportins; the same cargo can carry both and shuttle |
| The switch | Ran-GTP **releases** cargo from an importin and is **required** for an exportin to bind cargo |
| The gradient | RanGEF in the nucleus makes Ran-GTP; RanGAP in the cytosol hydrolyses it, so Ran is GTP-bound inside and GDP-bound outside |
| Import follows | The importin's grip is broken only where Ran-GTP is, so import cargo is deposited in the nucleus |
| Export follows | The exportin's grip needs Ran-GTP, so it can only load inside and only lets go where Ran-GTP is destroyed, in the cytosol |
| What is paid | GTP hydrolysis, spent on maintaining the **spatial separation of RanGEF and RanGAP** against diffusion, not on moving cargo |
| The consequence | Direction is a property of **where two enzymes sit**, not of the pore or of the receptor, which is why inhibiting RanGAP stalls traffic both ways |
