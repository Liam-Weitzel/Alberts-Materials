Q: Why does a cell that relies on glycolysis alone have to burn far more glucose than a cell with working mitochondria?
A: Glycolysis stops at **pyruvate** and captures only a small part of the energy (**2 ATP** per glucose). Full oxidation to **CO₂** in the mitochondrion, through the citric acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, captures about **30 ATP**.

Read more: [About thirty ATP instead of two](#/chapter/ch14-energy-conversion-and-metabolic-compartmentation-mitochondria-and-chloroplasts/about-thirty-atp-instead-of-two)

Q: Besides making ATP, which of these are jobs of mitochondria?
- [x] Supplying precursors for amino acids, heme and lipids
- [x] Buffering Ca²⁺
- [x] Triggering apoptosis by releasing cytochrome c
- [x] Signalling through ROS
- [ ] Running glycolysis
A: Glycolysis runs in the **cytosol**; mitochondria take over at pyruvate. The other four make the organelle a **metabolic hub** and a **signalling and cell-fate** organelle as well as an energy converter.

Q: Cells with no mtDNA (ρ⁰ cells) survive on glycolysis if given pyruvate and uridine. What does that show, and why can such a cell still not do without the organelle?
A: That **respiration is dispensable** in culture as long as glycolysis is supported. The **organelle is not**: it is still needed for jobs unrelated to ATP, such as **iron-sulfur cluster assembly**.

Q: The two mitochondrial membranes create four compartments. Name them and the job of each.
A: **Outer membrane:** permeable to small molecules through porins. **Intermembrane space:** where pumped H⁺ accumulate. **Inner membrane (cristae):** ion-impermeable, holds the electron-transport chain and ATP synthase. **Matrix:** citric acid cycle, fatty-acid oxidation, mtDNA and mitochondrial ribosomes.

![Thin-section electron micrograph of a mitochondrion labelling the matrix, the inner membrane folded into cristae, the outer membrane and the intermembrane space, with the role of each compartment listed beside it](assets/img/slide-8-07.png)

Read more: [Two membranes, four compartments](#/chapter/ch14-energy-conversion-and-metabolic-compartmentation-mitochondria-and-chloroplasts/two-membranes-four-compartments)
Tags: core

Q: Why is the inner membrane folded into cristae?
A: Folding **increases the surface area** of the inner membrane, and the inner membrane is where the electron-transport chain and ATP synthase sit. More membrane means more energy-converting machinery in the same volume.

Read more: [Two membranes, four compartments](#/chapter/ch14-energy-conversion-and-metabolic-compartmentation-mitochondria-and-chloroplasts/two-membranes-four-compartments)

Q: True or false: the inner mitochondrial membrane is permeable to ions, which is what lets protons flow back into the matrix.
- [ ] True
- [x] False
A: The inner membrane is **ion-impermeable**, and that is what lets it **hold** a proton gradient. Protons return only through specific proteins: **ATP synthase** and H⁺-coupled carriers.

Read more: [Two membranes with very different permeability](#/chapter/ch14-energy-conversion-and-metabolic-compartmentation-mitochondria-and-chloroplasts/two-membranes-with-very-different-permeability)

Q: Contrast the outer and inner mitochondrial membranes: what makes one leaky and the other tight, and what does each difference allow?
A: **Outer:** **porins** (VDAC) form large aqueous channels, so small molecules pass and the intermembrane space is chemically like the cytosol. **Inner:** rich in **cardiolipin** and densely packed with proteins, impermeable to ions, so it can hold an H⁺ gradient; traffic crosses only through selective carriers (**SLC25** family) and channels.

Read more: [Two membranes with very different permeability](#/chapter/ch14-energy-conversion-and-metabolic-compartmentation-mitochondria-and-chloroplasts/two-membranes-with-very-different-permeability)
Tags: type/discriminate, core

Q: Isolated mitochondria are placed in a hypotonic medium. What happens to each membrane, and what is released?
A: Water enters the matrix and it **swells**. The **outer membrane ruptures**, releasing the contents of the **intermembrane space**; the **inner membrane stays intact** around the matrix.

![Fractionation of mitochondria: in a medium of low osmolarity the matrix swells and ruptures the outer membrane, releasing the intermembrane-space contents, beside a comparison of the porin-rich outer membrane and the cardiolipin-rich, ion-impermeable inner membrane](assets/img/slide-8-12.png)

Q+: How are the outer membrane, the inner membrane and the matrix then separated from one another?
A: **Density-gradient centrifugation** separates the light **outer membrane** from the dense matrix still enclosed by the inner membrane. **Disrupting** that fraction and centrifuging again separates the **inner membrane** from the soluble **matrix** components.

Q+: What does the citric acid cycle do with the acetyl groups, and where do the electrons go?
A: It **oxidises them to CO₂**. The high-energy electrons are captured by **NADH and FADH₂**, which pass them to the **electron-transport chain** in the inner membrane.

Q: Most of the energy in a glucose molecule is recovered in the mitochondrion rather than the cytosol. What does the mitochondrion make from the two pyruvates, and why is that not yet ATP?
A: **8 NADH, 2 FADH₂ and 2 GTP**, against only 2 ATP and 2 NADH from glycolysis in the cytosol. The NADH and FADH₂ are **reduced carriers**: they are worth ATP only once the **respiratory chain** has spent their electrons pumping H⁺ and **ATP synthase** has spent the gradient.

Read more: [Product yields from the oxidation of sugars and fats](#/chapter/ch14-energy-conversion-and-metabolic-compartmentation-mitochondria-and-chloroplasts/product-yields-from-the-oxidation-of-sugars-and-fats)

Q+: NADH made by glycolysis in the cytosol is worth less ATP than NADH made in the matrix. Why?
A: Its electrons have to be **shuttled into the matrix**, and the usual route delivers them further down the chain, skipping complex I. It is credited with about **1.5 ATP**, like FADH₂, against about **2.5** for matrix NADH.

Read more: [Product yields from the oxidation of sugars and fats](#/chapter/ch14-energy-conversion-and-metabolic-compartmentation-mitochondria-and-chloroplasts/product-yields-from-the-oxidation-of-sugars-and-fats)

Q: Per carbon atom, a fatty acid yields more ATP than glucose. Why?
A: Fatty-acid carbons are **more reduced** than sugar carbons, so each carbon delivers more electrons, as NADH and FADH₂, to the chain. A 16-carbon palmitate gives roughly **106 ATP**, about 6.6 per carbon, against about 5 per carbon for glucose.

Q: Energy from food and from sunlight ends up in the same intermediate form. What is it, and what can it be spent on?
A: A **transmembrane electrochemical H⁺ gradient**, the common currency of chemiosmosis. It is spent on **ATP synthesis**, **active membrane transport**, and in bacteria **flagellar rotation**.

Q: Describe the two stages of chemiosmosis.
A: **Stage 1:** electrons pass from high to low energy through membrane proteins that use the released energy to **pump H⁺ across the membrane**. **Stage 2:** the H⁺ **flow back down their gradient through ATP synthase**, which converts that energy into ATP.

![Chemiosmosis in two stages: electron transport drives a pump that moves protons across a membrane, then the proton gradient is harnessed by ATP synthase to make ATP from ADP and phosphate](assets/img/slide-8-18.png)

Read more: [The two stages](#/chapter/ch14-energy-conversion-and-metabolic-compartmentation-mitochondria-and-chloroplasts/the-two-stages)

Q: True or false: in the word chemiosmosis, "osmosis" refers to electrons crossing the membrane.
- [ ] True
- [x] False
A: *Chemi-* is the **redox chemistry**; *osmosis* is the **ion (H⁺) transport** across the membrane. The electrons never cross freely: they stay on the carriers of the chain. It is the **protons** that cross.

Q: In which direction does the respiratory chain pump protons?
- [x] From the matrix, across the inner membrane, into the intermembrane space
- [ ] From the intermembrane space into the matrix
- [ ] From the inner membrane to the outer membrane
- [ ] From the cytosol, across the outer membrane, into the intermembrane space

Q: Oxidative phosphorylation couples two reactions that never touch each other. What are they, and what links them?
A: **NADH + ½O₂ + H⁺ → NAD⁺ + H₂O**, which releases a large amount of free energy, and **ADP + Pᵢ → ATP**, which requires energy. They are linked by the **H⁺ gradient across the inner membrane**.

Q: In what form does NADH carry its electrons, and what happens when it donates them?
A: As a **hydride ion** (H⁻ = **H⁺ + 2 e⁻**) on its **nicotinamide ring**. On donation the hydride leaves as **a proton plus two electrons**, the ring rearranges back to **NAD⁺**, and the **two electrons go to the electron-transport chain**.

![NADH giving up its hydride ion: the hydride leaves as a proton and two high-energy electrons, which go to the electron-transport chain, and the ring passes through an unstable isomer and rearranges to NAD⁺](assets/img/slide-8-25.png)

Q: Why do cells oxidise NADH through a chain of carriers instead of letting its electrons react with O₂ in one step?
A: One step would release all the energy **at once, as heat**, like combustion, and none could be captured. **Small steps** release manageable amounts, so much of the energy can be **harnessed**, by pumping H⁺, at each large step.

![Combustion of hydrogen and oxygen releasing all its energy as an explosive burst of heat, compared with biological oxidation in which hydrogen is split into protons and electrons and the electrons pass down a series of carriers, each step harnessing part of the energy](assets/img/fig-14-13.png)

Read more: [Why the energy is released in steps](#/chapter/ch14-energy-conversion-and-metabolic-compartmentation-mitochondria-and-chloroplasts/why-the-energy-is-released-in-steps)

Q: The proton-motive force has two components. What are they, which way does each push protons, and which contributes more?
A: A **membrane potential (ΔV)**, with the matrix **negative**, and a **pH gradient (ΔpH)**, with the matrix more **alkaline**. **Both push H⁺ into the matrix**. **ΔV is the larger part** by far.

![The proton-motive force across the inner membrane shown as a membrane potential, positive outside and negative in the matrix, plus a pH gradient of pH 7 outside and pH 7.5 in the matrix, with the total of about 180 to 190 mV split into ΔV of 160 to 170 mV and ΔpH of 20 to 30 mV](assets/img/slide-8-28.png)

Q+: A pH difference is not a voltage. How can ΔpH be added to ΔV to give one number?
A: Both are **energies per proton**, so ΔpH can be expressed in the same units. At body temperature one pH unit is worth about **61 mV**, so the half-unit difference across the inner membrane adds about **30 mV** to the membrane potential.

Q: Why can protons move through water much faster than other ions?
A: A proton exists as **hydronium (H₃O⁺)** and passes along **hydrogen-bonded chains** of water: each molecule hands a proton to the next, so the **charge is relayed** without any one ion diffusing (the **Grotthuss mechanism**). Chains of water and polar side chains do the same inside membrane proteins.

![Protons hopping from water molecule to water molecule by forming and breaking hydronium ions, and a proton wire through a protein made of acidic and hydroxyl side chains and trapped water molecules](assets/img/fig-14-28.png)

Read more: [Protons hop through water](#/chapter/ch14-energy-conversion-and-metabolic-compartmentation-mitochondria-and-chloroplasts/protons-hop-through-water)

Q: How can electron transfer by itself move protons across a membrane?
A: Adding an electron to a carrier creates a **negative intermediate that picks up H⁺ from water**; removing it creates a **positive intermediate that releases H⁺ to water**. If **uptake and release happen on opposite faces** of the membrane, every electron passed carries protons across.

![An oxidised electron carrier taking an electron, becoming a transient negative intermediate and picking up a proton from water, and a reduced carrier losing an electron, becoming a transient positive intermediate and releasing its proton to water](assets/img/slide-8-32.png)

Q: Why is ubiquinone a major source of superoxide?
A: It takes its two electrons **one at a time**, so it passes through a half-reduced form, **ubisemiquinone, a free radical**. A radical on a mobile molecule can hand its unpaired electron straight to **O₂**, outside the controlled route through complex IV.

Q: What is the difference between ΔG° and ΔG for a reaction such as ATP hydrolysis?
A: **ΔG°** is a constant, the free-energy change when every concentration is 1 M, and it sets **where equilibrium lies** ($\Delta G^\circ = -RT \ln K$). **ΔG** includes the actual concentrations and says **how far the reaction is from equilibrium**, which is its driving force:

$$\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT \ln \frac{[\text{ADP}][\text{P}_i]}{[\text{ATP}]}$$

![Derivation linking hydrolysis and synthesis rates of ATP to the equilibrium constant, and showing that delta G equals delta G standard plus RT ln of ADP times phosphate over ATP, so that delta G standard equals minus RT ln K](assets/img/fig-14-30.png)

Read more: [Free energy: why the cell keeps ATP far from equilibrium](#/chapter/ch14-energy-conversion-and-metabolic-compartmentation-mitochondria-and-chloroplasts/free-energy-why-the-cell-keeps-atp-far-from-equilibrium)
Tags: type/discriminate, core

---

Q+: ATP hydrolysis releases considerably more free energy inside a cell than under standard conditions. Why?
A: Cells keep **[ATP]/[ADP][Pᵢ] far above equilibrium**, so the logarithmic term makes ΔG much more negative than ΔG°: about **−11 to −13 kcal/mol** in vivo against **−7.3** under standard conditions. The extra energy is in the **concentration ratio**, which mitochondria maintain.

Read more: [Free energy: why the cell keeps ATP far from equilibrium](#/chapter/ch14-energy-conversion-and-metabolic-compartmentation-mitochondria-and-chloroplasts/free-energy-why-the-cell-keeps-atp-far-from-equilibrium)

A: The **bottleneck**: only a few mtDNA copies pass into each oocyte, so the **mutant load can differ sharply** between siblings. The **threshold effect**: symptoms appear only once the **mutant fraction exceeds a tissue-specific level**, so a lower load can leave a child unaffected.

Q: A cell contains both normal and mutant mtDNA. What is this called, and why can it happen with mtDNA but not with a nuclear gene?
A: **Heteroplasmy.** A cell holds **many copies** of mtDNA, so normal and mutant copies can coexist in any proportion, whereas a nuclear gene has just two copies.

Q: Why does a mutation in a mitochondrial tRNA gene usually cause broader, multisystem disease than a mutation in a single mitochondrial protein-coding gene?
A: A mitochondrial tRNA is needed to translate **every mtDNA-encoded protein**, so one tRNA defect impairs **all** of them. A protein-coding mutation damages one subunit of one complex.

Q: Why can aminoglycoside antibiotics cause deafness in people carrying a particular variant of the mitochondrial 12S rRNA gene?
A: Mitochondrial ribosomes already **resemble bacterial ones**, and the variant makes them **more bacterial-like**, so aminoglycosides, which target bacterial ribosomes, bind them. The damage shows in the **cochlea**.

Q: A muscle biopsy shows many different deletions in the patient's mtDNA, or a severe loss of mtDNA copies. Where is the primary defect most likely to be, and why?
A: In a **nuclear gene** for **mtDNA maintenance** (such as *POLG*). Multiple deletions or depletion mean the machinery that replicates and maintains mtDNA is failing, and that machinery is **encoded in the nucleus**. A single large deletion, by contrast, is usually sporadic.

Q: Why are most mitochondrial diseases caused by nuclear genes, and how are they inherited?
A: Only a handful of mitochondrial proteins are encoded in mtDNA; **over a thousand are nuclear-encoded** and imported. So most defects are nuclear, and inherited in a **Mendelian** way: mostly **autosomal recessive**, some **dominant** (*OPA1*, some *POLG*), some **X-linked** (*TIMM8A*).

Q: Compare maternal spindle transfer and pronuclear transfer: when is each done, and what is moved?
A: **Maternal spindle transfer**, **before fertilisation**: the **spindle-chromosome complex** is moved from the mother's egg into an enucleated donor egg, which is then fertilised. **Pronuclear transfer**, **after fertilisation**: the **pronuclei** are moved from the mother's zygote into an enucleated donor zygote.

Q+: What questions about mitochondrial donation remain open?
A: **Carry-over of maternal mtDNA**, giving low-level heteroplasmy with possible **reversion** to the mutant type; the need for **long-term follow-up**; and the **inheritance of donor mtDNA by future generations**, through daughters.

Q: **Exam-shaped question.** "Explain how the energy stored in NADH is converted into ATP in the mitochondrion. Then predict what happens to O₂ consumption and ATP synthesis when (a) cyanide or (b) an uncoupler is added."
A: A checklist for marks:

| Point | Content |
|---|---|
| NADH as donor | Carries a hydride (H⁺ + 2 e⁻); passes two electrons to complex I and becomes NAD⁺ |
| Stepwise transfer | Electrons flow I → Q → III → cyt c → IV → O₂, towards higher redox potential, releasing energy in steps rather than as heat |
| Proton pumping | Complexes I, III and IV move H⁺ from the matrix to the intermembrane space (I and IV by conformational change, III by the Q cycle); complex II does not pump |
| The membrane | The inner membrane is ion-impermeable, so it holds the gradient |
| Proton-motive force | ΔV (matrix negative) + ΔpH (matrix alkaline); both push H⁺ back into the matrix |
| ATP synthase | H⁺ flow through F₀ turns the c-ring and central stalk; the stalk changes the shape of the β subunits in F₁, making ATP; the peripheral stalk holds the head still |
| Chemiosmosis | Name the principle: redox chemistry coupled to ATP synthesis through an H⁺ gradient |
| (a) Cyanide | Blocks complex IV at heme a₃-Cu_B: electron flow stops, O₂ consumption stops, no pumping, ATP synthesis stops |
| (b) Uncoupler | H⁺ return without passing ATP synthase: O₂ consumption continues (or rises), ATP synthesis stops, energy becomes heat (UCP1 in brown fat does this deliberately) |
