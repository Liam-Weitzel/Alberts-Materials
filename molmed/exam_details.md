## Structure and marking

The course is assessed by **two written papers of equal weight**. Each paper covers eight chapters, sixteen across the course, and the final grade is the mean of the two.

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| Papers | Two, equally weighted |
| Coverage | Eight chapters per paper, sixteen in total |
| Final grade | The mean of the two paper grades |
| Pass mark | Above 5.5 |
| Resits | Each paper may be resat once |

## Question format

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| Question types | Predominantly open questions, some with follow-up multiple choice |
| Questions per lecture | Exactly one |
| Weighting | Every question carries equal weight |

Because each lecture contributes exactly one equally weighted question, no lecture can be left out of revision without forfeiting a fixed and known share of the marks.

## Scope

**The examination covers the lectures, not the chapters.** The slides shown in a session are the syllabus for that session's question. Material in the chapter that was not lectured is background reading, useful for understanding but not itself examinable.

**Confirmed topic.** Oxidation and reduction, from Chapter 2, will appear on the exam.

## What is assessed

The questions are **largely conceptual**. What is assessed is understanding of the larger picture rather than recall of individual values or names, so committing figures and lists to memory is not a productive use of revision time.

**Terminology is the exception.** A concept cannot be expressed without the vocabulary that describes it, so the technical terms themselves do have to be understood. The distinction is between knowing what a term means and being able to use it, which is required, and reciting a roster or a measurement, which is not.

This is the principle the revision decks are written to. A card asks what sets the size of a quantity, what a ranking implies or what an arrangement is evidence of, rather than asking for the quantity itself.

## Notes on individual sessions

**Session 2, Protein Structure.** The concepts, and the *classes* of amino acid and what each class allows a protein to do. The twenty individual amino acids do not have to be committed to memory.
