Q: Mitochondria and chloroplasts are both energy-converting organelles. What does each one burn, and where are they found?
A: Mitochondria burn food molecules and make ATP by **oxidative phosphorylation**, and they are present in virtually all cells of animals, plants and fungi. Chloroplasts harness solar energy and make ATP by **photosynthesis**, and they occur only in plants and green algae. So a plant cell has both, and uses its mitochondria in the dark and in tissues such as roots that never see light. In electron micrographs the striking feature of both is their extensive internal membrane system, which is where the protein complexes that harvest energy sit.
Tags: organelles, core

Q: What does the term **chemiosmotic coupling** refer to, and where does the odd name come from?
![The membranes that carry out chemiosmotic energy conversion: the plasma membrane of a bacterium, the folded inner membrane of a mitochondrion, and the thylakoid membrane inside a chloroplast](assets/img/fig-14-01.png)
A: The mechanism shared by mitochondria, chloroplasts, bacteria and archaea for turning energy into ATP. The name joins the two halves of the process: "chemi" for the chemical bond-forming reactions that make ATP, and "osmotic" for the membrane transport processes that drive them. The shared mechanism is itself evidence for the common evolutionary origin of the energy-converting machinery in all of these. In each case the membrane that carries it is the one that was the bacterium's plasma membrane: the inner membrane of a mitochondrion and the thylakoid membrane inside a chloroplast.
Tags: chemiosmosis, core

Q+: Chemiosmotic coupling runs in two linked stages. What happens in each?
![Energy from sunlight or from food oxidation driving electron transfer in membrane complexes that pump protons across a membrane to build an electrochemical gradient](assets/img/fig-14-02.png)
A: In **stage 1**, high-energy electrons, from the oxidation of food, from pigments excited by sunlight, or from other sources, are passed along an electron-transport chain of protein complexes embedded in a membrane. Each transfer releases a small amount of energy, which is used to pump protons across the membrane and build up a large electrochemical gradient. In **stage 2**, the protons flow back down that gradient through **ATP synthase**, a membrane protein machine that uses the energy to make ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate. The gradient between the two stages is a temporary energy store, much like a charged battery, and it can drive other things besides ATP synthesis.
Tags: chemiosmosis, core

---

Q: ATP synthase is described as working like a turbine. What drives it, and which way do the protons go?
![An ATP synthase embedded in a membrane, with protons flowing down through it from the side where they are concentrated while ADP and phosphate are joined into ATP on the other side](assets/img/fig-14-03.png)
A: A flow of protons moving down their electrochemical gradient, from the side of the membrane where they have been pumped to the side they were pumped from. The flow through the membrane part of the machine drives the catalytic head, which sits on the side the protons flow into, to make ATP. In this way energy captured from food or sunlight in stage 1 ends up as the chemical energy of a phosphate bond in ATP. The same enzyme is found in all these membranes, which is why it is called ubiquitous.
Tags: chemiosmosis, atp-synthase

Q: What does NAD⁺ carry, and why does the mitochondrion need it?
A: NAD⁺ is a water-soluble small molecule that takes up **two electrons and one H⁺** from food molecules to become NADH. Electrons can move through proteins via tightly bound metal ions, but they also need small mobile carriers to get from one location to another, and NADH is the one that carries electrons from the breakdown of fats and carbohydrates to the inner mitochondrial membrane. There its electrons are handed from complex to complex, dropping to a lower energy state at each step, until the final complex combines them with O₂ and protons to make water. The energy released along the way drives proton pumps in three different complexes.
Tags: respiratory-chain, core

Q: Compare the net result of electron transfer in a mitochondrion with that in a chloroplast.
![Side-by-side electron-transport pathways: in the mitochondrion food-derived NADH passes electrons down a chain to oxygen, making water, while in the chloroplast light drives electrons from water to NADPH, releasing oxygen and feeding carbon fixation](assets/img/fig-14-04.png)
A: They run in opposite directions. In mitochondria, electrons derived from food are passed to O₂, and water and CO₂ are the final products. In chloroplasts, electrons are taken **from** water, releasing O₂, and are used via NADPH to make carbohydrates from CO₂. The products of one are the inputs of the other. Yet both use an electron-transfer chain to build a proton gradient, and two of their key components are closely related: the ATP synthase and a proton pump. What photosynthetic organisms have that mitochondria lack are the two **photosystems**, which use chlorophyll to capture light and power the electron transfers uphill.
Tags: photosynthesis, chemiosmosis

Q+: Why is it said that the complementary chemistry of mitochondria and chloroplasts was fundamental to a sustainable ecosystem?
A: Because between them they close two loops. Chloroplasts generate O₂ and take up CO₂, while mitochondria consume O₂ and release CO₂, so the two balance each other's gases. And plants make carbohydrate that animals eat and burn, so carbohydrate production is balanced by carbohydrate consumption. Neither process on its own could run indefinitely on a closed planet, since each would exhaust its own inputs.
Tags: photosynthesis

Q: How much of a eukaryotic cell can mitochondria occupy, and why is the usual picture of them as small bacterium-like beans misleading?
A: Up to 20% of the cytoplasmic volume. The textbook image of a short body 0.5 to 1 μm across misses how dynamic they are: mitochondria constantly move about the cell, change shape, divide and fuse. They are often associated with the cytoskeleton, which determines their distribution, and they have extensive contacts with other membranes, most notably the ER, at specialized domains thought to exchange lipids, calcium and perhaps other molecules. Those ER contact sites are also where mitochondria divide.
Tags: mitochondria

---

Q: How far can a mitochondrion travel in a neuron, and how does it get there?
A: Up to a meter or more, in the extended axons of neurons. Mitochondria are propelled along microtubule tracks from the cell body, where they are formed, to sites of high energy demand such as synapses. In other cell types they are parked at the point of demand instead of shuttling about.
Tags: mitochondria

Q+: Where do mitochondria sit in heart muscle and in a sperm?
![Mitochondria packed between the myofibrils of a cardiac muscle cell, and wrapped around the core of a sperm flagellum, shown in drawings and electron micrographs](assets/img/fig-14-05.png)
A: Right next to what consumes the ATP. In cardiac muscle they pack between the myofibrils of the contractile apparatus, and an electron micrograph of heart muscle shows a preponderance of them. In a sperm they are wrapped tightly around the portion of the flagellum that needs ATP for its beating, which a cross-section through the tail shows as a ring of mitochondria around the flagellar core. Placing the supplier beside the customer keeps diffusion distances for ATP and ADP short.
Tags: mitochondria

Q: How much more ATP does a cell get from a sugar with mitochondria than by glycolysis alone, and why was that a turning point in evolution?
A: About **15 times** more. Glycolysis converts glucose to pyruvate and releases only a small fraction of the free energy potentially available from glucose oxidation. In mitochondria the oxidation is completed: pyruvate is imported and oxidized all the way to CO₂ and H₂O. The book calls the acquisition of mitochondria a prerequisite for the evolution of complex animals, since without them animal cells would have to live on glycolysis. That energy source only became available after enough O₂ had accumulated in the atmosphere to be used as the final electron acceptor.
Tags: mitochondria, evolution

Q: Name the membranes and spaces of a mitochondrion.
![A tomographic slice and a surface rendering of a mouse heart mitochondrion alongside a schematic, labelling the outer membrane, the inner boundary membrane, cristae, crista junctions, the intermembrane space, the crista space and the matrix](assets/img/fig-14-06.png)
A: Two membranes and three spaces. The **outer membrane** surrounds everything. The **inner membrane** surrounds the **matrix**, and is divided into two regions: the **inner boundary membrane**, which runs parallel to the outer membrane, and the **cristae**, invaginations that protrude deeply into the matrix. The **intermembrane space** is the narrow gap of 20 to 30 nm between the inner boundary membrane and the outer membrane. Each crista is a membrane disc or tubule about 20 nm wide that encloses the **crista space**. Where the crista membrane joins the boundary membrane it narrows into tubes or slits called **crista junctions**.
Tags: mitochondria, core

---

Q+: In an electron tomogram, the matrix looks dark and the intermembrane and crista spaces look light. Why?
A: Because of protein content. The matrix contains most of the mitochondrial protein, at more than 500 mg/mL, and dense protein scatters electrons, so it appears dark. The intermembrane space and crista space have much lower protein content and appear light. That same contrast is what lets you trace the continuous folds of the inner membrane in a tomographic slice.
Tags: mitochondria

---

Q: The outer mitochondrial membrane is freely permeable to molecules as large as 5000 daltons. What makes it so leaky, and what follows for the intermembrane space?
A: It is studded with **porins**, a class of β-barrel membrane proteins that form aqueous pores across the membrane, the same arrangement as in a bacterial outer membrane. Because ions and small molecules pass freely, the intermembrane space has a pH and ionic composition very similar to the cytoplasm, and there is **no electrochemical gradient** across the outer membrane. The barrier that matters for energy conversion is the inner membrane.
Tags: mitochondria

Q: Mitochondrial DNA is under 1% of the total DNA in a mammalian cell. Is it always such a small fraction?
A: No, and the variation is large.

| Organism | Cell type | DNA molecules per organelle | Organelles per cell | Organelle DNA as % of total |
|---|---|---|---|---:|
| Rat | Liver | 5 to 10 | 1000 | 1 |
| Yeast | Vegetative | 2 to 50 | 1 to 50 | 15 |
| *Chlamydomonas* (chloroplast) | Vegetative | 80 | 1 | 7 |
| Maize (chloroplast) | Leaves | 0 to 300 | 20 to 40 | 0 to 15 |

The wide range for yeast mitochondria per cell comes from fusion and fission, which change how many separate organelles there are at any moment. The maize figure drops to zero because in mature leaves, once cell division stops, the chloroplast DNA is degraded and stable mRNAs persist to keep protein synthesis going. In cells where organelle DNA is a large fraction, a large fraction of all RNA and protein synthesis also happens inside the organelles.
Tags: mitochondria, organelle-genetics

Q: How can you see mitochondria in a living cell, and what do you see them doing?
![The mitochondrial reticulum of a yeast cell, a diagram of fission and fusion balancing each other, and a time-lapse series at 3-minute intervals showing fission and fusion events remodelling the network](assets/img/fig-14-07.png)
A: They are large enough for light microscopy. You can express a mitochondrial protein fused to GFP, or add a fluorescent dye that mitochondria take up specifically because of their membrane potential. Time-lapse imaging shows them constantly dividing by **fission**, **fusing**, and changing shape. In yeast they form a continuous **reticulum** just inside the plasma membrane, and frames taken three minutes apart already show the network remodelled by several fission and fusion events. The balance between the two decides whether a cell has many small spherical or wormlike mitochondria or a single highly branched net.
Tags: mitochondria

---

Q+: Why would a cell need to cut its mitochondria into pieces at all?
A: For three reasons the book gives. Small pieces can pinch off the network and reach remote regions, such as the thin extended axon and dendrites of a neuron. Fission lets mitochondria be distributed to each of two daughter cells at division. And it lets a damaged part be isolated and degraded by mitochondrial autophagy. All of this has to happen while preserving the separate compartments defined by the two membranes, which is what makes fission and fusion topologically difficult.
Tags: mitochondria

---

Q: How does the fission machinery cut a mitochondrion?
![Dynamin dimers assembling on the outer mitochondrial membrane into a spiral that constricts the tubule, then GTP hydrolysis tightening it further until the organelle divides](assets/img/fig-14-08.png)
A: With **dynamin-related GTPases**, the same family that pinches off vesicles. Dynamin exists as dimers in the cytosol and forms larger oligomers in a GTP-dependent process. At special sites of ER contact, these assemblies bind the outer membrane through adaptor proteins and form a helical spiral of GTP-dynamin that constricts the tubule. A concerted round of GTP hydrolysis then produces the mechanical force that severs **both** the outer and inner membranes in one step.
Tags: mitochondria

---

Q+: Fission needs one machine, but fusion needs two. Why the asymmetry, and what does each fusion step require?
![Outer membrane fusion driven by a GTPase complex spanning the two outer membranes, followed by inner membrane fusion driven by a dynamin-related tethering complex](assets/img/fig-14-09.png)
A: A single constriction can sever both membranes at once, but fusion has to join two pairs of membranes in sequence without mixing compartments, so each membrane needs its own machine. Outer membranes fuse first, through an outer-membrane GTPase that forms an oligomeric complex with subunits anchored in each of the two membranes; this needs GTP **and an H⁺ gradient** across the inner membrane. Inner membranes fuse next, through a dynamin-related protein that tethers the two inner membranes; this needs GTP **and an electrical potential** across the inner membrane. The dependence of fusion on the gradient is the link to quality control.
Tags: mitochondria

---

Q: What happens to a mitochondrion that loses its electrochemical gradient?
A: It becomes excessively fragmented, because fission carries on while fusion, which needs the gradient, stops. The fragments are then degraded by an organelle-specific form of autophagy called **mitophagy**. So the requirement for a gradient in fusion is not an incidental detail: it means that a failing mitochondrion is automatically cut out of the network, cannot rejoin it, and is removed.
Tags: mitochondria

---

Q+: How do Pink1 and Parkin detect a depolarized mitochondrion?
A: Through the import machinery. Mitochondrial protein import needs the electrochemical gradient, so when the gradient is lost import becomes inefficient. The protein kinase **Pink1** is normally imported into mitochondria and so kept out of the way; in a depolarized mitochondrion it is instead retained on the outer membrane. There it phosphorylates both mitochondria-associated ubiquitin and the mitophagy regulator **Parkin**, which initiates formation of an autophagosome that engulfs the damaged organelle. Parkin is named after Parkinson's disease, since mutations in its gene, and in Pink1's, are associated with the disease, which can involve an accumulation of nonfunctional mitochondria.
Tags: mitochondria, disease

---

Q: The two parts of the inner membrane are continuous, yet they have different protein compositions. What is found in each?
A: The **inner boundary membrane** contains the machinery for protein import, for inserting new membrane proteins, and for assembling the respiratory-chain complexes. The **crista membrane** contains the working machinery: the large complexes of the respiratory chain and the ATP synthase, which is also thought to support the high curvature of the cristae structurally. Specialized protein complexes at the **crista junctions** act as a diffusion barrier that keeps the two regions separate, and also seem to anchor the cristae to the outer membrane, holding the whole folded topology in place. Unlike the outer membrane, the inner membrane as a whole is a diffusion barrier to ions and small molecules, crossed only by specific transporters.
Tags: mitochondria

---

Q: Crista membranes have one of the highest protein densities of any biological membrane. What is it, and how much crista membrane do you have?
A: About **75% protein and 25% lipid** by weight. The folding into cristae greatly increases the area available for oxidative phosphorylation: in highly active cardiac muscle cells the total area of crista membrane can be up to **20 times** the area of the cell's plasma membrane. Summed over a human body, the cristae add up to roughly the area of a football field.
Tags: mitochondria

Q: Which fuels do mitochondria burn, and which are only minor ones?
![Amino acids, fatty acids, pyruvate and ketone bodies entering the mitochondrial matrix, converging on acetyl CoA, feeding the citric acid cycle, with NADH passing electrons to the respiratory chain and the resulting proton flow driving ATP synthase](assets/img/fig-14-10.png)
A: Mainly **pyruvate**, derived from glucose and other sugars, and **fatty acids**, derived from fats. Amino acids from protein breakdown and the **ketone bodies** made from fatty acids are normally minor fuels, but become very important in specific situations such as prolonged fasting. All of them are carried across the inner membrane by specialized transport proteins and converted in the matrix into **acetyl CoA**, the common entry point to the citric acid cycle.
Tags: mitochondria, citric-acid-cycle

---

Q+: In the citric acid cycle, what happens to the carbon and what happens to the energy?
A: The carbon atoms of the acetyl groups are oxidized to CO₂, which diffuses out of the mitochondrion and is released as a waste product. The more important product is the energy: a great deal of the bond energy released by the oxidation is saved as high-energy electrons carried by **NADH**. Those electrons go to the respiratory chain in the inner membrane, where chemiosmotic coupling turns their energy into phosphate-bond energy in ATP.
Tags: citric-acid-cycle

---

Q: The mitochondrial matrix has a protein concentration of more than 500 mg/mL. What is that comparable to, and why is it so high?
A: It is close to the protein concentration in a **protein crystal**. The matrix is packed because it holds a very large number of enzymes: those of the citric acid cycle and fatty acid oxidation, those needed to maintain the mitochondrion's own genetic system, since mitochondrial DNA and ribosomes are in the matrix, and those of the many biosynthetic pathways that run through mitochondria.
Tags: mitochondria

Q: Mitochondria do more than make ATP. List their other main functions.
A: Table 14-2 names:

| Function | What it involves |
|---|---|
| ATP production | Oxidative phosphorylation supplies most of a eukaryotic cell's ATP |
| Redox buffering | NAD⁺ for glycolysis is regenerated when NADH passes its electrons to O₂ |
| Biosynthetic precursors | Citric acid cycle intermediates for amino acids, nucleotides, fatty acids |
| Heme and iron-sulfur clusters | Metal-containing cofactors made in the organelle |
| Ca²⁺ buffering | Uptake of calcium, which signals in muscle contraction and elsewhere |
| Reactive oxygen species | Damaging, but also involved in signaling |
| Apoptosis | Released molecules trigger a proteolytic cascade that kills the cell |

The co-housing of so many functions in one organelle is why a mitochondrial defect can have effects that look nothing like an energy shortage.
Tags: mitochondria

Q: Cytosolic NADH made in glycolysis cannot cross the inner mitochondrial membrane. How do its electrons get to the respiratory chain?
A: By a **shuttle**. Glycolysis needs a constant supply of NAD⁺ for the step converting glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate to 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate, and turns it into NADH, so the NAD⁺ has to be regenerated by sending the electrons somewhere. The electrons are passed from NADH to smaller molecules in the cytosol that can cross the inner membrane. In the matrix, these reduce mitochondrial NAD⁺ to NADH and then return to the cytosol to be recharged. With enough oxygen, the shuttle plus the respiratory chain give the cell a nearly boundless sink for electrons. With too little, as in strenuously exercised muscle or the centre of a poorly vascularized tumour, the cell has to change its metabolic program substantially.
Tags: mitochondria, glycolysis

---

Q: Where does the reducing power for biosynthesis come from, and how does mitochondrial citrate feed fatty acid synthesis?
A: The reducing power is **NADPH**, a close relative of NADH, and most of it is made in the cytosol by the **pentose phosphate pathway**, a side branch of sugar breakdown. The carbon skeletons come almost entirely from intermediates of glycolysis or of the citric acid cycle. Citrate made in the matrix is transported down its electrochemical gradient to the cytosol and there converted back into acetyl CoA, which supplies the fatty acids and sterols needed for new membranes. Rapidly dividing cells, including cancer cells, often reshape their metabolism to boost this kind of drain on the cycle.
Tags: mitochondria, biosynthesis

---

Q+: Which other biosynthetic pathways run partly through mitochondria?
A: Several. Mitochondria make the **single-carbon units** needed for both purine and pyrimidine synthesis. Mitochondrial acetyl CoA, α-ketoglutarate and oxaloacetate are required for de novo amino acid synthesis, and the traffic runs both ways, since disturbed amino acid metabolism feeds back on the cycle intermediates and on mitochondrial energetics. Two critical steps of the **urea cycle**, which turns ammonium from nitrogen breakdown into urea, happen in liver mitochondria and the rest in the cytosol. **Heme** synthesis is likewise split between mitochondrion and cytoplasm.
Tags: mitochondria, biosynthesis

---

Q: It has been proposed that, for cells able to make enough ATP by glycolysis, the most essential mitochondrial function is not ATP production. What is it instead?
A: Making **iron-sulfur clusters**. These are needed both for electron transfer in the respiratory chain and for proteins that maintain and stabilize the nuclear genome, and both mitochondria and chloroplasts produce them. Loss of function of iron-sulfur proteins can sometimes be linked to nuclear genome instability, which is a hallmark of cancer. The argument is that a cell can survive without mitochondrial ATP if it has glucose to spare, but not without those clusters.
Tags: mitochondria, biosynthesis

Q: What is odd about **cardiolipin**, and what is it thought to do in cristae?
![Cardiolipin as two phospholipid units joined through a glycerol with four fatty acid tails, compared with phosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidylcholine, and a sketch of cone-shaped cardiolipin packing into a curved membrane](assets/img/fig-14-11.png)
A: It is a two-headed phospholipid: two phospholipid units covalently linked, with **four** fatty acid chains instead of the usual two. It is confined to mitochondrial membranes and is made there, and it interacts closely with the proteins of oxidative phosphorylation and ATP transport. Its shape, a relatively small head for a large tail region, is thought to support the high curvature of crista membranes. The most abundant mitochondrial lipids are still the conventional ones, phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine.
Tags: mitochondria, lipids

---

Q+: Which phospholipids do mitochondria make for the rest of the cell, and which come from the ER?
A: Phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylglycerol and phosphatidic acid are made in the mitochondrion. Phosphatidylinositol, phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylserine are made mainly in the ER. Since most of the cell's membranes are assembled in the ER, lipids have to be exchanged between the two organelles, and this is thought to happen at special sites of close contact between them rather than by vesicles.
Tags: mitochondria, lipids

Q: The citric acid cycle is part of aerobic metabolism, yet it never uses O₂. Where is oxygen actually consumed?
![Energy of high-energy electrons in NADH converted by electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation in the inner membrane into the energy of high-energy phosphate bonds in ATP](assets/img/fig-14-12.png)
A: Only at the very last step of oxidative metabolism, where electrons combine with O₂ at the end of the respiratory chain to form water. Nearly all the energy from the earlier stages is saved in energy-rich compounds, mostly NADH, that feed electrons into the respiratory chain. The energy released as those electrons pass from NADH to O₂ builds an electrochemical gradient that drives ADP plus phosphate to ATP, which is why the whole final series is called **oxidative phosphorylation**. The inner membrane acts as a device that changes one form of chemical-bond energy into another.
Tags: respiratory-chain, core

Q: The respiratory chain releases the same total energy as burning hydrogen in oxygen. What is the difference?
![Combustion of hydrogen and oxygen releasing all its energy as an explosive burst of heat, compared with biological oxidation in which hydrogen is split into protons and electrons and the electrons pass down a series of carriers, each step harnessing part of the energy](assets/img/fig-14-13.png)
A: Burning $\text{H}_2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}$ in a single step has a strongly negative $\Delta G$ and releases it all unproductively as heat. The respiratory chain divides the same favourable reaction into many small steps. The hydrogen atoms, which in reality come covalently bonded to NADH, are split into protons and electrons, and the electrons pass through a series of carriers. At three distinct steps much of the energy released is captured as an electrochemical gradient, and at the end the electrons and protons recombine with oxygen to give water. About **half** the energy is stored in a useful form; the rest is still released as heat.
Tags: respiratory-chain, thermodynamics

Q: What two things does pumping H⁺ out of the matrix create across the inner membrane?
![The electrochemical proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane, split into a large membrane potential component and a smaller pH gradient component, with pH 7.2 outside and 7.9 in the matrix](assets/img/fig-14-14.png)
A: A **pH gradient** ($\Delta$pH), with the matrix alkaline, close to pH 8, and the crista space more acidic. And a **voltage gradient** ($\Delta V$), a membrane potential with the matrix side negative and the crista space positive. The two push the same way, since the negative matrix attracts any positive ion into it and pushes negative ions out. Together they make up the **electrochemical proton gradient**, measured in millivolts, which exerts a **proton-motive force** driving H⁺ back into the matrix.
Tags: chemiosmosis, core

---

Q+: The proton-motive force across the inner membrane of a respiring mitochondrion is typically about {{180}} mV, inside negative, made up of a membrane potential of about {{150}} mV and a pH gradient of about {{0.5 to 0.6}} pH units.
A: The conversion is that each pH unit is equivalent to about 60 mV of membrane potential, so the pH term contributes only around 30 to 36 mV: in mitochondria the electrical component dominates. The same force drives ATP synthesis and also the uphill transport of selected molecules across the inner membrane, including the import of nuclear-encoded proteins.
Tags: chemiosmosis

---

Q+: Where does the "about 60 mV per pH unit" conversion come from?
A: From the Nernst equation. A concentration ratio across a membrane is worth $\frac{RT}{zF}\ln(\text{ratio})$ volts, and at 37°C, $RT/F$ is about 26.7 mV. For a tenfold ratio, which is one pH unit for protons with $z = 1$, that is $26.7 \times \ln 10 \approx 61.5$ mV. So a tenfold proton concentration difference pushes about as hard as a 60 mV voltage. The book states the equivalence and points to Panel 11-1 for the Nernst equation; the arithmetic is what makes the number memorable.
Tags: chemiosmosis, beyond-the-book

---

Q: The chapter claims that chemiosmotic mechanisms underlie the function of all living organisms. Who is the most surprising member of that club?
A: The anaerobes that get all their energy from electron transfers between two **inorganic** molecules. They eat no organic food and see no light, yet they still run an electron-transport chain that pumps protons and an ATP synthase that spends the gradient. Mitochondria, chloroplasts, bacteria and archaea all use closely similar machinery, which is why working out how one respiratory chain pumps protons explains the principle for all of them.
Tags: chemiosmosis, evolution

---

Q: What is a **redox pair**, and how is it analogous to a conjugate acid-base pair?
A: Two forms of a compound that interconvert by gaining or losing electrons, as NADH and NAD⁺ do: $\text{NADH} \rightleftharpoons \text{NAD}^+ + \text{H}^+ + 2e^-$. The analogy is exact. An acid such as acetic acid becomes its conjugate base, acetate, by losing a proton, and the reduced member of a redox pair becomes the oxidized member by losing electrons. Whenever one molecule is oxidized another is reduced, and whether the transfer happens spontaneously depends on the free-energy change, which depends on the relative electron affinities of the two pairs.
Tags: redox, core

---

Q+: NADH is a strong electron donor. Why does that force NAD⁺ to be a weak electron acceptor?
A: Because the two are linked by the same bond. NADH's two donatable electrons sit in a covalent bond whose breaking releases energy, which is why passing them on is favourable to so many acceptors. Forming that bond from NAD⁺, two electrons and a proton costs exactly the same energy that breaking it releases. So if handing the electrons away is strongly downhill, taking them back is equally uphill, and NAD⁺ is of necessity a poor acceptor. The strength of a donor and the weakness of its oxidized partner are one fact seen from two sides.
Tags: redox

---

Q: How is a **redox potential** measured?
![Panel 14-1: two beakers joined by a salt bridge and a wire through a voltmeter, one with an equimolar mixture of a redox pair and one with the hydrogen reference, plus the calculation of free-energy change from redox potentials and the effect of changing the ratio of NADH to NAD+](assets/img/panel-14-01.png)
A: By building an electrical circuit. One beaker holds a 1:1 mixture of the reduced and oxidized members of the pair being tested; the other holds the hydrogen reference standard ($2\text{H}^+ + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2$), whose potential is assigned zero by international agreement. A salt bridge of concentrated KCl lets ions move to balance charge, a wire gives electrons a resistance-free path, and a voltmeter reads the difference. If electrons flow from the test pair toward H⁺, the pair has a **negative** redox potential; if they flow from H₂ to the test pair, it has a **positive** one. Electrons move spontaneously from pairs of lower potential to pairs of higher potential.
Tags: redox

---

Q+: What is the difference between $E_0$ and $E'_0$, and why do biologists use the second?
A: $E_0$ is measured with every reactant at 1 M, **including H⁺**, which means pH 0. Biological reactions run near pH 7, so biologists define the standard state as equal concentrations of the reduced and oxidized forms with $[\text{H}^+] = 10^{-7}$ M, and call the result $E'_0$. For any pair whose reduction takes up protons, the two values differ substantially, so mixing them up gives wrong answers.
Tags: redox

---

Q+: How do you get a standard free-energy change from redox potentials? Work the example of one electron from NADH to ubiquinone.
A: With $\Delta G° = -n(0.096)\Delta E'_0$, where $n$ is the number of electrons, $\Delta E'_0$ is in millivolts, $\Delta G°$ comes out in kJ/mole, and $\Delta E'_0 = E'_0(\text{acceptor}) - E'_0(\text{donor})$. NADH/NAD⁺ is at −320 mV and ubiquinone at +30 mV, so $\Delta E'_0 = +30 - (-320) = +350$ mV and $\Delta G° = -1 \times 0.096 \times 350 = -34$ kJ/mole. The same sum for one electron from ubiquinone to oxygen gives −76 kJ/mole, and the whole trip from NADH to oxygen is the total, about −110 kJ/mole. The 0.096 is just the Faraday constant, 96.5 kJ per volt per mole of electrons, expressed per millivolt.
Tags: redox, thermodynamics

---

Q+: A cell holds its NADH well above its NAD⁺. Does that make NADH a better or a worse electron donor than the standard value suggests?
A: Better. The standard potential of −320 mV is for a 1:1 mixture. An excess of NADH over NAD⁺ makes the real redox potential more negative and the $\Delta G$ for passing its electrons to any acceptor more negative too, exactly as a high ratio of reactant to product drives any reaction forward. The book adds that the true potential inside a cell also depends on pH, temperature and electrostatic surroundings, which matter most when the redox pair is buried in a protein and surrounded by charged residues.

Q: Why are transition metals so useful for moving electrons in proteins?
A: Because their ions exist in several oxidation states with closely spaced redox potentials, so they accept and give up electrons readily. That same property helps them catalyse reactions. They also come with a practical bonus: unlike the colourless H, C, N and O that make up most biological molecules, transition metal ions are often brightly coloured, so proteins containing them can be studied by visible-light spectroscopy. Q: What makes ubiquinone different from every other electron carrier in the respiratory chain?

Q+: The fact that a quinone picks up protons along with electrons turns out to matter a great deal. Why?

Q: Describe the architecture of NADH dehydrogenase, Complex I.

Q+: If electron transfer and proton pumping happen in different arms of Complex I, how are they connected?
A: Mechanically, on the leading hypothesis. Reduction of the quinone at the junction of the two arms is thought to trigger conformational changes that are passed to a 6-nm-long amphipathic α helix lying along the matrix side of the membrane arm. That helix may act like the connecting rod of a steam engine, delivering a power stroke that pulls on broken transmembrane helices in the three antiporter-like subunits and shifts charged residues in their proton channels. That would move three protons, and a fourth may be moved at the interface of the two arms. Each quinone reduced and released thus pumps four protons, and Complex I generates roughly **half** the total proton-motive force.

Q+: Walk through the **Q cycle** in cytochrome c reductase.

Q+: What does the Q cycle gain, compared with simply oxidizing ubiquinol and passing both electrons to cytochrome c?
A: It doubles the number of protons moved per electron. Without it, each electron reaching cytochrome c would carry one proton across, from the matrix-side reduction to the crista-side oxidation. With it, one of every quinol's two electrons is recycled back into the quinone pool at the matrix side, picking up more matrix protons, so that for every electron that goes on to cytochrome c, **two** protons are moved into the crista space. That is the "two per electron" figure for Complex III, and it is energy that would otherwise be lost across one of the three big redox steps.

Q: Why is oxygen both so valuable as an electron acceptor and so dangerous to handle?
A: Valuable because its very high electron affinity means reducing it to water releases a large amount of free energy, far more than anaerobic metabolism can extract, which is thought to be why all large organisms respire. Dangerous because O₂ that picks up **one** electron becomes the **superoxide radical anion** ($\text{O}_2^{\bullet-}$), which is so reactive that it grabs three more electrons from whatever is nearby, damaging its surroundings. We can breathe oxygen at all only because the uptake of that first electron by a free O₂ molecule is slow and inefficient, which gives enzymes the chance to control it.

Q+: How does cytochrome c oxidase avoid releasing partly reduced oxygen?
![Four electrons from cytochrome c passing one at a time through copper atoms and heme a to a bimetallic site where heme a3 iron and a copper atom hold O2 until it has received all four, releasing two waters](assets/img/fig-14-25.png)

Q+: The chapter notes that reduced Complex II activity contributes to specific cancers, without saying which or how. What is known?

Q: What is a respiratory-chain **supercomplex**, and what does the book say it is for?

Q+: Is it established that supercomplexes channel ubiquinone directly from Complex I to Complex III?
A: No, and there is good evidence against it. Blaza, Hirst and colleagues (*PNAS*, 2014) set out predictions that should hold if ubiquinone were channelled inside supercomplexes, and if the quinone pool were split between the NADH and succinate pathways. Kinetic and spectroscopic measurements on mammalian mitochondrial membranes showed instead that the NADH and succinate pathways share a **single, universally accessible ubiquinone pool** that is neither partitioned nor channelled. The main contrary evidence, from flux control analysis, turned out to depend on the particular behaviour of one inhibitor. Supercomplexes themselves are accepted as real structures; what is disputed is the catalytic advantage. The paper ends by discussing what else supercomplexes might be good for; the candidates usually raised are stabilizing the individual complexes and packing proteins efficiently into an extremely crowded membrane. The book's hypothesis is worth holding loosely for that reason.

Q: How does a proton get across the hydrophobic interior of a membrane protein?
![Protons hopping from water molecule to water molecule by forming and breaking hydronium ions, and a proton wire through a protein made of acidic and hydroxyl side chains and trapped water molecules](assets/img/fig-14-28.png)
A: Along **proton wires**. In water, a proton moves not by travelling itself but by hopping: one water molecule passes a proton to its neighbour, which passes a different proton on, through the continual formation and dissociation of H₃O⁺. Proton-translocating proteins build the same thing into their interiors: rows of side chains that readily accept and release protons (Asp, Glu), side chains with water-like hydroxyl groups (Ser, Thr), and trapped water molecules, spaced closely enough that protons can jump from one to the next. Along such predefined paths protons move up to **40 times faster** than through bulk water.

Q+: What is the minimum mechanism for a protein to pump protons using the energy of electron transport?
![A pump protein cycling through conformations: open to the matrix with high proton affinity, picking up a proton, occluded, then open to the crista space with low affinity, releasing it, with the transitions driven one way by electron transport](assets/img/fig-14-29.png)

The cycle is made **unidirectional** because each transition is allosterically coupled to an energetically favourable step of electron transport; without that coupling the protein would move protons equally in both directions and pump nothing. This general model is thought to apply to NADH dehydrogenase and cytochrome c oxidase and to many other pumps, though how the conformations are formed and regulated in the respiratory complexes is still incompletely understood.

Q: What is the $\Delta G$ for ATP hydrolysis inside a cell, and why is it so different from $\Delta G°$?
![Derivation linking hydrolysis and synthesis rates of ATP to the equilibrium constant, and showing that delta G equals delta G standard plus RT ln of ADP times phosphate over ATP, so that delta G standard equals minus RT ln K](assets/img/fig-14-30.png)
A: Roughly **−46 to −54 kJ/mole** under normal cellular conditions, against a standard value $\Delta G°$ of only **−30.5 kJ/mole**. The difference is the concentration term: for a reaction $A \rightarrow B + C$,
$$\Delta G = \Delta G° + RT \ln \frac{[\text{ADP}][\text{P}_i]}{[\text{ATP}]}$$
and because mitochondria keep ATP very high relative to ADP and phosphate, the log term is large and negative. $\Delta G°$ is what you would get with everything at 1 M, which never happens in a cell.

Q+: What would happen to a cell if its ATP/ADP ratio collapsed to equilibrium?
A: It would die. At equilibrium $\Delta G = 0$: ADP and phosphate join to make ATP as fast as ATP is hydrolysed, and hydrolysis can drive nothing. The book's point is that it is $\Delta G$, not $\Delta G°$, that says how far a reaction is from equilibrium and whether it can drive other reactions. ATP is useful only because it is held very far from equilibrium, and at low ATP concentrations many essential coupled reactions would become unfavourable and run backward.
Tags: thermodynamics

Q+: $\Delta G°$ and $\Delta G$ tell you different things about a reaction. What does each tell you?
A: $\Delta G°$ tells you where equilibrium lies, since at equilibrium $\Delta G° = -RT\ln K$; it is a fixed property of the reacting molecules. $\Delta G$ tells you how far the reaction currently is from that equilibrium, and so how hard it is being driven. The book draws the parallel explicitly: $\Delta G$ is the "driving force" for a chemical reaction in just the way the proton-motive force is the driving force for moving protons across a membrane.

Q: Describe the overall architecture of ATP synthase.
![ATP synthase drawn and as a structure: a membrane rotor ring of c subunits attached to a central rotor stalk inside a catalytic head of alternating alpha and beta subunits, held stationary by a peripheral stator stalk anchored to the a subunit in the membrane](assets/img/fig-14-31.png)
A: A nanomachine of 23 or more protein subunits, about 600,000 daltons, built as a **rotor** and a **stator**. The **Fo** part sits in the membrane (named for "oligomycin-sensitive factor"), and the catalytic **F₁** head projects into the matrix. The rotor is a ring of **c subunits** in the membrane attached to a central **rotor stalk** (γ, linked by ε) that pokes up into the head. The head is a ring of three α and three β subunits, and is prevented from spinning by a peripheral **stator stalk** joined to it by the δ subunit and anchored in the membrane through the **a subunit**. Protons flowing through the membrane turn the rotor ring, which turns the stalk inside the stationary head. It runs in either direction, and to distinguish it from other enzymes that hydrolyse ATP it is also called an F₁Fo ATP synthase or F-type ATPase.

Q+: How does rotation of the central stalk make ATP in the head?
A: Like a **camshaft**. Each of the three β subunits has a catalytic site at an α-β interface, and at any moment the three are in different conformations depending on which face of the asymmetric stalk they are touching. As the stalk turns it pushes each site in turn through a cycle: one conformation binds ADP and phosphate with high affinity, the next squeezes them into ATP, and the next releases the ATP so the cycle can restart. Mechanical work done by the stalk is therefore converted directly into phosphate-bond energy, which is the only way the energetically unfavourable synthesis can happen.

Q+: How fast does it spin, and how much ATP does one synthase make?

Measured by the torque it can produce when hydrolysing ATP, it is 60 times more powerful than a diesel engine of the same size. Seen from the F₁ head, the rotor turns counterclockwise during synthesis and clockwise during hydrolysis. Rates in the literature vary with conditions: Walker and colleagues, who solved the bovine structure, give about 100 rotations per second, or 6000 rpm, which would mean 300 ATP per second.

Q: How does a c-ring convert a proton gradient into rotation?
![Atomic force microscopy of rotor rings from a cyanobacterium with 13 c subunits, and the x-ray structure of a 15-subunit ring from Spirulina, each subunit a hairpin of two helices with a proton-binding glutamate in the membrane](assets/img/fig-14-32.png)
A: Each c subunit is a hairpin of two membrane-spanning helices with a proton-binding **glutamate or aspartate** halfway through the bilayer. The **a subunit** of the stator forms two narrow **half-channels** at the rotor-stator interface, each spanning half the membrane: one open to the crista space, one to the matrix. A proton arriving through the first half-channel neutralizes the acidic side chain on a passing c subunit, which lets that subunit rotate away from the a subunit into the lipid. The proton rides round the ring for almost a full turn, and is then displaced by a positively charged **arginine** of the a subunit and escapes through the second half-channel into the matrix. Directional proton flow therefore turns the ring.

---

Q+: Why does the neutral glutamate matter? What would happen if the binding site stayed charged as the ring turned?
A: A charged glutamate cannot move into the hydrophobic core of the bilayer, which is where the ring sits. Only a protonated, uncharged side chain can rotate away from the a subunit and travel round through the lipid. So each c subunit can only move off once it has picked up a proton, and can only re-enter the interface to release it. The arginine's positive charge both pushes the proton off and keeps the ring from slipping backward. That is what makes the rotation depend on proton flow.

Q: The number of c subunits in the rotor ring varies between species. How, and what does it change?
A: Mammalian mitochondria have **8**, yeast mitochondria 10, bacteria and archaea 11 to 13, plant chloroplasts **14**, and some cyanobacteria 15. Since each full turn makes three ATP in the head regardless, the ring size sets how many protons each ATP costs: $8/3 \approx 2.7$ in mammals, but $14/3 \approx 4.7$ in chloroplasts and 5 with a 15-ring. The book likens the subunits to the cogs on a bicycle gear.

Q+: Which organisms want a high gear and which a low one, and what does a low gear buy?
- [x] Mitochondria use a high gear (few c subunits) because protons are scarce; chloroplasts use a low gear because light makes protons plentiful
- [ ] Mitochondria use a low gear because their proton gradient is steep
- [ ] Chloroplasts use a high gear because they need ATP faster
- [ ] Gear ratio makes no difference to the ATP/ADP ratio achievable
A: A small ring spends fewer protons per ATP, which is what you want when the proton supply is limited, as in mitochondria. A large ring spends more protons per ATP, but each proton then contributes a smaller share of the energy of ATP synthesis, so the synthase can keep pushing against a higher ATP/ADP ratio. Photosynthetic organisms have abundant light-driven protons and so can afford this, and in return they maintain a higher ratio of ATP to ADP, and therefore a more negative $\Delta G$ for ATP hydrolysis.

Q+: The book says mitochondria get about 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 per FADH₂, but never shows where the numbers come from. How do they follow from the proton counts?
A: From counting protons in and out. Per pair of electrons from NADH, Complex I pumps 4 H⁺, Complex III 4 (two per electron) and Complex IV 2, a total of **10**. From FADH₂ or succinate, which enter at ubiquinone and skip Complex I, the total is **6**. On the synthase side, the bovine ring has 8 c subunits, so each ATP costs $8/3 \approx 2.7$ protons at the rotor (Watt, Walker and colleagues, *PNAS*, 2010, who showed the c₈ ring is probably shared by all vertebrates). Getting phosphate into the matrix and swapping ATP out for ADP costs roughly one more proton's worth. That gives about 3.7 H⁺ per ATP made and exported, so $10/3.7 \approx 2.7$ ATP per NADH and $6/3.7 \approx 1.6$ per FADH₂. The rounded textbook values of 2.5 and 1.5 come from assuming 4 H⁺ per ATP, $10/4$ and $6/4$, and they agree with experimental measurements. Either way the P/O ratio is not a whole number, because nothing couples an electron pair rigidly to a fixed number of ATP molecules: the proton gradient sits in between.

Q: Why does a mitochondrion sometimes need to run its ATP synthase backward?
A: Because the proton gradient does more than make ATP. Mitochondria must keep a gradient to import essential proteins and metabolites even when they cannot or need not make ATP, for example if the respiratory chain is not working. In that case ATP synthase runs in reverse as an ATP-driven proton pump, turning ATP made elsewhere back into a gradient. Many bacteria switch direction routinely: synthesis mode during aerobic respiration, hydrolysis mode during anaerobic growth, to keep the plasma-membrane gradient that drives nutrient uptake and the flagellar motor. What decides the direction is simply the net $\Delta G$ of proton translocation plus ATP synthesis taken together.

Q+: How are the V-type ATPases related to the F-type ATP synthase?
A: They are architecturally similar rotary machines, but normally run only in the ATP-hydrolysing direction, as ATP-driven proton pumps that acidify organelles such as lysosomes and endosomes. They are, in effect, the design used permanently in reverse.

Q: How are ATP synthases arranged in crista membranes, and what does that do for the shape of the membrane?
![An electron tomogram of a small mitochondrion showing ATP synthase heads in long paired rows along the cristae ridges, and a sub-tomogram average of one ATP synthase dimer bending the membrane](assets/img/fig-14-33.png)
A: They project like lollipops on the matrix side, and cryo-electron tomography shows that they are not scattered randomly but form **long rows of dimers** along the ridges of the cristae. Each dimer is bent, and the rows induce or stabilize these regions of high membrane curvature, which would otherwise be energetically unfavourable. The mitochondrial enzyme has extra subunits, mostly near the membrane end of the stator stalk, that make it dimerize; bacterial and chloroplast ATP synthases lack them and stay monomeric.

Q+: What happens in yeast when the dimer-specific subunits of ATP synthase are mutated?
A: The ATP synthase stays **monomeric**, the mitochondria have **no cristae**, respiration **drops by half**, and the cells grow more slowly. So dimerization is not a detail of packing: the enzyme's rows are what shape the cristae, and the shape of the cristae matters for how well the organelle works.

Q: The pH difference across the inner membrane is only 0.5 to 0.6 units, yet ATP synthase in vitro needs about 2 pH units to make ATP at the rate cells require. How is the gap closed?
![A crista with ATP synthase dimers at its tip forming a proton sink, and respiratory complexes along its flat sides pumping protons that flow along the membrane surface to the tip](assets/img/fig-14-34.png)
A: By the geography of the crista. The respiratory complexes that pump protons sit in the flat membrane on either side of the ATP synthase rows, and the synthases sit at the tip. Protons pumped into the crista space are thought to diffuse very rapidly along the membrane surface toward the tip, where the synthases act as a **proton sink**. So the local gradient the synthase sees is steeper than the bulk difference between cytosol at pH 7.4 and matrix at pH 7.9. The cristae work as proton traps. Chloroplasts, where the pH gradient is much larger, have no need for this arrangement and lack it.

Q: How does the ADP/ATP carrier move ATP out and ADP in, and what drives the exchange?
![The ADP/ATP carrier exchanging ADP from the intermembrane space for ATP from the matrix, and its structure of six transmembrane helices around a central cavity occupied by a bound inhibitor](assets/img/fig-14-35.png)
A: It is an **antiporter**: six transmembrane helices form a cavity that binds either ADP or ATP. ADP binding from the outside triggers a conformational change that releases it into the matrix, and ATP then binds on the matrix side and is carried out to the intermembrane space, from where it diffuses through the outer membrane to the cytosol. The exchange is driven by the membrane potential: **ATP⁴⁻** carries one more negative charge than **ADP³⁻**, so the matrix-negative potential pushes the more negative ATP out and pulls the less negative ADP in. The carrier is one of the most abundant of the **mitochondrial carrier family**, which has more than 50 members, most of them using the proton gradient to drive transport.

Q: How does brown fat make heat instead of ATP?
A: With the **uncoupling protein**, another member of the mitochondrial carrier family, in the inner membranes of the large mitochondria of brown and beige fat cells. It lets protons flow back down their gradient **without** passing through ATP synthase, so the energy of oxidation is dissipated as heat. When heat is needed the process is switched on, and the cells oxidize their fat stores at a high rate. It protects newborn babies against the cold, and in genetically engineered mice activating it protects against obesity and diabetes.

Q: Anaerobic bacteria have no oxygen. What can they use as the final electron acceptor?
A: Strict anaerobes get energy either from glycolysis alone, by fermentation, or from an electron-transport chain that ends at a molecule other than O₂. The alternatives include nitrogen compounds (**nitrate** or nitrite), sulfur compounds (**sulfate** or sulfite), and carbon compounds (**fumarate** or carbonate). The carriers in their plasma membranes are comparable to those of mitochondrial chains, and nearly all bacteria have an ATP synthase very similar to the mitochondrial one.

Q: What do bacteria use their plasma-membrane proton gradient for, besides making ATP?
![An aerobic bacterium whose respiratory chain builds a proton gradient used to make ATP, import proline, succinate, lactose and lysine, and export sodium; and the same bacterium under anaerobic conditions using its ATP synthase in reverse to build the gradient from glycolytic ATP](assets/img/fig-14-36.png)
A: To drive the **flagellar motor**; to pump Na⁺ out through an Na⁺-H⁺ antiporter, which does the job of the Na⁺-K⁺ pump in eukaryotes; and to import most amino acids and many sugars through **symporters** that drag each nutrient in with one or more protons. Animal cells, by contrast, drive most uptake with the Na⁺ gradient made by the Na⁺-K⁺ pump. Q+: Some bacteria live in very alkaline environments. Why can they not run chemiosmosis on protons, and what do they do instead?
A: They must keep their cytoplasm at physiological pH, which means H⁺ is **more** concentrated inside than outside. Any electrochemical proton gradient they tried to build would be opposed by that concentration gradient pointing the wrong way. Some such bacteria substitute **Na⁺** for H⁺ throughout: the respiratory chain pumps Na⁺ out, the transporters and flagellar motor run on inward Na⁺ flow, and an Na⁺-driven ATP synthase makes ATP. The book draws the lesson that the principle of chemiosmosis is more fundamental than the proton-motive force it usually runs on.

Q: Where did the organic material that living cells consume come from before and after photosynthesis?
A: It has been proposed that the first organisms had access to abundant organic compounds made by geochemical processes, but those would have been used up early in life's history. Since then, the vast majority of the organic material used by living things has been made by photosynthetic organisms, plants and photosynthetic bacteria. The core machinery of photosynthesis seems to have evolved more than **3 billion years ago** in the ancestors of present-day bacteria, and it is now the main mechanism for storing solar energy on Earth.

Q: What makes cyanobacteria the most advanced photosynthetic bacteria, and why do they matter to us?
A: They have minimal nutrient requirements: they take electrons from **water** and use sunlight to convert atmospheric CO₂ into organic compounds, the process called **carbon fixation**, and in doing so they release O₂. The evolution of something like a modern cyanobacterium from more primitive photosynthetic bacteria is thought to be what eventually made possible all the aerobic life forms on Earth, including us, since it produced both the oxygen and, as the ancestor of chloroplasts, the photosynthesis of plants.

Q: Where are chloroplasts found in a leaf, and what do they look like?
![A cross-section of a leaf, a mesophyll cell full of bright green chloroplasts, an electron micrograph of a wheat leaf cell with chloroplasts in a thin rim of cytoplasm around a large vacuole, and a close-up of a chloroplast showing the envelope and stacked thylakoids](assets/img/fig-14-37.png)

In an electron micrograph of a leaf cell they sit, along with the nucleus and mitochondria, in a thin rim of cytoplasm squeezed against the wall by a huge water-filled vacuole. Q: Compare the compartments of a chloroplast with those of a mitochondrion. What is the crucial difference?
![A mitochondrion and a much larger chloroplast drawn side by side, labelling the outer and inner membranes, matrix, stroma, thylakoid membrane, DNA and ribosomes](assets/img/fig-14-38.png)
A: The envelope is the same idea: a highly permeable **outer membrane**, a much less permeable **inner membrane** with transport proteins, and a narrow intermembrane space between them. The inner membrane surrounds the **stroma**, the equivalent of the matrix, which holds metabolic enzymes, the ATP synthase heads, and the chloroplast's own DNA, ribosomes and RNAs. The difference is that the chloroplast inner membrane is not folded into cristae and has **no** electron-transport chains. Those, along with the light-capturing systems and ATP synthase, sit in a separate **thylakoid membrane** that is not connected to the inner membrane at any point, whereas cristae are continuous with the inner mitochondrial membrane at the crista junctions.

Q+: A mitochondrion also has three spaces: intermembrane space, crista space and matrix. What makes the chloroplast's third space different in kind?
A: The mitochondrion's crista space is only partly walled off: the crista membrane is continuous with the inner boundary membrane, and the crista space opens into the intermembrane space through the crista junctions. The chloroplast's **thylakoid lumen** is a genuinely separate compartment. The thylakoids are flattened disc-like sacs, stacked in many places into grana and joined by unstacked thylakoids, and the lumens of all of them connect to form a single separate space that is continuous with neither the intermembrane space nor the stroma. Like the crista membrane, the thylakoid membrane has a very high protein density and an unusual lipid composition.

Q: Split photosynthesis into its two broad sets of reactions.
![Overview of chloroplast metabolism: light reactions in the thylakoid membrane taking water, releasing oxygen, pumping protons and producing ATP and NADPH, which feed the carbon-fixation cycle in the stroma to make sugars, amino acids and fatty acids from CO2](assets/img/fig-14-39.png)
A: The **photosynthetic electron-transfer reactions**, or "light reactions", happen in two reaction centres in the thylakoid membrane. A photon knocks an electron out of chlorophyll, the electron passes down a transport chain much as in mitochondria, protons are pumped across the thylakoid membrane to drive ATP synthesis, and finally the electrons, with H⁺, reduce NADP⁺ to NADPH. The chlorophyll quickly gets its electron back from water, releasing O₂. The **carbon-fixation reactions**, or "dark reactions", happen in the stroma and use that ATP and NADPH to turn CO₂ into the three-carbon sugar glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate.

---

Q+: The carbon-fixation reactions are called "dark reactions". Do they happen in the dark?
A: Not in any useful sense. They do not need light directly, only the ATP and NADPH the light reactions make, but the two sets of reactions are linked by elaborate feedback that lets a plant make sugar only when appropriate. Several chloroplast enzymes required for carbon fixation are **inactive in the dark** and are reactivated by light-driven electron transport. So "dark" means "not directly driven by light", not "running at night".

Q: What happens to the glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate made in the stroma?
A: It is an intermediate of glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, so it can become almost anything. Exported to the cytosol of the leaf cell, it is used to make **sucrose** and many other metabolites. Sucrose is exported to the non-photosynthetic tissues of the plant, supplying both carbon skeletons and energy for growth. Some sugar is stored as starch granules inside the chloroplast, and some is converted to starch in special storage tissues, which are a major food source for animals.

Q: What does **Rubisco** do, and why does a plant need so much of it?
![Rubisco adding CO2 and water to the five-carbon ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate, passing through an unstable six-carbon intermediate that splits into two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate](assets/img/fig-14-40.png)
A: Ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase catalyses the central reaction of carbon fixation, turning inorganic carbon into organic carbon: CO₂ from the air plus the five-carbon **ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate** plus water give two molecules of the three-carbon **3-phosphoglycerate**. It is extraordinarily slow, turning over only about **3** substrate molecules per second against about 1000 for a typical enzyme, so a plant needs an enormous amount of it. Rubisco makes up as much as **50%** of chloroplast protein and is one of the most abundant proteins on Earth.

Q+: How much Rubisco is there on Earth, and how hard is it actually working?
A: Bar-On and Milo (*PNAS*, 2019) put the total at about **0.7 gigatons**, more than ten times earlier estimates. More than 90% of it is in the leaves of land plants, where it makes up about 3% of leaf mass. Dividing global carbon fixation by that amount of enzyme gives an effective time-averaged rate of about **0.03 per second** on land and 0.6 per second in the ocean: roughly 100-fold slower than Rubisco's maximum rate measured in vitro on land, and sevenfold slower in the ocean. Lower temperatures and the enzyme being idle at night explain most of the marine gap but not the terrestrial one. So the 3 per second in the book is already the flattering laboratory figure.

Q+: Making carbohydrate from CO₂ and water is energetically unfavourable, yet the Rubisco reaction itself is favourable. How can both be true?
A: Because the energy has already been put into the other substrate. **Ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate** is an energy-rich compound, and its carboxylation is downhill. It is consumed by the reaction, though, and regenerating it takes ATP and NADPH from the light reactions. So the unfavourable overall conversion is paid for in the steps that rebuild ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate and reduce the product, not at the carboxylation step itself.

Q: For every {{3}} CO₂ fixed into one glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate, the carbon-fixation cycle consumes {{9}} ATP and {{6}} NADPH.
![The Calvin-Benson-Bassham cycle: three CO2 and three ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate give six 3-phosphoglycerate, which are phosphorylated and reduced to six glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate; five are used to regenerate three ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate and one leaves the cycle](assets/img/fig-14-41.png)
A: In each turn, 3 CO₂ combine with 3 ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate (15 carbons) to give 6 molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate (18 carbons). Those are phosphorylated to 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate using 6 ATP and reduced to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate using 6 NADPH. Five of the six (15 carbons) are rearranged to regenerate 3 ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate, which costs the other 3 ATP, and one (3 carbons) leaves the cycle as the product. The pathway is called the **Calvin-Benson-Bassham cycle**, and it was one of the first metabolic pathways worked out with radioisotope tracers.

Q: What is **photorespiration**, and why is it a problem?
A: Rubisco prefers CO₂, but it can also use O₂ in its place, and at low CO₂ it adds O₂ to ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate instead. That is the first step of photorespiration, whose net effect is to use up O₂ and release CO₂ with no useful energy stored. In many plants about **one third** of the CO₂ fixed is lost again this way. It is worst in hot, dry conditions: the plant closes its **stomata**, the gas-exchange pores in the leaf, to save water, the CO₂ level inside the leaf falls sharply, and the balance tips further toward oxygenation.

Q: How does a C4 plant such as corn or sugarcane beat photorespiration?
![Leaf anatomy of C3 and C4 plants, with carbon fixation confined in C4 leaves to bundle-sheath cells around the veins, and the mesophyll cycle in which phosphoenolpyruvate fixes CO2 into four-carbon oxaloacetate and malate, which releases CO2 in the bundle-sheath cell and returns as pyruvate](assets/img/fig-14-42.png)
A: By dividing the work between two cell types and pumping CO₂. All of the plant's Rubisco and all of its Calvin cycle are in **bundle-sheath cells**, which are protected from the air. The surrounding **mesophyll cells** capture CO₂ in their cytosol, as bicarbonate, onto the activated three-carbon **phosphoenolpyruvate**, giving a four-carbon compound. That diffuses into the bundle-sheath cells and is broken down to release CO₂ and pyruvate, and the pyruvate returns to the mesophyll to be reactivated at the cost of ATP. Rubisco thus sees a high ratio of CO₂ to O₂ and rarely oxygenates.

Q+: Why are they called C4 and C3 plants, and when does paying for the pump make sense?
A: C4 plants first capture CO₂ into a **four**-carbon compound; everyone else first captures it into the **three**-carbon 3-phosphoglycerate made by Rubisco. Pumping CO₂ costs ATP, as all vectorial transport does, but in hot, dry places the cost is much smaller than what a C3 plant loses to photorespiration. And because C4 plants can photosynthesize at lower CO₂ inside the leaf, they open their stomata less and fix about **twice** as much net carbon per unit of water lost. C4 fixation has evolved independently in several plant lineages. Most plant species are C3, but C4 crops are much better at turning sunlight into biomass, which is why they are so important in agriculture.

Q: How do many algae concentrate CO₂ for Rubisco without a C4 pathway?
A: With a **pyrenoid**. CO₂ is often scarce in water, and algae package Rubisco together with a scaffold protein into a biomolecular **condensate**, the same kind of membraneless compartment met in Chapter 12. The condensate is threaded with membrane tubules containing **carbonic anhydrase**, which converts dissolved bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) into CO₂ right where Rubisco is concentrated. That favours carboxylation over photorespiration.

Q: How does a plant cell use its sugar over the day and night, and how do chloroplasts and mitochondria share the work?
![Chloroplasts making sugars from CO2 and exporting them to the cytosol, where they feed mitochondria that make ATP by oxidative phosphorylation, with oxygen and carbon dioxide passing between the two, and a light micrograph of red mitochondria clustered near green chloroplasts](assets/img/fig-14-43.png)
A: When photosynthesis is in excess, much glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate is turned into glucose and stored as **starch** granules in the stroma, and some into fat droplets. At night these are broken down to glucose and fatty acids, exported to the cytosol, and oxidized: sugar through glycolysis to pyruvate, then both pyruvate and fatty acids into the **mitochondria**, where oxidative phosphorylation makes ATP. So a plant goes through a daily cycle much like the fasting and feeding cycles of animals. The two organelles also trade gases: the O₂ from photosynthesis feeds respiration and the CO₂ from the citric acid cycle feeds carbon fixation, and in a leaf the mitochondria cluster close to the chloroplasts.

Q+: Chloroplasts make ATP in the light. Why can't they just export it to the rest of the plant cell?
A: Because the inner chloroplast membrane is **impermeable** to the ATP and NADPH made in the stroma. The mitochondrial inner membrane has the ADP/ATP carrier; the chloroplast has no equivalent route for bulk export. So photosynthetic ATP and NADPH are spent inside the chloroplast, largely on carbon fixation, and what the chloroplast exports to the cell is fixed carbon. The cell's cytosolic ATP comes from mitochondria burning that exported sugar, even in a leaf in full sun.

Q+: Why is sucrose, rather than glucose, the sugar a plant ships around?
A: The book states only that sucrose is the major form in which sugar moves between the cells of a plant, doing the job glucose does in animal blood. The glucose made by starch breakdown or gluconeogenesis in the cytosol is converted into this disaccharide before export from the leaves. What the book leaves unsaid is that sucrose is a **non-reducing** sugar: both anomeric carbons are tied up in the glycosidic bond, so unlike glucose it cannot react with proteins or be phosphorylated by the ordinary hexokinases on the way, which makes it a stable form to carry long distances.

Q: Which four protein complexes in the thylakoid membrane carry out **photophosphorylation**?

Photophosphorylation is simply the generation of ATP with sunlight.

Q: What features of chlorophyll suit it to its job?
![The structure of chlorophyll a: a magnesium atom held in a porphyrin ring with delocalized electrons shaded, attached to a long hydrophobic tail](assets/img/fig-14-44.png)
A: A long hydrophobic tail that makes it behave like a lipid and keeps it in the membrane, and a **porphyrin ring** with an extensive system of delocalized electrons in conjugated double bonds and a central **magnesium** atom. It is structurally analogous to heme with its central iron. The delocalized electrons are what absorb visible light: a photon moves one of them from a low-energy orbital to a higher one.

Q+: Red light and blue light both drive photosynthesis. What happens to the extra energy of a blue photon?
A: It is wasted as heat. A red photon, about 600 to 700 nm, raises an electron to a low-level excited state. A higher-energy blue photon, about 400 to 500 nm, raises it to a higher excited state, but that decays very quickly to the same low-level excited state by giving the difference off as molecular motion. Everything useful then happens from the low-level state, so a blue photon does no more photosynthetic work than a red one.
Tags: photosynthesis

---

Q: An excited chlorophyll can get rid of its energy in three ways. What are they, and which two does photosynthesis use?
A: 1. By releasing it as heat, or heat plus longer-wavelength light (**fluorescence**). This is what an isolated chlorophyll in solution does, and it is useless.
2. By passing the **energy but not the electron** to a neighbouring chlorophyll, by **resonance energy transfer**.
3. By passing the **excited electron** itself to a nearby acceptor, after which the positively charged chlorophyll takes an electron back from a donor.

Only the second and third are useful, and both depend on the chlorophylls being held at exactly defined distances and orientations in a protein, usually by a histidine that coordinates the magnesium from within the membrane interior.
Tags: photosynthesis

---

Q+: What makes a **special pair** different from the other chlorophylls in a photosystem?
A: Most protein-bound chlorophylls, when excited, simply pass the energy on by resonance transfer. In a few specially placed ones the gap between ground and excited state is just right for the photon to trigger a chemical reaction, and that special character comes from close interaction with a second chlorophyll in the same complex: the two together form the special pair. A photon of suitable energy **ionizes** one of them, separating it into an electron and a positively charged chlorophyll ion. The electron is passed very quickly to a quinone in the same complex, so that it cannot recombine unproductively with the chlorophyll ion.
Tags: photosynthesis

---

Q: What is the **charge-separation** step, and why is it the heart of photosynthesis?
![Sunlight exciting the special pair in a reaction center, the electron moving across the membrane to a mobile carrier and leaving a positive chlorophyll, which is then refilled, and the carrier leaving to feed a transport chain](assets/img/fig-14-45.png)
A: It is the moment light energy becomes chemical energy. The electron knocked out of the special pair is carried by a set of internal carriers to a mobile electron carrier bound at the **opposite** membrane surface. The result is a positively charged chlorophyll ion on one side and a negatively charged carrier on the other. Their physical separation keeps them from recombining for long enough that the chlorophyll ion, a very strong oxidant, can pull an electron from a low-energy donor, which in oxygenic photosynthesis is water. The reduced carrier then diffuses off as a strong electron donor to an electron-transport chain, where the slower subsequent chemistry happens.
Tags: photosynthesis, core

Q: What is the division of labour between **antenna** chlorophylls and the **reaction centre**?
![A photosystem in the thylakoid membrane: light-harvesting antenna complexes full of chlorophylls pass absorbed energy from molecule to molecule until it reaches the special pair of the reaction center](assets/img/fig-14-46.png)
A: Antenna chlorophylls, which are most of the chlorophyll in a plant cell, only absorb light and hand the energy around by resonance transfer. The energy hops randomly from one to another until it reaches the reaction centre, where it ionizes a special-pair chlorophyll. The special pair holds its electrons at a lower energy than the antenna chlorophylls do, so energy that arrives there is **trapped** and cannot hop back. Only energy moves through the antenna; electrons move only from the reaction centre onward. Together, the antenna and reaction centre make up a **photosystem**.

---

Q+: Why does a reaction centre need an antenna at all?
A: Because it would otherwise be starved of photons. On its own a reaction-centre chlorophyll would absorb only about **one quantum per second**, even in broad daylight, while effective photosynthesis needs hundreds per second. The antenna acts as a funnel, collecting light over a large area and delivering it to the few reaction centres. Additional **light-harvesting complexes** (LHCs) extend the antenna further and contain orange **carotenoids**, which absorb wavelengths chlorophyll misses and also protect the membrane by preventing the formation of harmful oxygen radicals.

Q: Why are the two photosystems numbered backward, and where in the thylakoid does each sit?
![The Z scheme drawn against redox potential: light drives electrons from water up through photosystem II, down through plastoquinone, the cytochrome b6-f complex and plastocyanin, up again through photosystem I, and down via ferredoxin to NADP+](assets/img/fig-14-47.png)
A: They were named in the order of their **discovery**, not their action: electrons are energized first in **photosystem II** and then passed to **photosystem I**. Photosystem I is confined to the unstacked stroma thylakoids, and photosystem II to the stacked grana thylakoids. The two are functionally different but structurally related, and each feeds light-energized electrons into an electron-transfer chain.

Q+: What is the **Z scheme**, and why does oxygenic photosynthesis need two photosystems?
A: The Z-shaped path of electrons from water to NADP⁺ through both photosystems in series. Photosystem II takes an electron from water and boosts it; it runs downhill through plastoquinone, the cytochrome $b_6$-$f$ complex and plastocyanin to photosystem I, pumping protons on the way; photosystem I boosts it again, and it runs down to NADP⁺. Two boosts are needed because the gap is too large for one: water holds its electrons very tightly (+820 mV) and NADP⁺ is a very poor acceptor (−320 mV), and the book states that a single quantum of visible light does not carry enough energy to do both jobs. In raw terms a 680 nm photon carries about 1.8 eV, more than the 1.14 V gap, but a charge separation fast and irreversible enough to be useful has to lose a large part of that at each step to keep the electron from falling back, so one photosystem in practice spans much less than its photon's energy. Simpler photosynthetic bacteria with a single photosystem cannot use water, and must take electrons from richer donors instead.
Tags: photosynthesis, redox

Q+: The Z scheme evolved in cyanobacteria. How did plants get it?
A: By acquiring it whole. The ability to take electrons from water, and so to make O₂, came to plants when their ancestors took up the endosymbiotic cyanobacteria that became chloroplasts. The Z scheme itself evolved in cyanobacteria so that they could use water, a universally available electron source.

Q: What lets photosystem II, and nothing else in biology, pull electrons out of water?
![Photosystem II with its P680 special pair and manganese cluster splitting water into oxygen and protons and passing electrons to plastoquinone, and the pigments and cofactors of its core: the manganese cluster, a tyrosine, chlorophylls, pheophytins, plastoquinones and an iron](assets/img/fig-14-48.png)
A: Two things together. Its special pair, **P680** (named for its 680 nm absorbance maximum), becomes the strongest oxidant in biology when ionized by light: the P680/P680⁺ redox potential is **+1270 mV**, well above water's +820 mV. And next to it sits a **manganese cluster** of four manganese atoms, one calcium and five oxygens, which catalyses $2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 4\text{ photons} \rightarrow 4\text{H}^+ + 4e^- + \text{O}_2$. Water is an inexhaustible source of electrons but an extremely stable one, which is why it takes this much oxidizing power.

Q+: Water is split in four steps. How does photosystem II do that without releasing oxygen radicals?
A: Each step takes one photon. The photon turns P680 into P680⁺, which pulls an electron from water bound at the manganese cluster through an ionized **tyrosine** side chain. Except for the electrons, which leave, all the intermediates stay firmly bound to the manganese cluster until **four** electrons have been removed from two water molecules and O₂ can be released whole. This is the same logic as cytochrome c oxidase run in reverse: there, oxygen is held until it has received four electrons; here, water is held until it has given up four. The four protons are released into the thylakoid space, adding to the gradient.

Q+: In photosystem II, where does the electron go after leaving P680?
A: Along a chain inside the complex through a chlorophyll and a **pheophytin** to the permanently bound plastoquinone **Q$_A$** and then to a second plastoquinone, **Q$_B$**. Once Q$_B$ has picked up two electrons and two protons it leaves its site and enters the lipid bilayer as mobile plastoquinol, and a fresh plastoquinone takes its place. The chlorophylls and pheophytins form two symmetrical branches, but only **one** is active, which ensures that the plastoquinone is fully reduced as fast as possible.

Q: What is in a complete photosystem II, and how long has it been this way?

Q: How do electrons get from photosystem II to photosystem I?
![Electron flow from photosystem II through plastoquinol to the cytochrome b6-f complex, which pumps protons, then via plastocyanin to photosystem I and on through ferredoxin and ferredoxin-NADP+ reductase to NADPH](assets/img/fig-14-50.png)
A: Through a chain that mirrors the mitochondrial one. Photosystem II reduces **plastoquinone** to plastoquinol, a strong donor like ubiquinol that diffuses rapidly in the thylakoid bilayer. Plastoquinol hands its electrons to the **cytochrome $b_6$-$f$ complex**, which pumps H⁺ into the thylakoid space using the same **Q cycle** as mitochondrial Complex III. The complex passes electrons one at a time to **plastocyanin**, a small copper protein that takes the place of cytochrome c, and plastocyanin diffuses along the membrane surface to photosystem I.

Q: What does photosystem I do with the electrons it receives, and what is the strongest electron donor in biology?
![The central electron-transfer chain of photosystem I from the P700 special pair through two branches of A0 chlorophylls and plastoquinones to three iron-sulfur clusters and ferredoxin](assets/img/fig-14-51.png)
A: It uses a second photon to carry out a second charge separation, receiving electrons from plastocyanin at its special pair **P700** on the thylakoid-lumen side and moving them to **ferredoxin**, a small iron-sulfur protein on the stromal side. Ferredoxin feeds **ferredoxin-NADP⁺ reductase**, which makes NADPH for carbon fixation and other biosynthesis in the stroma. Because NADP⁺/NADPH is already at −320 mV, reducing it needs something even lower: a chlorophyll near the stromal surface of photosystem I, **A$_0$**, sits at **−1000 mV**, the strongest known electron donor in biology.

Q+: Photosystems I and II do very different jobs. What suggests they share an ancestor, and how do they differ in using their two branches?
A: Their central electron-transfer chains are structurally similar, both arranged as two symmetrical branches of chlorophylls and quinones leading away from a special pair, which points to a common evolutionary origin. The difference is that in photosystem II only **one** branch carries electrons, whereas in photosystem I **both** branches are active.

Q: Where do all the protons in the thylakoid gradient come from?
![The thylakoid membrane with photosystem II, the cytochrome b6-f complex, photosystem I, ferredoxin-NADP+ reductase and ATP synthase, showing protons released by water splitting and pumped by b6-f into the thylakoid space, protons consumed in the stroma making NADPH, and ATP made in the stroma](assets/img/fig-14-52.png)
A: From three contributions. Protons are **pumped** into the thylakoid space by the cytochrome $b_6$-$f$ complex through its Q cycle. They are **released** into the thylakoid space when water is oxidized at photosystem II. And they are **consumed** on the stromal side when NADP⁺ is reduced to NADPH. The first two add protons inside and the third removes them outside, so all three steepen the gradient. The chloroplast ATP synthase, whose head faces the stroma, uses it to make ATP there, just as the mitochondrial one makes ATP in the matrix.

Q+: What is **cyclic** electron flow, and why would a chloroplast switch to it?
A: Instead of passing ferredoxin's electrons to ferredoxin-NADP⁺ reductase, the chloroplast sends them back to the $b_6$-$f$ complex to reduce plastoquinone. The electrons then cycle round through $b_6$-$f$, plastocyanin and photosystem I, which in effect turns photosystem I into a light-driven proton pump: no NADPH is made, but more protons are pumped and more ATP made. Carbon fixation needs 9 ATP to 6 NADPH, a ratio of 3 to 2, so a supply of extra ATP is useful. Elaborate regulation switches between the **linear** mode, for more NADPH, and the **circular** mode, for more ATP, according to what the cell needs.

Q: The proton-motive force across a thylakoid membrane is about the same as across the inner mitochondrial membrane. How is it composed differently?
- [ ] Both are dominated by the membrane potential
- [x] Mitochondria rely mainly on membrane potential; chloroplasts mainly on the pH gradient
- [ ] Mitochondria rely mainly on the pH gradient; chloroplasts mainly on membrane potential
- [ ] Chloroplasts have a much larger proton-motive force overall
A: In the light, protons are pumped out of the stroma, at around pH 8 like the mitochondrial matrix, into the thylakoid space, at pH 5 to 6. That is a gradient of **2 to 3 pH units**, worth about 180 mV, very close to the proton-motive force of a respiring mitochondrion. But in the mitochondrion the **membrane potential** makes the largest contribution, while in the chloroplast the **H⁺ concentration gradient** dominates. That large pH difference is also why chloroplasts need no crista-like proton traps.

Q: The book proposes that chemiosmotic ATP synthesis evolved in three stages. What are they?
![A primitive bacterium with an ATP-driven proton pump, then one with an electron-transport protein that pumps protons, then one in which protons pumped by electron transport flow back through the ATP-driven pump running in reverse to make ATP](assets/img/fig-14-53.png)
A: **Stage 1.** The first cells may have lived by fermenting geochemically made organic molecules, dumping organic acids such as lactic or formic acid that acidified their surroundings. Cells with an ATP-driven pump to throw H⁺ out would have kept their cytosol from becoming too acidic; that pump may be the ancestor of ATP synthase.
**Stage 2.** As nutrients dwindled, cells that could pump H⁺ without spending ATP gained an advantage, perhaps by evolving electron-transport proteins that pumped using redox energy from their neighbours' waste acids. Some present-day bacteria grow this way on formic acid, passing electrons to fumarate.
**Stage 3.** Some such pumps became efficient enough to make more proton gradient than pH control required. Protons then leaked back through the ATP-driven pumps, running them in reverse to make ATP, and cells that could do this needed far less of the scarce fermentable food.

Q: Why did cells need photosynthesis as a source of **reducing power**, not just of energy?
A: Because fixing carbon needs both. Once geochemical organic nutrients ran low, cells needed another carbon source, and CO₂ was abundant, but turning CO₂ into carbohydrate requires reducing it with a strong electron donor such as NADPH. Strong reducing agents are thought to have been plentiful early on but not indefinitely. Once an ancestral ATP synthase was supplying most of the ATP, cells could evolve a new way of making strong reductants, and the breakthrough was the photochemical reaction centre, which uses light to make molecules like NADPH.
Tags: evolution, photosynthesis

Q+: Green sulfur bacteria use hydrogen sulfide rather than water. Why can they get by with one photosystem?
A: Because H₂S is a far better electron donor than water: its redox potential is **−230 mV** against **+820 mV** for water. The gap from H₂S up to NADPH is small enough to span with one quantum of light absorbed by a single photosystem, through a relatively simple electron-transport chain. Present-day green sulfur bacteria use light to move hydrogen atoms, as an electron plus a proton, from H₂S to NADPH. Photochemical reaction centres are thought to have arisen first in their ancestors, and the price of this simpler design is dependence on a donor that is not available everywhere.
Tags: evolution, photosynthesis, redox

Q: What changed when cyanobacteria learned to use water as an electron source?
A: It needed two new things, a water-splitting enzyme and a second photosystem in series with the first to bridge the large gap from H₂O to NADPH. The result was, for the first time, organisms that could live on water, CO₂ and sunlight plus a few other elements, free of the need for H₂S or organic acids, so they could spread into environments closed to earlier photosynthetic bacteria. Large amounts of reduced organic material accumulated, and **oxygen entered the atmosphere** for the first time. This is thought to have happened with the development of cyanobacteria perhaps 3 billion years ago.
Tags: evolution, photosynthesis

Q+: Why was the arrival of oxygen a problem, and why did it take so long to build up?
![Atmospheric oxygen over Earth's history, near zero for billions of years after the first water-splitting photosynthesis, then rising in steps toward the present 21% after about 0.5 billion years ago, with major evolutionary events marked](assets/img/fig-14-54.png)
A: Oxygen is highly toxic, because it oxidizes biological molecules indiscriminately and irreversibly; most anaerobic bacteria are still killed rapidly by air. It built up slowly because the early oceans held huge amounts of dissolved ferrous iron (Fe²⁺), and nearly all the early O₂ was used up oxidizing it to ferric Fe³⁺, which precipitated as the **banded iron formations** found in sedimentary rocks from about 2.7 billion years ago, dating the spread of cyanobacteria. By about 2 billion years ago the Fe²⁺ was exhausted and iron deposition stopped, and only then did O₂ start to accumulate, reaching roughly current levels about 0.5 billion years ago. The slow rise gave organisms time to evolve protective mechanisms, and in time enabled aerobic respiration.

Q: Where do mitochondria and chloroplasts sit on the bacterial tree?
![The postulated evolution of bacteria: from ancestral fermenting bacteria through H2S photosynthesis in green sulfur bacteria, purple bacteria and water-splitting cyanobacteria, to oxygen respiration, with chloroplasts arising from cyanobacteria and mitochondria from alpha-proteobacteria](assets/img/fig-14-55.png)
A: Sequence analysis puts **chloroplasts** among the oxygen-evolving **cyanobacteria**, and **mitochondria** among the **α-proteobacteria**. The nearest relatives of mitochondria are three closely related groups, the rhizobacteria, agrobacteria and rickettsias, all of which form intimate associations with eukaryotic cells today. Respiration using oxygen is thought to have developed about 2 billion years ago, once O₂ was available.

Q+: How did aerobic respiration come about, and what shows that the change was not a large one?
A: By modifying components of existing electron-transport complexes into a cytochrome oxidase, so that electrons from organic or inorganic substrates could be sent to O₂ as the terminal acceptor, releasing the large energy of breaking fuels all the way down to CO₂ and water. That this was an adaptation rather than an invention is shown by some present-day **purple photosynthetic bacteria**, which switch between photosynthesis and respiration according to the availability of light and O₂, with only relatively minor reorganizations of their electron-transport chains.

Q: The chapter calls evolution "conservative". What is the clearest example in bioenergetics?
![Three electron-transport chains compared: purple nonsulfur bacteria using cyclic electron flow and reverse electron flow to make NADH, the plant Z scheme, and the mitochondrial chain, each with a quinone-accepting b-c complex that pumps protons](assets/img/fig-14-56.png)

Bacteria, chloroplasts and mitochondria all have a membrane complex resembling mitochondrial cytochrome c reductase: each accepts electrons from a quinone and pumps H⁺, their protein structures show they are evolutionarily related, and in reconstituted systems they can even **substitute for one another**. Q+: Purple nonsulfur bacteria have only one photosystem and cannot use water. How do they make NADH?
A: By running NADH dehydrogenase **backward**. They use a cyclic flow of electrons from their photosystem through a $b$-$c_1$ complex and back, which pumps protons and builds a large electrochemical gradient. That gradient then drives **reverse electron flow** through NADH dehydrogenase, forcing electrons uphill to reduce NAD⁺ to NADH. It is the same reversibility seen in ATP synthase: a chemiosmotic machine goes whichever way the net free-energy change dictates.

Q: How is organelle DNA arranged inside a cell?
![A fibroblast with blue nuclear DNA, a red mitochondrial network snaking through the cytoplasm, and green dots marking mitochondrial nucleoids distributed along it](assets/img/fig-14-57.png)

Q: When did mitochondria and chloroplasts arise, and from what partnership?
A: The mitochondrion came first. Eukaryotes are thought to have originated from a merger of an **archaeon**, which provided the nucleus, and an aerobic **α-proteobacterium**, which became a respiring, ATP-producing endosymbiont and eventually the mitochondrion. This most likely happened roughly **1.6 billion years ago**, when substantial oxygen had entered the atmosphere. The chloroplast came later, after the plant and animal lineages had split, when a descendant of that first eukaryote took up an oxygen-producing cyanobacterium by endocytosis.

Q: What features of organelle genetic systems support the endosymbiont hypothesis?
A: Several, stronger for chloroplasts than for mitochondria. Organelle genomes, like bacterial ones, are typically **circular** and share features of genome organization. Chloroplast ribosomes are very like bacterial ribosomes in structure and in their sensitivity to antibiotics such as **chloramphenicol, streptomycin, erythromycin and tetracycline**. Chloroplast protein synthesis starts with **N-formylmethionine**, as in bacteria, not methionine as in the eukaryotic cytosol. Mitochondrial genetic systems have diverged further, but their ribosomes are still sensitive to antibacterial antibiotics and their transcription machinery resembles that of bacteria and bacteriophages. The enzymes of organelle transcription, translation and replication resemble bacterial or even viral ones rather than their eukaryotic counterparts.

Q+: Who encodes the organelle's own genetic machinery?
A: Mostly the nucleus. Despite the effort of keeping independent organelle genomes, the nuclear genome encodes the vast majority of mitochondrial proteins, including in most species the enzymes that express the mitochondrial genome itself. The chloroplast genome is larger and encodes several transcription and translation enzymes, but even so more than **95%** of chloroplast proteins are typically nuclear-encoded.

Q: Why is moving a gene from an organelle to the nucleus expected to be difficult?

It must adapt to nuclear transcription, it must suit cytoplasmic translation, which for mitochondrial genes may even use a different genetic code, and its protein must gain a signal sequence to be imported into the right organelle after being made in the cytosol. Yet such transfers clearly happened on a large scale, and comparing mitochondrial genomes across species shows that some happened relatively recently.

Q+: Protein traffic between cytosol and mitochondria is almost entirely one-way. What is the important exception?

Q: The human mitochondrial genome is extraordinarily compact. How compact, and what is the cost?
A: It contains almost no noncoding DNA: nearly every nucleotide belongs to a coding sequence for a protein, an rRNA or a tRNA, and the genes run directly into one another. That leaves very little room for regulatory sequences. The little noncoding DNA there is sits mainly at the origin of replication, where it has essential roles in guiding replication and controlling transcription. The contrast with the nuclear genome, where coding sequence is a small minority, could hardly be greater.

Q+: Mitochondria translate all 64 codons with only 22 tRNAs, against 30 or more in the cytosol. How?
A: With **relaxed** codon-anticodon pairing. Many mitochondrial tRNAs recognize any of the four nucleotides in the third, wobble, position of a codon. This "2 out of 3" pairing lets one tRNA read a whole family of four codons, so fewer tRNAs are needed. Ordinary wobble pairing, by comparison, only lets a tRNA tolerate a limited set of mismatches at that position.

Q+: The genetic code's near-universality is strong evidence for common ancestry. How then can mitochondria get away with changing it?
A: Because they encode so few proteins. The mitochondrial code is not even the same across lineages: *Reclinomonas*, with the largest mitochondrial gene set, uses the standard code, UGA means tryptophan in mammalian, fungal and invertebrate mitochondria, and AGG is a stop codon in mammalian mitochondria but serine in *Drosophila*. That pattern points to **random drift** rather than a single designed change. With only a handful of proteins, the occasional reassignment of a rare codon damages few of them and can be tolerated, whereas in a genome of thousands of genes the same change would alter many proteins at once and kill the cell. This is the other side of the "frozen accident" argument for why the nuclear code does not change.

Q: What does a chloroplast genome encode?

Q+: List the ways chloroplast genomes resemble bacterial ones.
A: Their basic regulatory sequences, promoters and terminators, are virtually identical to bacterial ones. The proteins they encode are clearly recognizable as bacterial. Several clusters of functionally related genes, such as those for ribosomal proteins, are arranged the same way in chloroplasts, *E. coli* and cyanobacteria. And chloroplasts divide like bacteria, with **FtsZ**, a self-assembling tubulin-related GTPase that forms a contractile ring beneath the membrane at the middle of the dividing cell and recruits the other division proteins.

Q+: Chloroplasts and mitochondria both divide using GTPases. What is fundamentally different about how they do it?
A: The direction. Chloroplasts are divided **from the inside**, by an FtsZ ring, as bacteria are. Mitochondria are divided **from the outside**, by dynamin-related GTPases assembled on the outer membrane, a eukaryotic membrane-constriction machine borrowed from vesicle formation. So chloroplasts have stayed closer to their bacterial origins in this respect than mitochondria have.

Chloroplast transcripts have their 5′ and 3′ ends generated by processing, and polycistronic transcripts are cleaved apart. Q: How is mitochondrial DNA inherited in budding yeast, and what is **mitotic segregation**?
A: **Biparentally** at mating: two haploid cells of equal size fuse and contribute equal amounts of mitochondrial DNA to the zygote. But during subsequent vegetative growth only a small sample of mitochondrial DNA passes from mother cell into each bud, so within a few generations any given cell has mtDNA from only one parent or the other. That random sorting of organelle genomes as cells divide is mitotic segregation, and it produces **non-Mendelian**, or **cytoplasmic**, inheritance.

Q: Why is mitochondrial DNA inherited only from the mother in humans?
A: Partly by dilution and partly by active elimination. A human oocyte contains about **100,000** copies of mtDNA and a sperm only a few. On top of that, the DNA in sperm mitochondria is degraded as the sperm matures, and the sperm mitochondria that do enter the egg are recognized and destroyed by **autophagy**, through ubiquitylation and delivery to lysosomes, just as damaged mitochondria are. So inheritance of mitochondria in animals and plants is uniparental, specifically maternal.

Q+: A 2018 report described families in which mitochondrial DNA seemed to have been inherited from the father. Has maternal-only inheritance in humans been overturned?
A: No. Wei, Chinnery and colleagues (*Nature Communications*, 2020) searched whole-genome sequences from 11,035 parent-child trios and found the same apparent signature, a second mtDNA haplotype matching the father's at 5 to 25%, in 7 families. In all 7, the father carried a large rare **mega-NUMT**, a **nu**clear insertion of **m**i**t**ochondrial DNA made of concatenated copies of the mitochondrial genome in a chromosome. It was transmitted like an autosomal gene, and it was being read by sequencing as though it were mitochondrial. They concluded there was no evidence of paternal transmission of mtDNA in humans. This is the gene transfer from organelle to nucleus that the chapter describes as ancient, still happening: a later survey of 66,083 genomes found new NUMT insertions in about one in every 10,000 births.

Q: How does maternal inheritance of chloroplasts vary among plants, and what produces a variegated leaf?
![A variegated leaf with irregular green and white sectors](assets/img/fig-14-62.png)
A: In about two thirds of higher plants the chloroplast precursors from the pollen do not enter the zygote, so chloroplast DNA is maternally inherited like mitochondrial DNA. In the rest, pollen plastids do enter and inheritance is biparental. In such plants a zygote can start with a mixture of normal and defective chloroplasts, and as the plant grows, **mitotic segregation** sorts them out into lineages of cells with only one kind. The result is **variegation**: green patches where cells inherited normal chloroplasts and white patches where they inherited defective ones.

Q: Why does mitochondrial DNA accumulate mutations so much faster than nuclear DNA?
A: About **100-fold** faster, for two reasons. The mitochondrion is the cell's main source of reactive oxygen species, such as hydrogen peroxide, superoxide and hydroxyl radicals, so its DNA sits right next to the damage. And mitochondria have less elaborate DNA replication and repair systems, so the damage is corrected less efficiently. Mathematical modelling suggests most such mutations arise in childhood or early adulthood and then spread by clonal expansion later in life; through mitotic segregation some cells end up with a high enough load of faulty mtDNA to impair their metabolism.
Tags: organelle-genetics, disease

Q: A mother carries a mixture of normal and mutant mitochondrial DNA. Why might her children be affected to very different degrees?
![A primordial germ cell with a mixture of normal and mutant mitochondria giving rise to oocytes with high, intermediate and low proportions of mutant mtDNA, leading to affected, mildly affected and unaffected offspring](assets/img/fig-14-63.png)
A: Because the mixture is resampled on the way to each egg. When a primordial germ cell carries both kinds, the genomes are distributed **randomly** into primary oocytes and then replicated as each oocyte matures. Some oocytes end up with a high load of mutant mtDNA, some with a low load, so children inherit very different proportions. A child with a low fraction will be healthy unless mitotic segregation happens to enrich the defective mitochondria in a particular tissue later. The same randomness is why severity, age of onset and even the tissue affected vary so much within one family.

Q+: How can you recognize a disease caused by a mitochondrial DNA mutation in a family tree, and which tissues suffer most?
A: It passes from affected **mothers to both daughters and sons**, but only the **daughters** pass it on to their children; affected fathers never transmit it. Symptoms vary greatly between relatives. Muscle and the nervous system are the most commonly affected, probably because of their especially high demand for ATP. Diseases caused by mutations in **nuclear**-encoded mitochondrial proteins do not follow this pattern; they are inherited in the ordinary Mendelian way, even though the defect is in the mitochondrion.

Q: Keeping a separate genetic system is expensive. What does it cost the cell?
A: More than **90 proteins** must be encoded in the nucleus solely for the purpose, including many ribosomal proteins, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, DNA and RNA polymerases, and RNA-processing and modifying enzymes. The mitochondrial genome also carries the risk of disease described above. And because most organelle-encoded proteins work in complexes with nuclear-encoded partners, the two genomes' expression has to be coordinated, with a quality-control burden whenever the two supplies do not match. Signaling systems have evolved that adjust nuclear transcription in response to damage or other disturbances in mitochondrial or chloroplast function.

Q+: Peroxisomes and lysosomes have no genomes. Which reasons does the book offer for mitochondria and chloroplasts keeping theirs?
A: Three, none settled.
1. The non-ribosomal proteins still encoded in organelles are typically **highly hydrophobic**, and making them in the cytosol and importing them may be too difficult or costly. The weakness is that several very hydrophobic proteins are made in the cytosol and imported successfully.
2. The arrangement may be the remnant of an ancient **endosymbiotic conflict**: with essential complexes needing both genomes, neither the nucleus nor the organelle can control them alone.
3. Gene transfer may simply be **still in progress**, and the cell has no alternative for now.

The book calls the question one of the most interesting unanswered problems in evolutionary biology.
Tags: organelle-genetics, evolution

Q+: Has anyone tested the hydrophobicity explanation against the actual pattern of gene loss across eukaryotes?
A: Yes. Johnston and Williams (*Cell Systems*, 2016) inferred the order in which genes were lost from 2015 complete mitochondrial genomes across the eukaryotic tree. The genes retained most persistently encode the **structural cores** of the electron-transport complexes, the subunits around which the rest assemble. Hydrophobicity alone did not explain the pattern: it took **high protein hydrophobicity combined with high GC content** of the gene, and a model built on those pressures also predicted which artificial gene transfers to the nucleus succeed in experiments. The book's objection to hypothesis 1, that some hydrophobic proteins are imported fine, is therefore partly answered: hydrophobicity is one of the pressures, not the whole story.

Q: True or false? The three respiratory enzyme complexes in the mitochondrial inner membrane tend to associate with each other in ways that facilitate the correct transfer of electrons between appropriate complexes.
A: **True**, as far as the association goes. Gently isolated Complexes I, III and IV come out together as **supercomplexes**, 1.7 megadaltons for the bovine heart one, with the quinol-binding sites of I and III facing each other and the cytochrome c sites of III and IV close together, and their formation depends on cardiolipin and dedicated proteins. The trap is in "facilitate". That the complexes associate is established; that the association speeds electron transfer by channelling carriers directly from one complex to the next is not. Kinetic work shows a single shared ubiquinone pool that is neither partitioned nor channelled, so supercomplexes may matter more for stability and packing than for catalysis.

Q: True or false? The number of c subunits in the rotor ring of ATP synthase defines how many protons need to pass through the turbine to make each molecule of ATP.
A: **True**. Each c subunit carries one proton-binding glutamate or aspartate, so one full turn of the ring passes exactly as many protons as there are c subunits, and one full turn makes three ATP in the F₁ head because there are three β catalytic sites. The cost per ATP is therefore ring size divided by three: about 2.7 protons for the 8-subunit mammalian ring and about 4.7 for the 14-subunit chloroplast ring. The one qualification is that the "turbine" cost is not the whole cost of ATP to the mitochondrion, since moving phosphate in and exchanging ATP for ADP across the inner membrane spends roughly one more proton's worth per ATP.

Q: True or false? Mutations that are inherited according to Mendelian rules affect nuclear genes; mutations whose inheritance violates Mendelian rules are likely to affect organellar genes.
A: **True**, with the word "likely" doing real work. Nuclear genes segregate at meiosis in Mendelian ratios. Organelle genes do not go through meiosis: they are inherited maternally in animals and most plants, and within a lineage they sort out by random mitotic segregation, which gives non-Mendelian patterns such as a mother passing a disease to all her children in variable degree while an affected father passes it to none. The converse is the trap to avoid: a disease of **mitochondrial function** is not necessarily non-Mendelian, since many are caused by mutations in nuclear-encoded mitochondrial proteins and follow ordinary Mendelian rules. And non-Mendelian inheritance has other causes too, which is why it only makes an organellar gene "likely".

Q: Heart muscle oxidizing glucose consumes O₂ at 10 μmol/min per gram of tissue and keeps ATP at a steady 5 μmol/g. Glucose gives 30 ATP, 26 of them from oxidative phosphorylation using 12 electron pairs, and each pair reduces one oxygen atom. How many seconds does it take the heart to use up its steady-state ATP?
A: About **6 seconds**.
- 10 μmol O₂ per minute is 20 μmol of oxygen atoms, so 20 μmol of electron pairs per minute.
- Each pair yields $26/12$ ATP by oxidative phosphorylation, giving $20 \times 26/12 = 43.3$ μmol ATP per minute.
- Adding substrate-level ATP: 6 O₂ are used per glucose, so glucose is burned at $10/6 = 1.67$ μmol/min, and $1.67 \times 30 = 50$ μmol ATP per minute in total.
- $5 / 50 = 0.1$ minutes $= 6$ seconds (or about 7 seconds counting oxidative phosphorylation alone).

So the heart turns over its entire ATP pool roughly every six seconds. It holds almost no reserve: stop the oxygen supply and contraction fails within seconds, which is why a blocked coronary artery is so quickly damaging.

Q: Phosphate is carried into the mitochondrial matrix together with protons, as HPO₄²⁻ plus 2H⁺. How do the membrane potential and the pH gradient each affect this transport?
![Phosphate and two protons moving together through a carrier in the inner membrane into the matrix](assets/img/fig-q14-01.png)
A: The **membrane potential has no effect** and the **pH gradient drives it**. The transported unit carries two negative charges on the phosphate and two positive charges on the protons, so it is electrically **neutral**, and the matrix-negative voltage neither attracts nor repels it. The pH gradient, however, favours moving protons into the matrix, where their concentration is lower, and the carrier couples phosphate uptake to that downhill proton movement. So phosphate import taps only the ΔpH part of the proton-motive force, which is one reason the mitochondrion cannot let ΔpH fall to zero even though the membrane potential dominates the total. It is also part of the hidden proton cost of each ATP made.

Q: Isolated mitochondria given succinate but no oxygen reduce all their electron carriers. When oxygen is added, the carriers reoxidize at different rates: cytochrome (a + a₃) fastest, then c, then c₁, and b slowest. What order are they in along the chain?
![Percent reduced cytochrome over time: all carriers reduced after adding succinate, then after adding O2, cytochrome a plus a3 reoxidizes fastest, followed by c, c1 and b](assets/img/fig-q14-02.png)
A: **b → c₁ → c → (a + a₃) → O₂**. With no oxygen, electrons pile up and every carrier is reduced. When O₂ arrives, it can take electrons only from the carrier next to it, so that one is oxidized first and fastest; it then pulls electrons from its upstream neighbour, which is oxidized next, and so on back up the chain. The carrier furthest from oxygen stays reduced longest because it is the last to have anywhere to send its electrons. Reading the curves from fastest to slowest therefore reads the chain backward from oxygen. This is the logic of Chance's classic spectroscopic ordering of the respiratory chain.

Q: Both H⁺ and Ca²⁺ move through the cytosol. Why do protons move so much faster, and what would freezing do to each?
A: Because a proton does not have to travel. H⁺ moves by **hopping** through the hydrogen-bonded network of water: a hydronium ion hands a proton to its neighbour, which hands a different proton on, so the charge passes along a chain of water molecules while each individual proton moves only a short distance. A Ca²⁺ ion has to diffuse physically, dragging its shell of bound water with it, and in the cytosol it is also bound and buffered by proteins. On freezing, Ca²⁺ is essentially immobilized, since ions cannot diffuse through a solid lattice. Protons can still hop through the hydrogen-bonded lattice of ice, so they keep moving and become far faster **relative** to Ca²⁺. Whether protons move faster in ice than in liquid water has been argued both ways; the safe conclusion is that freezing stops calcium but not protons.

Q: Pasteur found that when he added O₂ to yeast growing anaerobically on glucose, their glucose consumption fell dramatically. Explain the **Pasteur effect**.
A: With oxygen, yeast can oxidize glucose completely and get about 30 ATP from each molecule instead of the 2 of fermentation, roughly 15 times as much. To meet the same ATP demand they therefore need only about a fifteenth as much glucose, and they consume less. The mechanism is feedback: when respiration supplies abundant ATP, the high ATP level, and intermediates such as citrate, inhibit the regulated early steps of glycolysis, notably phosphofructokinase, so glucose uptake slows to match need. Without oxygen the brake is released and glycolysis runs flat out to compensate for its poor yield.

Q: In the classic single-molecule experiment, an α₃β₃γ complex is fixed to a surface by its β subunits and a fluorescent actin filament is attached to γ. With ATP added, the filament rotates. At low ATP it turns in 120° steps separated by pauses. Why steps, and why 120°?
![An F1 head anchored upside down by its beta subunits with an actin filament attached to the gamma subunit, and a trace of revolutions against time rising in steps](assets/img/fig-q14-03.png)
A: Because there are **three** β catalytic sites around the head, spaced 120° apart, and each step is the hydrolysis of **one** ATP at one site. Hydrolysis at a site pushes the asymmetric γ subunit round by a third of a turn, bringing the next site into the conformation that binds ATP. At low ATP concentration the next ATP takes a variable time to arrive, so the rotor pauses between steps for variable lengths of time, exactly as the trace shows. This was the direct visual proof of rotary catalysis that the indirect evidence had pointed to (Yasuda et al., 1998).

Q+: In its normal mode inside the cell, how many ATP molecules would you expect ATP synthase to make per 360° rotation of γ?

The number is fixed by the head, not by the rotor ring: the ring size decides how many protons pay for the turn, but every turn still makes three.

Q: A respiring liver mitochondrion has a spherical matrix 1 μm across. Its matrix is at pH 7.5 against a cytosol at pH 7. How many free protons are in the matrix, and how many would have to be pumped out to take it from pH 7 to 7.5?
A: About **10** free protons, and only about **22** pumped.
- Volume: $\frac{4}{3}\pi (0.5 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m})^3 = 5.24 \times 10^{-19}$ m³ $= 5.24 \times 10^{-16}$ L.
- At pH 7.5, $[\text{H}^+] = 10^{-7.5} = 3.16 \times 10^{-8}$ M, so $3.16 \times 10^{-8} \times 5.24 \times 10^{-16} \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} \approx 10$ protons.
- At pH 7 there would be about 31.5 protons, so reaching pH 7.5 means removing $31.5 - 10 \approx 22$.

The point is how absurdly small that is. A single mitochondrion has thousands of respiratory complexes each pumping many protons per second, so the free protons of the matrix could be exhausted almost instantly. In reality the matrix is heavily **buffered**, so the number of protons that must actually be pumped to shift its pH is vastly larger, and much of the pumped proton gradient is stored in the membrane potential rather than as free H⁺.

Q: The uncoupler 2,4-dinitrophenol was once prescribed as a diet drug. How would an uncoupler cause weight loss, and why is it no longer prescribed?
A: Dinitrophenol is a lipid-soluble weak acid that carries protons across the inner membrane, letting them leak back into the matrix without passing through ATP synthase. Normally electron flow is tightly coupled to ATP production: if the gradient is not used, it builds up until it stops the pumps and respiration slows. An uncoupler removes that brake. Electrons flow flat out, fuel, including fat, is burned at a high rate, and because the ATP yield is poor the body burns yet more to meet its needs, with the energy released as **heat**. That is weight loss by the brown-fat mechanism, applied to every cell. It is no longer prescribed because the dose is hard to control: the heat can cause lethal hyperthermia, and ATP production can fall too low. Dinitrophenol caused deaths and was withdrawn in the late 1930s.

Q: Using $E = hc/\lambda$, with $h = 6.6 \times 10^{-37}$ kJ sec/photon and $c = 3.0 \times 10^{17}$ nm/sec, what is the energy of a mole of photons at 400 nm, 680 nm and 800 nm?
A: $E$ per mole $= 6.6 \times 10^{-37} \times 3.0 \times 10^{17} \times 6 \times 10^{23} / \lambda = 1.19 \times 10^5 / \lambda$ kJ/mole.
- 400 nm (violet): **297 kJ/mole**
- 680 nm (red): **175 kJ/mole**
- 800 nm (near-infrared): **149 kJ/mole**

Energy is inversely proportional to wavelength, so violet photons carry twice the energy of near-infrared ones. For scale, a mole of red photons carries nearly six times the 30.5 kJ of standard ATP hydrolysis.

Q: Why are plants green? "Because they contain chlorophyll" is not enough.
A: Because chlorophyll absorbs light strongly in the **blue** (about 400 to 500 nm) and the **red** (about 600 to 700 nm) and absorbs relatively poorly in between. The green light that is not absorbed is reflected or transmitted, and that is what reaches our eyes. So a plant is green because green is the part of sunlight it largely **rejects**, not the part it uses. The accessory carotenoids absorb some of the blue-green range that chlorophyll misses, but not enough to change the overall colour.

Q: In a variegated *Aucuba* leaf, yellow patches surrounded by green are common, but green patches surrounded by yellow are never seen. Why?
![A leaf of Aucuba japonica spotted with many yellow flecks and a few larger yellow patches on a green background](assets/img/fig-q14-04.png)
A: Because mitotic segregation runs one way visibly. The leaf starts from cells carrying a **mixture** of normal and defective chloroplasts, and such cells look green because the normal chloroplasts are enough to colour them. As they divide, chloroplasts are shared out randomly, and occasionally a daughter cell receives only defective ones. That cell and all its descendants can never regain normal chloroplasts, since chloroplasts arise only from existing chloroplasts, so they form a **yellow patch** within the green. Cells that segregate the other way, to all-normal, are also green, so they are indistinguishable from their mixed neighbours. A green patch inside yellow would require a lineage that had already lost all its normal chloroplasts to produce green descendants, which is impossible.
