Q: Before 1953, what were the two questions about heredity that any candidate molecule had to answer?
A: First, how could the information needed to specify an organism be carried in a chemical form? Second, how could that information be duplicated and copied from generation to generation? The chapter frames the problem historically: geneticists knew the hereditary information was copied and transmitted from cell to daughter cell millions of times over the life of a multicellular organism and survived essentially unchanged, so whatever carried it had to be capable of accurate and almost unlimited replication while also directing development and the daily life of every cell. The double helix answered both at once, the first because DNA is a linear polymer of four monomers strung out in a defined sequence like letters in an alphabetic script, the second because each strand is exactly complementary to its partner and can therefore act as a template for rebuilding it.
Tags: core

---

Q: Why did biologists in the 1940s find it so hard to believe DNA was the genetic material?
A: The molecule seemed too simple. It is a long polymer built from only four types of nucleotide subunit, and those four resemble one another chemically, so it looked like a monotonous structural element rather than an archive. Protein, with twenty different amino acids and visibly varied structures, looked far more promising as an information carrier. Chromosomes were known to be roughly half DNA and half protein, and for decades the DNA was thought to be the scaffolding. The x-ray diffraction work of the early 1950s, showing that DNA was two strands wound into a helix, was one of the major clues that led to the Watson and Crick model, and the model made DNA's potential for replication and information storage immediately apparent.
Tags: core, history

Q: What is a **chromosome**, and why is it visible in the light microscope only some of the time?
![Two adjacent plant cells stained with DAPI, one with a single sausage-shaped chromosome shaded dark brown just before division and one whose chromosomes are too extended to distinguish](assets/img/fig-4-01.png)
A: A chromosome is a threadlike structure in the nucleus of a eukaryotic cell, made of one enormously long DNA molecule together with the proteins bound to it. It becomes visible as a distinct object under the light microscope only when it becomes a compact, sausage-shaped structure just before cell division. For the rest of the cell's life the same chromosomes are present but in a much more extended conformation, so a stained nucleus shows a diffuse mass rather than separate objects. The name means "coloured body", from the Greek *chroma*, and the same root gives chromatin, which the book glosses that way because of the staining properties of the DNA-protein complex.

Q: Trace the two experiments that identified DNA as the genetic material.
![The S and R strains of Streptococcus pneumoniae, with heat-killed S transforming R cells, and a cell-free extract fractionated into RNA, protein, DNA, lipid and carbohydrate to find the active component](assets/img/fig-4-02.png)
A: Two strains of *Streptococcus pneumoniae* differ both in colony appearance and in whether they kill: the smooth (S) strain is pathogenic, the rough (R) strain is not. In the 1920s experiment, R cells grown in the presence of heat-killed S cells, or of a cell-free extract of them, were transformed into S cells whose daughters were themselves pathogenic. That established only that some substance carrying heritable information was present in S cells, since heat-killed cells are not alive to donate anything else. The 1940s experiment then fractionated the cell-free extract into purified RNA, protein, DNA, lipid and carbohydrate and tested each for the ability to transform R cells. Only the DNA fraction worked, which identified the molecule.

Q: What are the parts of a **nucleotide** in DNA, and how are nucleotides joined into a strand?
![The building blocks of DNA, a single strand, a double-stranded molecule with hydrogen-bonded base pairs, and the same molecule wound into a double helix](assets/img/fig-4-03.png)
A: A nucleotide is a five-carbon sugar carrying a phosphate group and a nitrogen-containing base. In DNA the sugar is deoxyribose, which is where the name deoxyribonucleic acid comes from, and the base is adenine, cytosine, guanine or thymine. The nucleotides are covalently linked through their sugars and phosphates, so the chain has a backbone of alternating sugar-phosphate-sugar-phosphate with the bases hanging off it. Because only the base differs between the four subunits, a DNA strand is like a necklace with one kind of chain and four kinds of bead. The same four letters are used loosely for either the bases alone or the whole nucleotides.

Q+: What gives a DNA strand its **chemical polarity**, and what is the knob-and-hole picture the book uses for it?
![A phosphodiester bond joining the 5′ carbon of one sugar to the 3′ carbon of the next, with the 5′ end of the chain at the top and the 3′ end at the bottom](assets/img/fig-4-04.png)
A: The linkage is a phosphodiester bond, and its two ends are chemically different: it connects the 5′ carbon of one sugar to the 3′ carbon of the next. Think of each sugar as a block with a protruding knob on one side, the 5′ phosphate, and a hole on the other, the 3′ hydroxyl. A chain assembled by fitting knobs into holes necessarily has every subunit lined up the same way, and its two ends are distinguishable: one terminates in a hole, the free 3′ hydroxyl, and the other in a knob, the free 5′ phosphate. That is why the ends are named the 3′ and 5′ ends, from the numbering of the carbons in the deoxyribose ring. A DNA strand is therefore both linear and directional, which is what lets it be read like the letters on a page.

Q: What holds the two strands of the double helix together, and why does the pairing have to be purine with pyrimidine?
![Hydrogen bonds forming between A and T and between G and C, and four base pairs viewed from the side of the helix lying perpendicular to its axis](assets/img/fig-4-05.png)
A: Hydrogen bonds between the bases, two between A and T and three between G and C. The shapes and chemical structures of the bases allow those bonds to form efficiently only in those two combinations. In each pair a bulkier two-ring purine is matched with a single-ring pyrimidine, which makes every base pair the same width and so holds the two sugar-phosphate backbones a constant distance apart the whole way along the molecule. A purine-purine pair would be too wide and a pyrimidine-pyrimidine pair too narrow, so either would buckle the backbone. The pairing works only if the two chains are antiparallel, and one consequence of all this is that all the bases sit on the inside of the helix and the backbones on the outside.

Q: The DNA double helix has {{10.4}} nucleotide pairs per complete turn, adjacent pairs sit {{0.34}} nm apart centre to centre, and the coiling of the two strands creates a wide {{major}} groove and a narrower {{minor}} groove.
![Space-filling model of the double helix showing the major and minor grooves and a 2 nm scale bar](assets/img/fig-4-06.png)
A: The helix is right-handed, and the two grooves are not a decorative detail: they are where proteins read the sequence without opening the helix, since the edges of the bases are exposed there. The grooves come out different sizes because the two backbones are not diametrically opposite each other across the helix axis. The book quotes 10.4 rather than the round 10 you often see, which is the value for B-form DNA in solution rather than in a fibre.
Tags: dna-structure, structure

Q: How does the structure of DNA answer the second question about heredity, the copying one?
![Strand S and strand S-prime separating, each acting as a template so that one parent helix gives two identical daughter helices](assets/img/fig-4-07.png)
A: Because A pairs only with T and G only with C, each strand contains a sequence exactly complementary to its partner, so either strand fully specifies the other. Designate the two strands S and S′: S can serve as a template for making a new S′, and S′ can serve as a template for making a new S. The strands separate, each acquires a newly built partner identical to the one it lost, and the result is two identical daughter helices. What makes it satisfying is that the copying mechanism was not an extra hypothesis bolted on, but a consequence of the structure that had been proposed to explain the x-ray data.
Tags: core, dna-structure

Q: What is a **genome**, and is every gene a recipe for a protein?
![Four genes along a DNA double helix, three of them transcribed into RNAs that direct the production of proteins and one whose final product is the RNA itself](assets/img/fig-4-08.png)
A: The genome is the complete store of information in an organism's DNA, specifying all the RNA molecules and proteins the organism will ever synthesize; the word is also used for the DNA itself. Not every gene codes for protein. Protein-coding genes are the majority and are the ones with the clearest mutant phenotypes, but there are also many RNA genes, segments of DNA whose functionally significant final product is an RNA molecule. So the common definition of a gene as a stretch of DNA specifying a particular protein is too narrow, and the human genome carries roughly 5000 noncoding RNA genes alongside its 20,000 protein-coding ones.
Tags: core, regulation

Q: The chapter uses two comparisons to convey how much information a genome holds. What are they?
![The nucleotide sequence of the human β-globin gene written out, with the three coding regions highlighted in yellow](assets/img/fig-4-09.png)
A: The nucleotide sequence of a very small human gene, the β-globin gene, written out in the four-letter alphabet, occupies a quarter of a page of text. The complete sequence of the human genome, written the same way, would fill more than a thousand books the size of *Molecular Biology of the Cell*. The β-globin figure is also worth reading for what it shows about gene structure: only three highlighted stretches of that quarter page actually specify the amino acid sequence of the protein, and the cell splices those three together at the RNA level. Hemoglobin has four subunits, two of each type, and the α-globin gene carrying the information for the other type sits on a different chromosome.

Q: In eukaryotes the DNA is enclosed in a nucleus. What is that compartment made of, and what does the separation buy the cell?
![Electron micrograph of a fibroblast nucleus and a schematic showing the double membrane, nuclear pores, continuity with the endoplasmic reticulum, the nuclear lamina and dark-staining heterochromatin](assets/img/fig-4-10.png)
A: The nucleus occupies about 10% of the cell volume in many cells and is delimited by a nuclear envelope of two concentric lipid bilayers, punctured at intervals by large nuclear pores through which molecules pass between nucleus and cytosol. The outer membrane is directly connected to the endoplasmic reticulum, so the space between the two nuclear membranes is continuous with the ER lumen. Internally the envelope is supported by the nuclear lamina, a thin feltlike mesh of intermediate filaments just beneath the inner membrane. The compartment concentrates the many proteins that act on DNA where they are needed, and it keeps nuclear and cytosolic proteins separate, which turns out to matter for a great deal of eukaryotic cell biology. Archaea and bacteria have no such compartment: their DNA sits with everything else inside the plasma membrane.
Tags: organelles, eukaryotes

Q: State the DNA packaging problem in numbers, and give the analogy the book uses.
A: If the double helices of all 46 chromosomes in a human cell were laid end to end they would reach about 2 metres, while the nucleus containing them is only about 6 μm across. Geometrically that is like packing 40 km, some 24 miles, of extremely fine thread into a tennis ball. The packing is done by specialized proteins that bind the DNA and fold it into organized coils and loops, which stops it becoming an unmanageable tangle. The constraint that makes the problem hard is that the compaction cannot be permanent: the DNA has to stay accessible to the enzymes that replicate it, repair it, and transcribe its genes.
Tags: chromatin, core

Q: What is **chromatin**, and does a bacterium have any?
![DNA threading through a row of nucleosomes with a crowd of differently shaped non-histone proteins bound along it, drawn beside a single histone protein](assets/img/fig-4-20.png)
A: Chromatin is the complex of DNA with the proteins tightly bound to it, meaning both the histones that package it and the many non-histone proteins needed for gene expression, replication and repair, plus numerous RNA molecules. By mass a chromosome is about one-third DNA and two-thirds protein, with histone and non-histone protein contributing roughly equally. Bacteria lack a nuclear compartment and generally carry their genes on a single, often circular DNA molecule, which is also associated with proteins that package and condense it. Those proteins are different from the eukaryotic ones, so although the bacterial DNA and its proteins are commonly called the bacterial chromosome, it does not have the structure of a eukaryotic chromosome.
Tags: chromatin, core

Q: How many chromosomes does a human cell contain, and which cells are the exceptions?
A: Each human cell nucleus contains 46 chromosomes: 22 pairs common to both sexes plus two sex chromosomes, XX in females and XY in males. The two members of a pair, one inherited from each parent, are called homologous chromosomes or homologs, and the only nonhomologous pair is the X and Y in males. The exceptions are the gametes, which are haploid, and a few highly specialized cell types that cannot multiply: red blood cells, which lack DNA altogether, and megakaryocytes, which have replicated their DNA repeatedly without completing cell division.
Tags: chromosomes, human-genome

Q: What is **chromosome painting**, and what is the older technique it partly replaced?
![Human chromosomes painted a different colour each, shown as they spilled from the lysed cell and then lined up in numerical order as a karyotype](assets/img/fig-4-11.png)
A: Painting uses DNA hybridization. Short strands of nucleic acid tagged with fluorescent dyes act as probes that find their complementary sequence and light up the chromosome wherever they bind, and by giving each chromosome's worth of probe a different dye combination every chromosome gets a distinct colour. The chromosomes are treated to separate the two strands of the helix enough for base-pairing while keeping overall structure intact, and the technique is called spectral karyotyping. It is usually done during mitosis, when chromosomes are compacted and easy to see. The older method is staining with dyes such as Giemsa, which produces a striking and reproducible pattern of bands along each mitotic chromosome. Those banding patterns are unique per chromosome and gave the first reliable way to identify and number the human chromosomes, though what they reflect, variations in chromatin structure or base composition or both, is still not well understood.
Tags: chromosomes, human-genome

Q+: The display of all 46 chromosomes at mitosis has a name, and both techniques are used clinically for the same purpose. What are they?
![The banding patterns of the human chromosomes numbered in approximate order of size, with the centromere position marked in red and knobs on chromosomes 13, 14, 15, 21 and 22](assets/img/fig-4-12.png)
A: The display is a karyotype. Both banding and painting are used by cytogeneticists to detect chromosome abnormalities, either inherited ones or the rearrangements that appear in cancer cells as they progress to malignancy, with painting the more sensitive of the two. Losing part of a chromosome, or swapping parts between chromosomes, changes the banding pattern or the colour pattern. The red knobs on chromosomes 13, 14, 15, 21 and 22 in the banding map are worth knowing separately: they mark the genes for the large ribosomal RNAs, which form the nucleolus.

Q: What is a **reciprocal chromosomal translocation**, and how does painting reveal one?
![Normal human chromosomes 4 and 6 painted green and pink, and the same two chromosomes in a carrier of a reciprocal translocation, each carrying a segment of the other's colour](assets/img/fig-4-13.png)
A: It is the product of an abnormal recombination event in which the DNA double helix of one chromosome crosses over with that of another, so each chromosome ends up carrying a piece of the other. Under painting the result is unmistakable, because a chromosome that should be one uniform colour now carries a block of a different colour at one end, and its partner carries the reciprocal block. That sensitivity to short translocated pieces is why painting beats banding here, and translocations are a frequent event in cancer cells.

Q: There is no simple relationship between chromosome number, genome size and organism complexity. What are the book's examples?
![A Chinese muntjac with 46 chromosomes and an Indian muntjac with 6, each shown with its karyotype](assets/img/fig-4-14.png)
A: Human cells have 46 chromosomes, some small deer have 6, and the common carp has more than 100. The two muntjac deer make the point most sharply: they are closely related, contain a similar number of genes, and differ enormously in chromosome number, because in the evolution of the Indian muntjac initially separate chromosomes fused without any major effect on the animal. Genome size varies just as independently. The human genome is 200 times larger than that of *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, 30 times smaller than that of some plants and amphibians, and 200 times smaller than that of one species of amoeba, and closely related bony fish can vary several hundredfold in DNA content while carrying roughly the same number of genes. The differences are overwhelmingly in noncoding DNA between the genes, not in gene number. Genomes and chromosomes have each been shaped by a unique history of seemingly random genetic events acted on by poorly understood selection pressures.

Q+: What exactly does "the human genome sequence" refer to, and where is the part of it that is not in the nucleus?
A: It refers to the complete nucleotide sequence of the DNA in the 24 nuclear chromosomes, the 22 autosomes plus X and Y, together with the mitochondria. Almost all of the $3.1 \times 10^9$ nucleotide pairs are distributed over those nuclear chromosomes. A minute fraction, 16,569 nucleotide pairs, is the mitochondrial genome, present in multiple copies per cell. The $3.1 \times 10^9$ figure is the haploid amount, so a diploid human somatic cell nucleus holds twice that, $6.2 \times 10^9$ nucleotide pairs, before it duplicates its chromosomes in preparation for division.

Q: If you drew the human genome with a 1 mm space between each nucleotide pair, how long would it be, and how far apart would the genes sit?
![The human genome drawn at 1 mm per nucleotide pair as a 3100 km line laid across the centre of Africa](assets/img/fig-4-16.png)
A: It would extend 3100 km, nearly 2000 miles, which is far enough to stretch across the centre of Africa, the site of our origins. At that scale there would be on average one protein-coding gene every 160 metres, an average gene would extend for 26 metres, and the coding sequence inside that gene would add up to only just over a metre. That last ratio is the whole point of the figure: within a gene, coding DNA is about 4% of the length, and across the genome as a whole it is about 1%.

Q: Why are human genes so large when the protein they encode is not?
![Chromosome 22 expanded tenfold three times, from the whole chromosome to a stretch with 40 genes, to four genes, to one gene showing its exons, introns and regulatory sequences](assets/img/fig-4-15.png)
A: Because most of a human gene is not coding sequence. Only about 1300 nucleotides are needed to encode a protein of average size, about 430 amino acids, yet the median protein-coding gene is 26,000 nucleotide pairs long. The difference is introns: the coding sequences sit in exons, which are interrupted by long noncoding intervening sequences, so a typical human gene is a long string of alternating exons and introns with most of its length being intron. On top of that, each gene has regulatory DNA sequences that decide when, where and how strongly it is expressed, and in humans those are spread out over hundreds of thousands of nucleotide pairs. Organisms with concise genomes, prokaryotes especially, either lack introns or have short ones, and their regulatory sequences are more compact and less numerous, which is why their genes are so much smaller and their chromosomes so much denser in coding DNA.
Tags: human-genome, regulatory-dna

Q: The chapter quotes a commentator describing the human genome. What is the description, and what point is being made?
A: "In some ways it may resemble your garage/bedroom/refrigerator/life: highly individualistic, but unkempt; little evidence of organization; much accumulated clutter (referred to by the uninitiated as 'junk'); virtually nothing ever discarded; and the few patently valuable items indiscriminately, apparently carelessly, scattered throughout." The point is that the archive of information needed to produce a human is in an alarming state of chaos: about 1% codes for protein, nearly half is mobile elements that inserted themselves over evolutionary time and multiplied like parasites, and the valuable parts are scattered through it without evident arrangement. That is a claim about history rather than about function, which is why the chapter defers the explanation to its final section on how genomes evolve.
Tags: human-genome, genome-evolution

Q: What three types of specialized DNA sequence does a linear eukaryotic chromosome need in order to be inherited?
![A chromosome through the cell cycle showing telomeres at each end, multiple replication origins, and a centromere that acquires a kinetochore and attaches to the mitotic spindle](assets/img/fig-4-19.png)
A: Replication origins, one centromere, and two telomeres, each of which binds specific proteins that guide the machinery of replication and segregation. Origins are the positions at which duplication of the DNA begins, and a eukaryotic chromosome has many of them so that the whole molecule can be replicated quickly. The centromere lets one copy of each duplicated chromosome be pulled into each daughter cell: a protein complex called the kinetochore forms there and attaches the chromosome to the mitotic spindle so that the two sister chromatids are pulled apart. The telomeres are the ends, carrying repeated sequences that let the ends be replicated efficiently, and they also form structures that stop the cell mistaking a natural chromosome end for a broken DNA molecule in need of repair. All three were defined by experiment in budding yeast, whose chromosomes are small and easy to manipulate.
Tags: chromosomes, centromeres, telomeres

Q+: How big are those three elements in yeast, and how well does that scale to a human chromosome?
A: In budding yeast all three are short, typically fewer than 1000 base pairs each, so they use only a tiny fraction of the chromosome's information-carrying capacity. Telomere sequences stay fairly simple and short in all eukaryotes. Centromeres and origins do not: in more complex organisms they are much longer, and a human centromere can contain up to a million nucleotide pairs. More awkwardly, the human centromere may not require any defined nucleotide sequence at all, and is instead thought to be a large, regularly repeating protein-nucleic acid structure that is inherited when the chromosome replicates. So the yeast result identified the functions but not, in the human case, the things that perform them.
Tags: centromeres, model-organisms

Q: Where in the cell cycle does a chromosome condense, and why does it need to?
![The eukaryotic cell cycle, with interphase covering gene expression and chromosome duplication, and a brief M phase in which the mitotic spindle separates the sister chromatids](assets/img/fig-4-17.png)
A: During interphase, which occupies most of the cycle, genes are expressed and chromosomes are replicated, with the two replicas staying together as a pair of sister chromatids. Throughout that time the chromosomes are extended, so individual chromosomes cannot easily be told apart in the nucleus. Only during the much briefer mitosis does each chromosome condense, and the reason is mechanical: the two sister chromatids have to be captured by the spindle and hauled to opposite ends of the cell, which is hopeless for a two-metre tangle of threads and straightforward for 46 compact rods. M phase occupies only about an hour in many mammalian cells. Almost every image of chromosomes in this chapter is a mitotic chromosome, which is worth remembering, because it is the least representative state a chromosome spends its life in.
Tags: chromosomes, core

Q: How much does a human chromosome have to be compacted, worked out for chromosome 22?
![Electron micrograph of a metaphase chromosome and a drawing of the same duplicated chromosome showing its two chromatids joined at the centromere](assets/img/fig-4-18.png)
A: Chromosome 22 holds 48 million nucleotide pairs, which as one stretched-out double helix would extend about 1.5 cm. At mitosis the chromosome measures only about 2 μm, so the end-to-end compaction ratio is more than 7000-fold. Interphase chromosomes are much less condensed than that but are still tightly packed, and they are dynamic rather than uniformly folded: specific regions decondense to give access for transcription, repair and replication, then recondense when the job is done. So the packaging has to permit rapid, localized, on-demand access rather than being a single static arrangement.

Q: What is a **nucleosome core particle**, and what did nuclease digestion establish about it?
![Chromatin digested by nuclease to release nucleosome core particles, which dissociate at high salt into a histone octamer and 147 nucleotide pairs of DNA, and further into H2A, H2B, H3 and H4](assets/img/fig-4-22.png)
A: It is a complex of eight histone proteins, two molecules each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4, with 147 nucleotide pairs of double-stranded DNA wound around the outside of that octamer. It was isolated by digesting chromatin with nucleases: the linker DNA running between core particles is exposed and gets degraded, while the DNA wrapped tightly around a core is protected, so what survives digestion defines the particle. Dissociating the released particle at high salt separates the octamer from its DNA, and measuring that DNA gives the 147 nucleotide pairs, enough to wrap 1.7 times around the core. Strictly, "nucleosome" means a core particle plus one adjacent linker, though it is often used for the core particle alone.
Tags: nucleosome, chromatin, core

Q+: What does chromatin look like in the electron microscope, and how much shortening do nucleosomes achieve?
![Chromatin isolated from an interphase nucleus appearing as a thick thread, and the same chromatin experimentally decondensed into beads on a string](assets/img/fig-4-21.png)
A: Chromatin taken straight from an interphase nucleus appears as a fibre of associated nucleosomes. Treated so that it unfolds partially, it resolves into the famous beads on a string: the string is DNA and each bead is a nucleosome core particle. Linker DNA between core particles varies from a few nucleotide pairs up to about 80, so nucleosomes repeat on average every 200 nucleotide pairs. A diploid human cell with $6.2 \times 10^9$ nucleotide pairs therefore contains roughly 30 million nucleosomes. All of this first level of packing converts the DNA into a thread about one-third of its original length, which is a modest return on the effort and shows how much of the 7000-fold compaction is left to the higher levels.
Tags: nucleosome, chromatin

Q+: A nucleosome core particle is {{8}} histone molecules with {{147}} nucleotide pairs of DNA wrapped {{1.7}} times around them, and successive nucleosomes repeat on average every {{200}} nucleotide pairs.
A: The repeat is longer than the wrapped length because of the linker DNA in between, which runs from a few nucleotide pairs up to about 80. Two of the numbers are hard limits and two are averages: the octamer is always eight histones and the wrapped stretch is always 147 nucleotide pairs, since that is what nuclease digestion leaves behind, while the linker and therefore the repeat vary from place to place and from organism to organism.

Q: What is the **histone fold**, and how is an octamer assembled from it?
![The four core histones each drawn with an N-terminal tail and a histone fold, the fold itself, the H2A-H2B handshake dimer, and the assembled octamer with all eight tails protruding](assets/img/fig-4-24.png)
A: The histone fold is a structural motif shared by all four core histones, three α helices connected by two loops. The core histones are all small proteins of 102 to 135 amino acids. In assembly, the histone folds first bind each other through an interaction called the handshake to form H3-H4 and H2A-H2B dimers; two H3-H4 dimers combine into a tetramer; and that tetramer then takes on two H2A-H2B dimers to give the compact octamer around which the DNA is wound. Each histone also has a largely unstructured N-terminal tail extending out from the core, and all eight tails protrude from the disc. The tails are highly flexible and serve as binding sites for other proteins, and they are the hot spots for the covalent modifications discussed later in the chapter.
Tags: histones, nucleosome, structure

Q: How is the DNA actually held on the histone core, and why can almost any sequence be wrapped?
![The nucleosome core particle from x-ray crystallography, viewed face-on and from the edge, with one H3 N-terminal tail visible extending from the core](assets/img/fig-4-23.png)
A: The DNA is wrapped in a left-handed coil of 1.7 turns around a disc-shaped core, and the interface is extensive: 142 hydrogen bonds form between DNA and histone in each nucleosome, nearly half of them between the amino acid backbone of the histones and the sugar-phosphate backbone of the DNA. Numerous hydrophobic interactions and salt linkages contribute too, and more than a fifth of the amino acids in each core histone is a lysine or an arginine, whose positive charges neutralize the negatively charged DNA backbone. Because so much of the binding is to the backbone and to charge rather than to bases, the sequence being wrapped is largely irrelevant, which is exactly what a general-purpose packaging device needs.
Tags: nucleosome, histones, structure

Q+: Sequence is not completely irrelevant, though. Which dinucleotides does a nucleosome prefer, and where?
![DNA bending around the histone octamer, with AA, TT and TA dinucleotides preferred where the minor groove faces inward and GC preferred where it faces outward](assets/img/fig-4-25.png)
A: The path around the core is not smooth: it kinks, and bending requires substantial compression of the minor groove on the inside of each turn. AA, TT and TA dinucleotides are especially easy to compress in this way, so they are preferred at the positions where the minor groove faces inward, and GC is preferred where the minor groove faces outward. That is why some sequences bind the histone core more tightly than others, and it probably explains the striking but unusual cases where a nucleosome sits at a precisely defined position on a stretch of DNA. The preference has to be weak, though, and it is: in most chromosomal regions nucleosomes can occupy any of a number of positions, so other factors dominate.

Q: How strongly conserved are the histones, and what does the book infer from that?
A: Extraordinarily strongly. The amino acid sequence of histone H4 from a pea differs from human H4 at only two of its 102 positions. H2A and H2B have been somewhat less constrained than H3 and H4, but the general picture holds. The inference is that the function of histones involves nearly all of their amino acids, so a change almost anywhere is deleterious. That is unusual even among conserved proteins, where a structural core is typically constrained and surface residues drift; a histone is essentially all interface, either with DNA, with other histones, or with the proteins that read its tails.

Q: A nucleosome was long assumed to be a fixed structure. What did kinetic experiments show instead?
A: That the DNA in an isolated nucleosome unwraps from each end about four times per second, staying exposed for 10 to 50 milliseconds before the partially unwrapped structure recloses. So most of the DNA in a nucleosome is in principle available for other proteins to bind, without anything having to disassemble. That matters because the fixed-nucleosome picture would have made genetic readout very difficult: mechanisms that need access to specific sequences, and the transcription and replication machinery that has to pass through chromatin, would all be blocked by a permanently wrapped octamer.
Tags: nucleosome, chromatin

Q: What is an **ATP-dependent chromatin-remodeling complex**, and how does it move a nucleosome?
![A remodeling complex with its motor subunit bound to the nucleosome core and to the DNA, using ATP hydrolysis to slide the DNA relative to the core, and the structure of the yeast SWR1 complex](assets/img/fig-4-26.png)
A: It is an abundant protein machine containing a motor subunit that hydrolyzes ATP, an ATPase evolutionarily related to the DNA helicases of Chapter 5. The motor binds both the protein core of the nucleosome and the double-stranded DNA wound around it, and it uses the energy of ATP hydrolysis to move that DNA relative to the core, temporarily loosening the DNA-histone contacts. Each cycle of ATP binding, hydrolysis and product release shifts the DNA by one base pair, so many cycles are needed to slide a nucleosome any useful distance. The consequence is that nucleosomes are frequently repositioned, and therefore that all the DNA sequences in chromatin are potentially available for binding by other proteins.

Q+: Sliding is not the only thing these complexes do. What else, and how often does it happen?
![A remodeling complex catalyzing exchange of H2A-H2B dimers for variant dimers, and complete removal of the histone octamer with the help of a histone chaperone](assets/img/fig-4-27.png)
A: Some types remove all or part of the nucleosome core: they catalyze the exchange of the H2A-H2B histones for a variant pair, or strip the whole octamer off the DNA, working with histone chaperones that hold the free histones. The turnover is brisk: a typical nucleosome is replaced on the DNA every one or two hours inside the cell. Cells contain dozens of different remodeling complexes specialized for different roles, and there is roughly one complex present per five nucleosomes, so this is not a rare event happening at a few special sites but a continuous background process across the genome.

Q: What actually decides where a nucleosome sits along a stretch of DNA?
A: Mostly the other proteins bound nearby, not the sequence. Some DNA sequences do bind the histone core more tightly than others, but that preference is weak. The dominant influence is that some bound proteins favour the formation of a nucleosome next to them while others create obstacles that force nucleosomes to move elsewhere, so nucleosome positions are set by the local protein landscape. Because ATP-dependent remodeling complexes are constantly acting, the arrangement is also highly dynamic and changes rapidly according to the cell's needs. The remodelers are not free agents either: their activity is controlled, and as genes are turned on and off they are brought to specific regions of DNA where they act locally.

Q: Does chromatin in a living cell actually look like beads on a string?
![A tetranucleosome from x-ray crystallography, its schematic, and a possible zigzag arrangement of nucleosomes in the chromatin fibre](assets/img/fig-4-28.png)
A: Probably rarely. The beads-on-a-string form is what you see after treatment that unfolds chromatin; gently lysed nuclei give a fibre considerably wider than a single nucleosome, in which nucleosomes are packed on top of one another into more condensed arrays. How they are organized in those arrays is unclear, and the chapter is honest about it. The structure of a tetranucleosome, from x-ray crystallography and high-resolution electron microscopy of reconstituted chromatin, supports a zigzag model of stacking. But cryo-electron microscopy of carefully prepared nuclei suggests most regions of chromatin are less regularly structured than any such model. The neat 30 nm fibre of older textbooks is exactly what this evidence has been pulling away from.

Q: What holds stacked nucleosomes together, and what is **histone H1**?
![The exit points of the eight N-terminal histone tails from a nucleosome, and the same tails reaching across to bind neighbouring nucleosomes](assets/img/fig-4-29.png)
A: One important factor is nucleosome-to-nucleosome attraction through the histone tails, the H4 tail most notably, which reach out from one nucleosome and contact the next. The other is histone H1, the linker histone, which is often present at a ratio of one per nucleosome core. H1 is larger than the individual core histones and has been considerably less well conserved during evolution. A nucleosome binds a single H1 molecule, which contacts both the DNA and the histone octamer and changes the path the DNA takes as it exits the nucleosome, and that change in exit path is thought to help compact nucleosomal DNA.

Q+: Where does H1 sit relative to the octamer?

Q: What is **epigenetic inheritance**, and why does chromatin structure count as a case of it?
A: It is inheritance superimposed on the DNA-based kind: a cell memory passed from a cell to its descendants that does not rest on any change in DNA sequence. The prefix *epi* is Greek for "on", which is the point. Chromatin qualifies because some types of chromatin structure can be directly passed down at cell division, so a daughter cell inherits not only its parent's sequence but the parent's decision about which parts of that sequence are packaged shut. The chapter restricts itself to the chromatin-based mechanisms and leaves the several other routes to epigenetic inheritance to Chapter 7.
Tags: chromatin, regulation, core

Q+: By mass, what is a chromosome made of, and why does the answer matter for epigenetics?
A: Roughly one-third DNA, one-third histones and one-third non-histone proteins. The last third is the interesting one: it is not a single species but an enormous number of different proteins, and it is where the mechanism of chromatin-based inheritance lives. Covalent modifications of the histones serve as recognition sites for protein domains that recruit different non-histone complexes to different regions of chromatin, so the mark is a small chemical change and the consequence is a large change in which proteins are sitting there. That is why a chromosome is two-thirds protein by mass and only one-third of that protein is packaging.

Q: How is the human genome divided between open and closed chromatin?
![The division of mammalian chromatin into euchromatin and heterochromatin, with percentages for accessible promoters and active genes, weakly transcribed genes, quiescent chromatin, facultative heterochromatin and constitutive heterochromatin](assets/img/fig-4-31.png)
A: Roughly 80% of the human genome is in the closed, inactive form and only about 20% is open and active. Within the closed 80%, half is compactly packaged as heterochromatin and the other half is less condensed and designated quiescent, meaning unmarked and bound by linker histone. Breaking it down further: about 8% is highly accessible promoters, enhancers and active genes, about 12% is weakly transcribed genes, about 40% is quiescent, and the remaining 40% is heterochromatin split evenly between the facultative and constitutive kinds. The old light-microscope distinction from the 1930s, condensed heterochromatin against everything else, survives but has been replaced in practice by the open-and-active against closed-and-inactive division.

Q: What is **heterochromatin**, and what is the difference between the constitutive and facultative kinds?
A: Heterochromatin is an especially compact form of chromatin whose packaging typically prevents gene expression. Some of it is concentrated at specialized regions, notably centromeres and telomeres, but it also occurs at many other places along chromosomes, and those places can vary with the developmental state of the cell. Constitutive heterochromatin permanently condenses many regions of the genome, which is where the name comes from; facultative heterochromatin can be regulated, and is used to control gene expression. The distinction has softened with better data, since some forms of the supposedly constitutive class turn out to be reversible and used for regulation too. The critical property both share is the ability to self-propagate.

Q: What is a **position effect**, and what happens to a normal gene that gets moved next to heterochromatin?
![A euchromatic region normally protected by a barrier sequence, and the same region after a chromosome rearrangement, with heterochromatin spreading different distances in different embryonic cells and each pattern being inherited by that cell's progeny](assets/img/fig-4-32.png)
A: It gets silenced. Errors in rejoining chromosomes after double-strand breaks can translocate a normally euchromatic piece of chromosome into the neighbourhood of heterochromatin, and this often inactivates genes that were previously active. The cause is that the heterochromatic state spreads into the originally euchromatic region. It was first recognized in *Drosophila* and has since been seen in yeasts, plants and humans. What makes it so informative is what happens next: in the early embryo the zone of silencing spreads for different distances in different cells, then stops, and thereafter each cell's pattern is stably inherited by all its progeny for the rest of the animal's life.

Q+: That inheritance has a visible consequence in the fly. What is **position effect variegation**?
![Fly eyes: a red wild-type eye, a white mutant eye, and a mottled eye with red and white patches where the White gene has been moved near heterochromatin](assets/img/fig-4-33.png)
A: A mottled eye. The *White* gene controls eye pigment and is named for the mutation that identified it, so wild-type flies have red eyes and *White* mutants have white ones. Move a perfectly normal *White* gene next to a region of heterochromatin and the eye comes out patched, red and white. Each white patch is a clone descended from one embryonic cell in which the spreading heterochromatin reached the gene and shut it off; each red patch is a clone descended from a cell in which it did not. The patches are large, which is the real result: a decision taken once in an ancestral cell about how to package one gene was inherited by every one of its daughters, with no change in the DNA sequence anywhere.

Q+: How were the molecules behind this found, and how many are there?
A: By genetic screens, generating large numbers of mutants and picking out those with an abnormality in the process. Screens in *Drosophila*, fungi and mice have identified more than 100 genes whose products either enhance or suppress the spread of heterochromatin and its stable inheritance, which is why they are named as enhancers or suppressors of position effect variegation. Many code for non-histone chromosomal proteins that interact with histones and modify or maintain chromatin structure, including the enzymes that add and remove covalent modifications on histone side chains. A hundred genes for one phenomenon is a fair warning about how much of the mechanism is still undescribed: the chapter says outright that nearly all of the details remain to be deciphered.

Q+: A fly carrying a normal *White* gene next to heterochromatin has an eye of large red and white patches rather than a fine pink speckle. What does the patch size tell you?
- [x] The packaging decision was taken early in development and inherited by every descendant of that cell
- [ ] The *White* gene mutates at a high rate in cells near heterochromatin
- [ ] Pigment diffuses between neighbouring cells, averaging out fine differences
- [ ] Heterochromatin spreads a fixed distance in every cell, so all cells of one lineage match by chance
A: That the state is heritable. If each cell decided independently whether to silence the gene, red and white cells would be interleaved at random and the eye would look uniformly pink at any distance. Large single-coloured patches mean one embryonic cell made the decision and all of its progeny kept it, so the size of a patch dates the decision: the bigger the patch, the earlier in development its founder cell lived. The spreading distance does vary from cell to cell, which is what makes some patches red and others white, but the variation is not the point; the inheritance is. No mutation is involved, since the DNA sequence of the *White* gene is unchanged throughout.

Q: What kinds of covalent modification are made to the core histones, and where?
![Lysine acetylation and the mono-, di- and trimethylated forms of lysine drawn as competing reactions, and serine phosphorylation](assets/img/fig-4-34.png)
A: The prominent ones are acetylation of lysines, mono-, di- and trimethylation of lysines, and phosphorylation of serines. Others include mono- or dimethylation of an arginine, phosphorylation of a threonine, addition of ADP-ribose to a glutamic acid, and the addition of a ubiquityl, sumoyl or biotin group to a lysine. Many of these occur on the eight unstructured N-terminal tails that protrude from the nucleosome, but there are also more than 20 specific modifications on the globular core itself. Two chemical points from the figure carry a lot of weight: acetylation removes the positive charge on a lysine, and an acetylated lysine cannot be methylated or the reverse, so the two modifications compete for the same side chain.

Q+: How is a histone mark written down, and what does H3K9me3 mean?
![The nucleosome with the first 30 amino acids of each of its eight N-terminal tails highlighted, and the sequences of the four core histone tails annotated with acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation and ubiquitylation sites](assets/img/fig-4-35.png)
A: First the histone, written H3, H4, H2A or H2B. Then the marked amino acid in its one-letter abbreviation followed by its distance from that histone's amino terminus, giving H3K9 for lysine 9 of histone H3. Then the modification: ac for acetylated, me2 for dimethylated, me3 for trimethylated. So H3K9me3 is histone H3 trimethylated on lysine 9. The notation is compact enough to hide an important fact, which is that the different methylation levels are genuinely different marks: mono-, di- and trimethyl lysine can be recognized by different binding proteins and can mean different things to the cell.
Tags: histones, chromatin

Q+: Who adds these marks and who takes them off?
A: All the modifications are reversible, and in each case one enzyme creates the mark and a different enzyme removes it. Acetyl groups go on specific lysines through a set of histone acetyl transferases (HATs) and come off through histone deacetylase complexes (HDACs); methyl groups go on through histone methyl transferases and come off through histone demethylases. The enzymes are highly specific, and each is recruited to particular sites at defined times in a cell's history. That initial recruitment usually depends on transcription regulatory proteins, which bind specific DNA sequences and are themselves produced at particular times and places, so the DNA sequence ultimately determines how the histones get modified. The interesting part is what happens afterwards: the modifications in heterochromatin can persist long after the regulatory proteins that induced them have gone, which is what gives the cell a memory of its own developmental history.
Tags: histones, enzymes, regulation

---

Q: What does a histone modification actually do, mechanically?
A: Two things, and the second matters more. Acetylating lysines on the N-terminal tails loosens chromatin structure directly, partly because adding an acetyl group removes the lysine's positive charge and so weakens the electrostatic grip on the DNA backbone. But the most profound effects come from recruitment: a mark is a binding site, and its main consequence is which other proteins it attracts to that stretch of chromatin. Trimethylation of one specific lysine on the H3 tail attracts the protein HP1 and promotes one type of heterochromatin, while trimethylation of a different lysine attracts a different protein and forms a second type. The chemistry of the mark is almost incidental; what it means is decided by whichever protein reads it.

Q: What is a **reader** module, and what does the ING PHD domain recognize?
![An ING PHD domain bound to a histone H3 tail, with the trimethyl group on lysine 4 highlighted and hydrogen bonds to the first six amino acids of the tail shown](assets/img/fig-4-37.png)
A: A reader is a small protein module that binds tightly to a specific mark. The ING PHD domain is the worked example: it recognizes histone H3 trimethylated on lysine 4, gripping the trimethyl group in a pocket while hydrogen bonds read the first six amino acids of the tail. It belongs to a family of PHD domains that recognize methylated lysines, whose members bind lysines at different positions and can discriminate mono- from di- from trimethylated. Other small modules do the same job for acetyl groups, phosphate groups and so on. So reading a mark means reading both the modification and its sequence context.

Q+: A single mark is a weak signal. How does a cell read a *combination*?
![A reader complex built from several modules, each recognizing a different histone mark, binding only where the right combination is present and attracting further protein complexes](assets/img/fig-4-38.png)
A: By linking the modules together in one large complex. A reader protein complex carries a series of modules, each recognizing a specific mark, so it binds tightly only to a region of chromatin that carries several of the marks it recognizes. That turns a set of individually ambiguous modifications into a specific address, and only chromatin with the right combination attracts the further complexes needed to catalyze the biological function. Reader modules are often built into large complexes such as ATP-dependent remodelers, so the recognition and the action sit in the same machine. The histone modifications are known to occur in coordinated sets, more than 15 of which can be identified in mammalian cells, which is the observation behind the idea of a "histone code", though the chapter is careful to say it is not yet clear how many of those combinations are functionally important.

Q: What is a **histone variant**, and how does it get into chromatin?
![H3, H3.3, CENP-A, H2A, H2AX, H2AZ and macroH2A drawn against the major histone each replaces, with their differing regions and special functions marked](assets/img/fig-4-36.png)
A: A variant is an alternative version of a core histone, present in much smaller amounts than the major histones and less well conserved over evolutionary time. Variants exist for each core histone except H4. They get in differently from the major histones: the major histones are made mostly during S phase and assembled onto the daughter helices just behind the replication fork, whereas most variants are made throughout interphase and inserted into chromatin that already exists. That insertion requires a histone-exchange reaction catalyzed by a special ATP-dependent remodeling complex that binds both the incoming variant and the nucleosome containing the histone to be swapped out, and subunits of the complex direct it to particular sites, so each variant ends up in chromatin in a highly selective way.

Q+: Name a function for each of the variants in Figure 4-36.
A: H3.3 goes with transcriptional activation. CENP-A replaces H3 at centromeres, where it is needed for centromere function and kinetochore assembly, and it carries a distinctive loop insert. H2AX is used in DNA repair and recombination. H2AZ works in gene expression and chromosome segregation. macroH2A, whose extra domain roughly doubles its length, goes with transcriptional repression and X-chromosome inactivation. The pattern worth noticing is that variants mark chromosomal locations that need to be treated as special cases: the point on the chromosome that grabs the spindle, the site of a break, the chromosome that has to be shut down wholesale.

Q: How does a chromatin modification spread along a chromosome instead of staying where it was written?
![A reader-writer complex recruited to a site, marking neighbouring nucleosomes and moving hand over hand until it reaches a barrier DNA sequence, and a reader-eraser complex removing the marks again](assets/img/fig-4-39.png)
A: Through a read-write cycle that works like a chain reaction. A writer enzyme is brought to a particular region by a sequence-specific DNA-binding protein and marks one or a few nucleosomes. In the same complex sits a reader protein whose module recognizes exactly the mark the writer produces. The reader binds the newly modified nucleosome, and that binding allosterically activates the attached writer and positions it near the next nucleosome along, which then gets marked too. Repeating that carries the complex hand over hand down the DNA, spreading the mark. The requirement that makes it self-sustaining is the loop: the reader must recognize the same mark the writer produces, and binding it must activate the writer.
Tags: heterochromatin, chromatin, core

---

Q+: The same architecture works in reverse. What does a reader-eraser complex do?
A: It removes a modification progressively along a chromosome instead of adding one. An eraser enzyme, a histone demethylase or a histone deacetylase, is recruited into the complex, and the read-erase cycle spreads the removal the same hand-over-hand way. In practice both directions are more complicated than the schemes suggest: readers and writers sit in complexes that may contain several readers, writers and erasers at once and may require multiple marks on a nucleosome before they will spread, and many of these complexes also carry an ATP-dependent remodeling protein, so reading, writing and physical repackaging happen together as the complex moves along.

Q: The two major classes of mammalian heterochromatin are built on two different marks. What are they and what is each for?
![Reader-writer complexes for H3K27me3 and H3K9me3 heterochromatin, the PRC2 complex bridging two nucleosomes, and an HP1 dimer bridging two nucleosomes through its two binding sites](assets/img/fig-4-40.png)
A: One is centred on trimethylation of H3K27 and the other on trimethylation of H3K9. The H3K27me3 class is made by the polycomb repressive complex, first discovered in *Drosophila*, in which a PRC1 complex creates the initial mark and PRC2 spreads it; because it is highly regulated it is the facultative class. The H3K9me3 class has a structural role in forming the centromere and silences a variety of selfish DNA elements, and it also blocks the frequent genetic recombination that would otherwise happen between the highly repeated sequences in the genome. It was long thought to be permanent in every cell, hence "constitutive", but some of its forms are reversible and are used to regulate expression, for instance to shut down genes that were active early in embryonic development once they are no longer needed. Roughly 40% of the human genome is heterochromatin, with H3K9me3 to H3K27me3 in a ratio of about 2 to 1.

Q: A chromosome is one continuous DNA molecule, so what stops a spreading chromatin state from running the length of it?
![Three barrier mechanisms: tethering chromatin to a nuclear pore complex, a barrier protein bound tightly to a group of nucleosomes, and a barrier that recruits enzymes to erase the marks heterochromatin needs](assets/img/fig-4-41.png)
A: Barrier DNA sequences, which mark the boundaries of chromatin domains and separate one from the next. Early studies of position effect variegation pointed to their existence, and several have since been identified and characterized by deleting specific DNA segments from chromosomes or inserting them. Three mechanisms are known and a combination may operate at any one site. A region of chromatin can be tethered to a large fixed structure such as a nuclear pore complex, which physically stops the spread. Barrier proteins can bind a group of nucleosomes tightly enough to make that chromatin resistant. Or the barrier can recruit highly active histone-modifying enzymes that erase the marks heterochromatin needs in order to spread.

Q+: One characterized barrier is a cluster of binding sites for histone acetylase enzymes. Why is that an effective barrier?
- [x] An acetylated lysine cannot also be methylated, so acetylating H3K9 blocks the H3K9 methylation that HP1 requires
- [ ] Acetylation adds a negative charge that repels the incoming heterochromatin proteins
- [ ] Acetylases physically outcompete the reader-writer complex for space on the nucleosome
- [ ] Acetylation destroys the histone tail, leaving nothing for a reader to bind

Methylation of lysine 9 on histone H3 is what HP1 reads, and it is what the reader-writer complex propagates; a potent acetylation of that same lysine makes methylation impossible there, so the spreading wave arrives at a stretch of nucleosomes it cannot mark and stops. Q: What is special about the chromatin at a **centromere**?
![The yeast centromeric DNA sequence assembling a single nucleosome containing the CENP-A variant of histone H3, and the kinetochore built on it capturing one microtubule](assets/img/fig-4-42.png)
A: It contains a centromere-specific variant of histone H3, called CENP-A, plus additional proteins that pack the nucleosomes into particularly dense arrangements and build the kinetochore. In many complex organisms including humans this centromeric chromatin persists throughout interphase, even though the attachment to the spindle only matters during mitosis, so the site is permanently marked rather than assembled on demand. In *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* a specific DNA sequence of about 125 nucleotide pairs is enough to serve as a centromere, and despite that tiny size more than a dozen different proteins assemble on it, including the CENP-A that forms a centromere-specific nucleosome with the other three core histones. The yeast kinetochore is unusual in capturing only a single microtubule; a human kinetochore captures 20 or more.

Q: Human centromeres extend over several million nucleotide pairs of alpha satellite DNA. Does that sequence define the centromere?
![Alpha satellite repeats at an active centromere with pericentric heterochromatin, an inactive centromere whose alpha satellite is nonfunctional, and a neocentromere formed on DNA with no alpha satellite at all](assets/img/fig-4-43.png)
A: No, and three observations say so. The same alpha satellite repeats, a 171-nucleotide-pair monomer tandemly repeated many thousands of times, are also found at non-centromeric positions on chromosomes, so having the sequence is not sufficient. Some human chromosomes carry two blocks of alpha satellite DNA, each of which presumably worked as a centromere in the chromosome it came from, and only one of them is active. And most strikingly, neocentromeres have been observed to form spontaneously on fragmented chromosomes at positions that were originally euchromatic and lack alpha satellite DNA altogether. So a centromere in a complex organism is defined by an assembly of proteins, essentially a set of CENP-A nucleosomes, rather than by a sequence, and once that special chromatin exists it is faithfully inherited when the chromosome replicates.

Q+: Roughly how often do extra chromosomes with new centromeres turn up in human births?
A: In about 1 in 2000 births, extra chromosomes are observed in the offspring's cells. Some of these have formed from a breakage event and carry alpha satellite sequences that have been co-opted into new centromeres; others lack alpha satellite entirely and made a centromere out of what was originally euchromatic DNA. A fragment with no centromere would simply be lost at the next division, so any extra chromosome that persists has necessarily acquired one, which is why these cases are where neocentromere formation gets caught happening.

Q+: If no sequence defines a human centromere, how does a new one get started in the right place and nowhere else?
A: By seeding followed by cooperative spreading. De novo centromere formation is proposed to require an initial seeding event, after which a specialized DNA-protein structure containing CENP-A nucleosomes assembles around it. In humans that seeding happens more readily on arrays of alpha satellite DNA than on other sequences, which is why alpha satellite is usually where centromeres are without being what defines them. The whole centromere then forms as an all-or-none entity, which is the signature of a highly cooperative process spreading out from a single seed rather than of many independent local decisions. All-or-none behaviour is also what a chromosome needs, since a partial centromere would be worse than none.

Q: Why must chromosome evolution involve inactivating centromeres as well as creating them?
A: Because stable inheritance requires exactly one centromere per chromosome, and rearrangement does not respect that. Closely related species often have very different chromosome numbers, and genome comparisons show that many of those changes came from chromosome breakage-and-rejoining events. A fusion of two chromosomes produces a molecule with two centromeres, which will be pulled toward both spindle poles at once and torn or lost; a fragment that inherits no centromere is not segregated at all. So for any rearranged chromosome set to have been stably maintained, surplus centromeres must have been inactivated and missing ones created de novo. The plasticity of centromere formation is not an oddity at the margins, it is the thing that made karyotype evolution survivable.
Tags: centromeres, genome-evolution

Q: How is a form of chromatin physically passed to both daughter cells at a replication fork?
![Modified nucleosomes in heterochromatin being split between the two daughter helices at chromosome duplication, reader-writer complexes marking the new unmarked nucleosomes, and heterochromatin-specific proteins reassembling on both daughters](assets/img/fig-4-44.png)
A: By splitting the old histones between the two daughters and letting reader-writer complexes fill in the rest. Experiments show that the H3-H4 tetramer from each nucleosome on the parent helix is directly inherited, partitioned equally between the two sister helices at the fork. Each half-old nucleosome is quickly completed by adding two H2A-H2B dimers, and new octamers are deposited to restore the normal density. Since the H3-H4 tetramer is where the marks live, each daughter helix inherits a diluted but correct pattern of marked nucleosomes. Reader-writer complexes then spread the same mark to the new unmarked neighbours, the original pattern is reestablished, and that recreates the binding sites for the heterochromatin-specific non-histone proteins. The same scheme accounts for centromeric chromatin: assume only that CENP-A in an inherited nucleosome selectively recruits more CENP-A to its new neighbours.
Tags: heterochromatin, chromatin, core

Q+: Does this inheritance mechanism apply to open chromatin as well?
A: It is thought not to. The propagation scheme is described for the H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 classes of heterochromatin and for centromeric chromatin, and explicitly not for the open structure of chromatin at active genes. That asymmetry makes sense of what epigenetic chromatin memory is for: what has to be remembered across divisions is which parts of the genome are shut, since an active gene is being held open by the regulatory proteins currently bound to it and does not need a separate memory. In vertebrates, but not in flies, the inheritance of heterochromatin is often reinforced by a second and independent system, the inherited pattern of methylated C nucleotides in the DNA itself.

Q: A single amino acid change in one histone can cause cancer. How is that possible when every histone is encoded by many gene copies?
A: It has to be dominant, and that is the interesting part. Histones are so abundant that each is encoded by multiple copies of its gene, so a mutation in one copy changes less than 10% of the molecules; any effect it produces must therefore override a large excess of normal histone. Such oncohistone mutations are present in about 4% of all tumours and in a few cancers are the predominant driver. The clearest case is H3K27M, which changes lysine 27 of histone H3 to a methionine and is almost universally present in diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma, a lethal paediatric brain tumour, while turning up occasionally in adult acute myeloid leukaemias and melanomas. It works by binding abnormally tightly to the PRC2 complex, which alters the overall pattern and level of H3K27me3 across the whole genome. So one mutant histone in ten poisons the enzyme that would have marked the other nine.
Tags: histones, cancer, heterochromatin

Q+: Oncohistones are not the only chromatin route into cancer. What are the others?
A: Mutations that alter the readers, writers or erasers of histone marks, and mutations that alter a chromatin-remodeling complex. Since changes in chromatin change gene expression, and cancer arises from a series of inherited changes in a cell's control systems accumulating in a clone, the chapter treats this as unsurprising. The genuinely surprising result was the histone itself, because a structural protein present in tens of millions of copies is not where anyone would look for a dominant cancer driver.

Q: A human chromosome packaged only into nucleosomes would still span the nucleus thousands of times. What is the next level of folding?
A: Folding of each chromosome into a series of large loops, catalyzed by ring-shaped SMC protein complexes, where SMC stands for structural maintenance of chromosomes. This applies to interphase chromosomes, not only mitotic ones: the nucleosome level of packing shortens the DNA only about threefold, so even a decondensed interphase chromosome needs a higher-order organization simply to fit. The chapter approaches this by first looking at two unusual chromosomes that can be seen directly in the light microscope, on the argument that exceptional cases reveal features shared with ordinary chromosomes that are normally invisible.

Q: What are **lampbrush chromosomes**, and what do their loops correspond to?
![Light micrograph of lampbrush chromosomes from an amphibian oocyte, with loops projecting from a linear axis](assets/img/fig-4-45.png)
A: They are the enormously extended, stiff chromosomes of growing amphibian oocytes, the largest chromosomes known, first described in 1878. Early in oocyte differentiation each chromosome replicates to begin meiosis and the homologous replicated chromosomes pair, so a lampbrush chromosome is two aligned sets of paired sister chromatids. Thousands of large chromatin loops emanate from a linear chromosomal axis. Each loop holds a particular DNA sequence and stays extended for as long as the oocyte grows, which can be months or years, while the cell builds up the supply of material it will need to develop into a new individual. The loops are the transcription: they are producing huge amounts of RNA for storage in the egg.

Q+: How is the DNA divided between the axis and the loops, and what makes the loops stand out so stiffly?
![A model of a small portion of a lampbrush sister-chromatid pair, with condensed chromatin near the chromatid axis and transcriptionally active loops extending from it, and micrographs staining active and inactive regions](assets/img/fig-4-46.png)
A: Most of the DNA sits near the junction of the two chromatids, highly condensed and transcriptionally inert, and its job is to organize each chromatid along a linear axis. The highly transcribed regions are the ones that extend from the axis as loops, ranging from tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of nucleotide pairs each. The loops are stiff and extended because they are being transcribed unusually heavily, with RNA polymerases spaced only about 100 nucleotide pairs apart, so the loop is coated in polymerases and growing transcripts. The great majority of the DNA nevertheless remains condensed at the axis, which is worth holding onto: the visible loops are the exceptional minority.

Q+: Do mammals make lampbrush chromosomes?
A: Not in their own oocytes. Meiotic chromosomes adopt the lampbrush state in the growing oocytes of all vertebrates except mammals. But human sperm chromosomes incubated in amphibian oocyte cytoplasm will form lampbrush chromosomes, which is the more interesting result: the capacity is there in mammalian DNA and it is the surrounding cytoplasm that decides whether it is used. Chromosomes are highly dynamic structures that restructure according to their environment rather than fixed objects with one native conformation.

Q: What is a **polytene chromosome**, and how does one arise?
![The complete set of polytene chromosomes from one Drosophila salivary cell squashed on a slide, with the four chromosome pairs joined at a chromocenter and normal mitotic chromosomes drawn to the same scale](assets/img/fig-4-47.png)
A: It is a giant chromosome made of hundreds or thousands of copies of the same DNA molecule aligned side by side in exact register, like drinking straws in a box. Some specialized cells in many organisms grow abnormally large by going through multiple cycles of DNA synthesis without cell division, which makes them polyploid; in the salivary glands of fly larvae this is taken to an extreme. In *Drosophila* the four chromosomes are additionally paired tightly with their homologs, which is not true in most nuclei, so each pair appears as a single cable many DNA strands thick. The four polytene chromosomes are normally joined near their centromeres by heterochromatic regions that aggregate into a single large chromocenter. Q+: What are the **bands** and **interbands**, and how much DNA is in each?
![Light micrograph of part of a polytene chromosome with dark bands of concentrated DNA, and an electron micrograph showing bands of different thickness separated by less condensed interbands](assets/img/fig-4-48.png)
A: Bands are the dark stripes visible in the light microscope, each formed from a thousand or more identical DNA sequences lying side by side in register; interbands are the lighter regions between them. About 95% of the DNA in a polytene chromosome is in bands and 5% in interbands. A very thin band can hold 3000 nucleotide pairs while a thick one may hold 200,000 in each of its chromatin strands. Bands look dark because the DNA in them is more condensed than in interbands, and they may also carry a higher concentration of proteins. Q+: What did mapping histone modifications along *Drosophila* polytene chromosomes reveal about chromatin types?

Q: What is a **chromosome puff**, and what does it show?
![Autoradiograph of a decondensed puff in a Chironomus polytene chromosome labeled with tritiated uridine, with silver grains marking RNA synthesis](assets/img/fig-4-49.png)
A: A puff is a swollen, decondensed region of a polytene chromosome, and it is a gene being transcribed. As an organism with polytene chromosomes moves from one developmental stage to the next, new puffs arise and old ones recede as genes are switched on and off. Inspecting a puff while it is still small enough to leave the banding pattern discernible shows that most puffs arise from the decondensation of a single band. Q+: The thyroglobulin gene makes the same point at the scale of a single gene. What happens to it?

Q+: Why is it useful for a piece of chromosome to leave its own territory?
A: Because the interior of the nucleus is heterogeneous, with functionally different regions produced by biomolecular condensates specialized to speed particular processes: RNA synthesis, RNA splicing, DNA replication. The nucleolus, specialized for producing ribosomes, and the nuclear speckles involved in RNA production are examples. A gene that can move out of its territory can move into the condensate that will accelerate the reactions it needs, so the mobility of a loop is what makes the compartmentalization usable.

Q: How does **Hi-C** work?
![The Hi-C protocol: formaldehyde cross-linking, restriction digestion, biotin marking of ends, dilute ligation, purification on streptavidin, then PCR and sequencing](assets/img/fig-4-53.png)
A: It measures how often any two genomic loci are held close together in the cell, by turning proximity into a sequence. Cells are treated with a cross-linking agent such as formaldehyde to make covalent DNA-protein-DNA links. A restriction nuclease then chops the DNA at defined sequences, leaving identical cohesive ends, and those ends are marked by incorporating biotinylated nucleotides. The critical step is that the DNA is diluted before ligation, so that only fragments already held near each other by cross-linking are likely to be joined; any two ends could be ligated in principle, and dilution is what makes the joins report on the cell rather than on the tube. The cross-links are then reversed, biotin at unligated ends is removed, the ligated fragments are purified on streptavidin beads, amplified by PCR and sequenced. A product exists only if proteins held those two sequences close together inside the cell.
Tags: chromosomes, chromatin

Q+: What is a **topologically associated domain**, and how does one appear in a Hi-C plot?
![A Hi-C contact map for part of human chromosome 8, with a strong diagonal, a large square marking a TAD, corner dots at the base of a loop, and a nested bouquet of subdomains](assets/img/fig-4-54.png)
A: A TAD is a stretch of chromosome within which any two DNA-binding proteins are much more likely to encounter each other than to encounter anything outside it. In a contact map, where the colour at position (x, y) is how often a sequence at x was ligated to a sequence at y, a TAD shows up as a square of elevated contact along the diagonal. The strong stripe on the diagonal itself is trivial, since sequences close together on the linear chromosome are bound to interact. The informative features are the squares, and the sharp dots at their corners, which are the unusually persistent contacts between the sequences at the base of a loop. The chapter's example loop contains about a million nucleotide pairs, and the boxed region shows a nested TAD, read as a bouquet of smaller subdomains gathered into a larger folding domain.

Q+: How big is a typical loop, and how many are there in the human genome?
A: A typical loop in a human chromosome holds between 50,000 and 200,000 nucleotide pairs, loops of a million nucleotide pairs also exist, and there appear to be roughly 10,000 loops in the genome. The conclusion drawn from the data is that all interphase chromosomes are organized as a long linear series of folded looped domains, with the DNA in each loop compacted but highly mobile. Since one DNA segment inside a TAD can be ligated to many others in the same TAD, the chromatin inside a loop must be folded so that any sequence within it can frequently reach any other.

Q+: On a Hi-C contact map, two sharp dots sit at the corners of a square. What are you looking at?
- [x] The two sequences at the base of a loop, held together far more persistently than anything else in the domain
- [ ] Two genes that are transcribed at the same time in the same nucleus
- [ ] The boundaries of a chromosome territory, where contacts with other chromosomes stop
- [ ] Two restriction sites that happened to be cut and religated more efficiently than their neighbours
A: The base of a loop. The square itself is a topologically associated domain, a stretch within which every sequence contacts every other more often than it contacts anything outside. The corner dots are sharper and darker than the body of the square because the two sequences at the base of a loop are not merely in the same neighbourhood, they are physically held against each other by the cohesin ring and the CTCF complexes that stopped it. Q: What is an **SMC protein complex**, and what is its architecture?
![An SMC protein folded into an antiparallel coiled coil with a globular ATPase head, two of them paired into a ring with a hinge at one end, plus the extra subunits that make cohesin or condensin, drawn beside a nucleosome for scale](assets/img/fig-4-55.png)
A: SMC stands for structural maintenance of chromosomes, and the complex is a large protein ring that binds and encircles the DNA double helix. The core is a protein of 1000 to 1500 amino acids that folds back on itself into a long antiparallel coiled coil with a globular head; two of these pair to make a ring with a flexible hinge at one end and ATP-binding domains at the other. The ring is large enough for DNA packaged in chromatin to pass through it, which the figure makes concrete by drawing it beside a nucleosome. Additional subunits then convert the core into either cohesin or condensin. Powered by its two ATPase domains, the ring can motor rapidly along DNA, and these complexes work similarly in archaea, bacteria and eukaryotes.

Q+: What do these rings do in a bacterium, and how do they move?
![SMC complexes loaded near the origin of the circular Bacillus subtilis chromosome and travelling to the terminus, separating the two daughter molecules, with an inchworm model of how the two ATPase heads step along DNA](assets/img/fig-4-56.png)
A: They separate the daughter chromosomes. The bacterial chromosome is one circular molecule replicated from a single origin, and a DNA sequence next to that origin binds a protein, ParB in *Bacillus subtilis*, which repeatedly loads SMC complexes. Those rings create a loop on each new double helix and then travel continuously along the DNA, using repeated cycles of ATP hydrolysis, all the way to the point where the two replication forks met. Extruding the two daughter molecules into separate loops in this way partitions them cleanly so that each can go to a different daughter cell. How the ring actually moves is still uncertain: the inchworm model has the two ATPase domains separating and rejoining in sequence to walk along the DNA, and alternatives exist.

Q: How does **cohesin** organize an interphase chromosome, and what stops it?
![Cohesin loaded onto chromatin creating a small loop, enlarging it through cycles of ATP hydrolysis until it meets a CTCF complex on each side, and a stretch of interphase chromosome drawn as a series of looped domains](assets/img/fig-4-57.png)
A: Cohesin is the SMC complex that matters during interphase, and it works much like its bacterial counterpart except that its rings are loaded at many sites along a chromosome rather than at one. A ring is loaded to form a small DNA loop and then, driven by ATP hydrolysis, continually enlarges that loop by reeling DNA through itself. It stops when it meets a DNA-bound CTCF complex on each side, and the CTCF dimer complex then becomes the base of the loop. Because CTCF binds specific sequences, loops form at specific places, which is what turns a general loop-extruding activity into a reproducible map of domains. CTCF is one of the insulator proteins of Chapter 7 and can also form part of a barrier that stops reader-writer complexes spreading chromatin structures.

Q+: The looped-domain model agrees with the old lampbrush and polytene pictures. Where does it disagree?
A: On stability. In lampbrush and polytene chromosomes the loops are fixed features that persist for months, and the model derived for vertebrate interphase chromosomes has the same shape, which the chapter finds satisfying. But methods that determine the organization of interphase chromosomes in single cells show that most of the loops in a typical interphase chromosome are unstable: they form in favoured locations, but their positions fluctuate rapidly, and loops form within loops. Averaged over a population of cells a Hi-C map looks like a fixed architecture, while any individual cell at any instant has a different set of loops. A typical interphase chromosome is therefore highly dynamic, and the special chromosomes that made the structure visible are also the least representative of it.

Q: How are euchromatin and heterochromatin arranged in space inside the nucleus?
![Antibody staining of a mouse fibroblast nucleus for H3K4me3, H3K27me3 and H3K9me3, and polymer models of a normal nucleus and an inverted one lacking lamina-heterochromatin interactions](assets/img/fig-4-58.png)

Hi-C sees the same thing independently: every chromosome can be subdivided into two compartments whose loci preferentially contact each other and avoid the other compartment, whether on the same chromosome or a different one, and those two compartments match euchromatin and heterochromatin in gene richness, gene expression and replication timing. Q+: What holds heterochromatin together, and where does it get anchored?
![HP1-associated heterochromatin deforming nucleosomes, HP1-HP1 and histone-histone associations, and the resulting phase-separated heterochromatin](assets/img/fig-4-59.png)
A: Many fluctuating weak interactions, in something that resembles phase separation. Q+: There is one known exception to this nuclear arrangement. What is it, and why is it striking?
A: The inverted nuclei of the rod photoreceptor cells of nocturnal mammals, where euchromatin and heterochromatin have swapped places: highly condensed inert chromatin occupies the very centre of the nucleus and active euchromatin is squeezed to the periphery. Despite the inversion the rod nuclei remain fully functional and highly transcriptionally active, which undercuts any strong functional claim for the usual arrangement. And polymer modelling reproduces both the normal and the inverted arrangement from a single change, the absence of lamina-heterochromatin interactions, assuming only that chromatin-chromatin attraction is strongest between H3K9me3 heterochromatin, weaker between the H3K27me3 kind, and absent between euchromatin. Q: What happens to chromosomes as a cell enters M phase?
![Cohesin loops lost within minutes of entering mitosis, condensin II forming large new loops radially around a central axis, and condensin I forming a second set of loops inside them](assets/img/fig-4-61.png)
A: Condensation reduces the length of a typical interphase chromosome by about tenfold. It begins in early M phase, when gene expression shuts down and specific histone modifications are made that may help reorganize the chromatin. The interphase loop organization created by cohesin is lost within minutes, and condensin takes over. Condensin II forms very large new chromatin loops by the same ATP-driven mechanism, organized radially around a central chromosome axis, and as those loops grow, condensin I forms a second set of loops inside them. That loops-within-loops arrangement, combined with an ever-tighter winding of the loops around the axis, gives the compact metaphase chromosome. Topoisomerase II works alongside condensin, and the axis is highly enriched in both condensin II and topoisomerase II; their combined action is believed to be what separates each chromosome into two sister chromatids.

Q: The whole packaging hierarchy, from the naked double helix to the metaphase chromosome. What are the levels and the dimensions?

Q+: What are the two purposes the chapter gives for this final round of condensation?

Q: What are **homologous genes**, and how far can the resemblance be pushed?
A: Genes that are similar in both nucleotide sequence and function because they descend from a common ancestral gene. The revelation of the past forty years has been how far this reaches: unmistakable homologs of many human genes are present in nematode worms, fruit flies, yeasts, plants and even bacteria. In many cases the resemblance is close enough that the protein-coding portion of a yeast gene can be replaced by its human homolog and the yeast still works, despite more than a billion years of separate evolution. Recognizing sequence similarity has become a major tool for inferring function: a match does not guarantee that two proteins do the same job, but it is an excellent clue, and it often lets the function of a human gene be predicted from a characterized gene in an organism that is easier to study.

Q+: Individual genes are much more tightly conserved than genome structure. Which features of genome organization vary freely?
A: Genome size, number of chromosomes, the order of genes along chromosomes, the abundance and size of introns, the amount of repetitive DNA, and the total number of genes. All of these differ greatly between distant organisms while the sequences of individual genes stay recognizable. That split is the theme of this whole section: what selection preserves is the parts list, not the filing system.

Q: Why is comparing genomes across species the most powerful way to find the functional parts of ours?
A: Because function is what stops a sequence drifting. Humans and mice diverged from a common mammalian ancestor about 90 million years ago, long enough for most nucleotides in both genomes to have been changed by random mutation. Any region that has stayed closely similar is a region where mutations impaired function and were removed by natural selection, so conservation is a direct readout of constraint. Those closely similar stretches are called conserved DNA regions, and they include exons, RNA genes, regulatory sequences, and sequences whose function is not yet known but is inferred to exist. Adding more genomes to the comparison, rat and chicken and fish and dog and chimpanzee, sharpens it further, since it is reading the results of a natural experiment that ran for hundreds of millions of years.
Tags: genome-evolution, human-genome, core

---

Q+: What fraction of the human genome does that analysis say actually matters?
A: About 10%, arrived at from two directions. Cross-species comparison identifies about 4.5% of the human genome as multispecies conserved sequence, and to the chapter's stated surprise only about a quarter of that codes for protein; most of the rest is thought to contain clusters of protein-binding sites involved in gene regulation, and some of it produces functional untranslated RNAs. Comparing the DNA of hundreds of thousands of individual humans then shows a further 5% of the genome with reduced variation within our own species, implying that it is constrained too. Adding those gives roughly 10%, which leaves about 90% whose exact sequence is thought to be of little consequence. That is why finding the exons is hard: they are short islands, median about 130 nucleotide pairs, in a sea of sequence where nothing marks the boundaries.

Q: In 2012 the ENCODE project reported that 76% of human DNA is transcribed and concluded there is very little junk. What does the chapter say about that?
A: That the inference does not follow from the observation. ENCODE used assays sensitive enough to detect RNA present at extremely low levels, and many of the transcripts they counted were found at less than a single RNA molecule per cell. The chapter accepts the transcription and rejects the conclusion: the strong scientific consensus is that most of the human genome is sequence whose identity is not relevant to biological function, and the evidence for that is that natural selection demonstrably fails to preserve it, both when species are compared and in analyses of human variation. Occasional RNA from junk DNA is explained as background noise in gene expression, which is very accurate but not perfect, and errors at a low level are expected and thought to have little or no consequence. The claim also came with the assertion that our genome contains tens of thousands of previously undetected noncoding RNA genes, and it received widespread publicity.
Tags: human-genome, genome-evolution

Q+: The disagreement turns on what the word "functional" is being used to mean. State both senses.
A: ENCODE used a biochemical definition: a sequence is functional if something measurable happens at it, such as being transcribed, being bound by a protein, or carrying a particular chromatin mark. The chapter uses a selected-effect definition: a sequence is functional if its exact identity is maintained by natural selection, meaning that changing it costs the organism something. The two come apart badly in a large genome, because transcription and protein binding are not perfectly specific and will happen at low rates on sequence that no one is selecting for. The evolutionary argument is the harder one to dismiss, since a sequence under no constraint accumulates changes at the neutral rate whether or not RNA polymerase occasionally visits it, and about 90% of our genome does exactly that.

Q: How fast do DNA sequences change in the human germ line, and why does that add up to more variation than it sounds like?
A: Along a given line of descent, about one nucleotide pair in a thousand is randomly changed in the human germ line every million years. That sounds glacial, but it has to be multiplied by population size before it means anything: in a population of 10,000 diploid individuals, every possible nucleotide substitution will have been tried out on about 20 occasions in the course of a million years, which is a short span relative to the evolution of species. So the raw material for selection is not scarce even at very high replication fidelity. Evolution, as the chapter puts it, depends on accidents and mistakes followed by nonrandom survival.

Q+: What kinds of genome alteration do those failures produce, and what is the other main source of change?
A: Errors in DNA replication, recombination or repair produce either simple local changes, point mutations such as the substitution of one base pair for another, or large-scale rearrangements: deletions, duplications, inversions and translocations of DNA between chromosomes. The other source is transposable elements, parasitic DNA sequences that spread within the genomes they colonize. In spreading they often disrupt the function or alter the regulation of existing genes, and on occasion they have created entirely novel genes by fusing transposon sequence with segments of existing genes.

Q: What is the human genome actually made of, by sequence class?

Q: Figure 4-63 names LINEs and SINEs and sends you to Chapter 5 for the mechanism. What are they, and which of them can move on its own?
A: A **LINE**, long interspersed nuclear element, is about 6 kb at full length and encodes its own machinery in two open reading frames: ORF1p, an RNA-binding protein that chaperones the transcript, and ORF2p, which carries both an endonuclease and a reverse transcriptase. It is therefore autonomous, and L1 is the only autonomous element still moving in humans. A **SINE**, short interspersed nuclear element, is a few hundred nucleotide pairs, encodes no protein at all, and is non-autonomous: it is transcribed and then captures L1's ORF2p to get itself copied. The human SINE is *Alu*, about 300 nucleotide pairs, derived from the gene for 7SL RNA, the RNA component of the signal recognition particle. A third family, SVA, is hominid-specific, about 2 kb, a composite of several sequence types, and also depends on L1. The numbers behind the figure's slices: L1 at roughly 500,000 copies and 17% of the genome, *Alu* at roughly 1.1 million copies and 11%, SVA at only about 3000 copies.

Q+: Both copy themselves through an RNA intermediate. How does the new copy actually get into the DNA?
A: By **target-primed reverse transcription**. The ORF2p endonuclease nicks one strand at a loose consensus of the form 5′-TTTT/AA-3′, which frees a 3′ hydroxyl at the target site, and that hydroxyl is what primes reverse transcription of the element's own RNA. The copy is therefore written directly into the target site rather than being made elsewhere and then inserted. Three signatures follow from the mechanism and can be read straight off a sequence: the new copy sits between short target site duplications, because the two strands are cut at staggered positions; it ends in a poly(A) tail, inherited from the polyadenylated RNA that was copied; and it is very often truncated at its 5′ end, because reverse transcription frequently fails to run the whole length. That last point explains why so few of the half-million L1 copies are of any use to the element: most arrived already broken.

Q+: If nearly half the genome is transposon relics, how many elements can still move, and how often do they manage it?
A: Very few, and rarely. Q: Do mobile elements actually cause human disease, or are they only a fossil record?
A: They cause disease, and showing that was how the field started. In 1988 Kazazian and colleagues found two unrelated boys with haemophilia A whose factor VIII gene had been interrupted by a *de novo* L1 insertion, which established that retrotransposition is still happening in the human germ line rather than having stopped in the distant past. By 2016 about 124 cases of human genetic disease caused by L1-mediated insertions had been catalogued, with examples from all three active families: L1 into factor VIII, *Alu* into the cystic fibrosis gene, and SVA into the gene behind X-linked dystonia-parkinsonism. That count is small beside the number of disease-causing point mutations, but it is an undercount rather than a fair measure, because an insertion of several kilobases is exactly the kind of variant that standard short-read alignment pipelines drop unless a caller is specifically looking for it.

Q+: There is a second way *Alu* causes disease that has nothing to do with a new element landing anywhere. What is it?
A: Recombination between two *Alu* copies that are already there. With over a million near-identical *Alu* elements scattered through the genome, any two of them in the same neighbourhood present the recombination machinery with a pair of homologous sequences at non-allelic positions, and a crossover between them deletes or duplicates everything in between. This is non-allelic homologous recombination, and the *LDLR* gene is the worked example: familial hypercholesterolaemia is caused variously by a 5 kb deletion removing the exons for the transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains, generated by recombination between two oppositely oriented *Alu* elements, and by a 14 kb duplication of exons 2 to 8 generated between *Alu* elements in introns 1 and 8. Around 10% of molecularly solved familial hypercholesterolaemia is copy-number change of this kind. So the elements do damage twice over: once when they insert, and then indefinitely afterwards by being a dispersed library of repeats that the genome can recombine against.

Q+: Given human and chimpanzee sequences, how do you reconstruct the gene the last common ancestor had?

Q: Human and chimpanzee genes are nearly identical. Is that because selection has held them the same?
A: Mostly no, it is because there has not been time. The evidence is that the two genomes are nearly identical even at positions under no functional constraint, such as the third position of synonymous codons, which specify the same amino acid whatever they carry. If selection were doing the preserving, unconstrained sites would have drifted while constrained ones stayed put, and they have not. Q: What is a **molecular clock**, and why is it really a set of clocks rather than one?
![A portion of the human and mouse leptin genes aligned across one exon and its adjacent intron, with substitutions boxed in green and insertions or deletions boxed in yellow, the exon far better conserved than the intron](assets/img/fig-4-66.png)
A: The observation that changes in a particular gene or protein sequence accumulate at a nearly constant rate, calibrated against absolute dates from radioisotope decay in the rock formations holding fossils. Rates differing from the norm by as much as twofold are seen in particular lineages, so it is an approximation. It is a set of clocks because the rate depends on the constraint. The clock runs fastest and most regularly in sequences under no purifying selection: portions of introns lacking splicing or regulatory signals, third positions of synonymous codons, and pseudogenes that mutation has irreversibly inactivated. It runs slowest for sequences under strong functional constraint, such as histones, which interact specifically with large numbers of other proteins, and the RNA subunits of the ribosome, on which all protein synthesis depends. The leptin alignment shows both clocks side by side in one gene: the exon is well conserved between human and mouse and the adjacent intron is a mess of substitutions and indels.

---

Q+: Which clock do you pick for which question?
A: A fast clock for recent events and a slow one for ancient ones. Mitochondrial DNA has been used to chronicle the divergence of the Neanderthal lineage from that of modern *Homo sapiens*, which works because in animals, though not in plants, clocks based on functionally unconstrained mitochondrial sequence run much faster than nuclear ones, the mutation rate in animal mitochondria being exceptionally high. For the deepest divergences, those between bacteria, archaea and eukaryotes, the sequences specifying ribosomal RNA are used, because they change slowly enough to remain alignable. So the pace of a clock is set by two things together, the degree of purifying selection and the underlying mutation rate.

Q+: Occasionally a previously conserved sequence starts changing quickly. Why is that interesting?
A: Because it is the signature of positive selection rather than of relaxed constraint. A sequence that has been held still for a long time is one where change was costly, so a sudden burst of change in one lineage suggests that in that lineage the mutations conferred an advantage. Those episodes are where the interesting evolutionary events are, and the chapter returns to them later with the human accelerated regions.
Tags: genome-evolution, evolution

Q+: Distinguish **purifying selection** from **positive selection**, and say what each looks like in a sequence comparison.
A: Purifying selection eliminates individuals carrying mutations that interfere with important genetic functions, so it removes change and its signature is conservation: a sequence that has stayed similar across species where the neutral clock says it should have drifted. Positive selection favours mutations that confer an advantage, so it drives change and its signature is the opposite, an unusually rapid burst of substitution in one lineage at a site that had been conserved everywhere else. The two are distinguished by comparison rather than by inspection, since neither leaves a mark you can see in a single sequence. The human accelerated regions are the positive-selection case, and the reason they can be identified at all is that purifying selection had held those same sequences still in every other mammal.

Q+: What do molecular clocks offer that the fossil record does not, and what do they still need it for?
A: They offer finer time resolution and a more reliable guide to the detailed structure of phylogenetic trees than classical methods based on family resemblances in anatomy and embryonic development. The precise family tree of great apes and humans was not settled until enough molecular sequence accumulated in the 1980s. But the fossil record remains essential as the source of absolute dates, from radioisotope decay in the rock formations where each fossil is found, so the clock can say how much change happened while only the fossils can say how long that took. Because the fossil record has many gaps, precise divergence times are hard to establish even for species that leave good fossils.

Q: What is a region of **synteny**, and how differently are the human and mouse genomes arranged?

Q+: Comparing syntenic blocks turned up a surprise about how genome size is maintained. What was it?

Q: DNA is added to genomes two ways. What are they, and how does the β-globin cluster show it?

Q+: What makes a stripped-down genome such as *Fugu*'s useful for studying humans?

Q: Why can a conserved DNA sequence often be deleted from a mouse with no visible effect?
A: Two reasons, and both are about the test rather than the sequence. What a mouse needs to survive in a laboratory cage is very much less than what it needs to succeed in nature, so a sequence that is crucial in the wild can be invisible under laboratory conditions. And the selective advantage needed to preserve a sequence over evolutionary time is tiny: population genetics calculations show that a difference in survival of less than 0.1% is enough to strongly favour retaining a sequence. No phenotyping regime detects a 0.1% difference in survival. So the traditional approach of disrupting a puzzling sequence and looking for consequences is expected to fail on much of the conserved noncoding DNA, and the failure is not evidence that the sequence does nothing.

Q: Most conserved noncoding DNA is not regulatory sequence that anyone has characterized. What is known about it?

Q: What is a **human accelerated region**, and what was found in the fastest-changing one?

Q: When two very different organisms are compared, what is rare and what is common?
A: Genes with no homolog in the other species are rare, even between a mammal and a worm. What is common is gene families with different numbers of members in the two species. So evolution has less often invented a gene from nothing than duplicated one it already had and let the copies diverge into new functions that differ from species to species. That process of duplication followed by divergence is what the chapter offers as the main source of genetic novelty, and it contributes to the vigorous DNA addition discussed earlier.

Q+: How fast does gene duplication actually happen?
A: In a detailed study of spontaneous duplications in yeast, duplications of 50,000 to 250,000 nucleotide pairs were commonly observed, most tandemly repeated, and they appeared to result from replication errors leading to inexact repair of double-strand breaks. In the primate case, comparing human and chimpanzee genomes shows that segmental duplications have added about 5 million nucleotide pairs to each genome every million years since the split, at an average duplication size of about 50,000 nucleotide pairs, with some five times larger. Counted in nucleotides, duplication events have created more differences between humans and chimpanzees than single-nucleotide substitutions have. Across eukaryotic genomes, the probability that any particular gene undergoes a duplication that spreads to most or all individuals of a species is approximately 1% every million years.

Q: What happens to a gene right after it is duplicated, and which outcome is more common?
A: Initially there is presumed to be little or no selection to maintain the duplicated state, because either copy provides an equivalent function. So the likeliest sequel is a loss-of-function mutation in one copy, restoring the one-gene state that preceded the duplication. Contemporary genomes are full of examples: one copy visibly inactivated by multiple mutations, becoming a pseudogene, and as more mutations accumulate the sequence similarity between the pseudogene and its functional parent erodes until the relationship is undetectable. The other outcome is that both copies stay functional while diverging in sequence and in expression pattern, taking on different roles, and that is what produced the large families of related genes found in complex organisms.

Q+: If neither copy is needed at first, why does the second outcome ever happen?
A: The attractive theory is that mildly deleterious mutations occur quickly in both copies, in complementary ways. One copy loses expression in a particular tissue through a regulatory mutation while the other loses expression in a different tissue. From that moment both copies are needed to supply the full range of functions the single gene once provided, so both are protected from inactivation, and over a longer period each is free to acquire new specialized features. The evidence comes from zebrafish, where at least one whole-genome duplication is thought to have occurred hundreds of millions of years ago: many duplicates have been lost by mutation, but perhaps 30 to 50% have diverged functionally with both copies still active, and the most obvious difference between such pairs is usually that they are expressed in different tissues or at different stages of development.

Q+: How does a **whole-genome duplication** happen, and why is it hard to detect afterwards?
A: It requires only one round of genome replication in a germ-line cell lineage without a corresponding cell division, which doubles the chromosome number in a single step. Such abrupt increases in ploidy are common, particularly in fungi and plants, and afterwards every gene exists as a duplicate pair. It is hard to detect because unless the event was recent enough that little has happened since, the outcome of a series of segmental duplications occurring at different times is hard to distinguish from the end product of one whole-genome duplication. In mammals the relative contribution of the two remains quite uncertain, though it is clear that a great deal of gene duplication happened in the distant past.

Q: Trace the evolution of the globin gene family from a single chain to the human clusters.

Q+: Why was a four-chain globin worth evolving at all?

Q+: How is the history of those duplications written into the arrangement of the genes?

Q+: The globin clusters also contain sequences that are not genes. What are they, and what is the general lesson?

Q: How does DNA duplication create a single new gene rather than a new gene family?
![An antibody molecule of two heavy chains with four domains each and two light chains with two domains each, every domain encoded by a separate exon](assets/img/fig-4-76.png)
A: By stringing together short duplicated segments within one gene. Proteins made this way are recognizable by their repeating similar domains covalently linked in series, and the immunoglobulins are the example: an antibody is two identical heavy chains of four similar domains each and two identical light chains of two domains each, every domain encoded by a separate exon, and all those exons are thought to have arisen by serial duplication of a single ancestral exon. Most fibrous proteins such as the collagens are encoded by genes that evolved the same way. More generally, both exons and regulatory elements have served as modular pieces that were duplicated and moved around the genome, so many present-day proteins are a patchwork of domains from different origins.

Q+: Why do introns make this kind of evolution easier?
A: Because they give recombination somewhere harmless to happen. In genes of this type each exon often encodes one protein folding domain, so duplicating a domain means duplicating an exon, and that can be done by breaking and rejoining the DNA anywhere in the long introns on either side of it. Without introns there would be only a few sites in the gene where a recombinational exchange could duplicate the domain without disrupting it, since a break inside coding sequence destroys the product. Introns also often contain sequences repeated many times in the genome, which facilitates recombination between different introns. By making many potential sites available instead of a few, introns raise the probability that a duplication event yields a working new protein.

Q: How much do two random humans differ, compared with a human and a chimpanzee?

Q: Every fixed difference between humans and chimpanzees began as one mutation in one individual. How does a **neutral** mutation ever become universal?
A: By a random walk. If the interbreeding population has size $N$, a new mutation in a diploid organism starts at an allele frequency of $1/(2N)$. A significantly deleterious mutation is removed by purifying selection and never fixes; a mutation conferring a major reproductive advantage can spread rapidly. But the great majority of mutations that are not harmful are not beneficial either, and these selectively neutral ones spread purely through random variation in the number of mutation-bearing offspring each carrier happens to produce. The allele may go extinct or may become commonplace. For an idealized population of constant size undergoing random mating, the probability that a new neutral mutation ultimately fixes is approximately $1/(2N)$, because there are $2N$ copies of the gene in the population and each has an equal chance of becoming the predominant version. For those that do fix, the average time to fixation is approximately $4N$ generations.

Q+: Put human numbers into that. What do they give?
A: Analyses of human genetic variation suggest an ancestral population of approximately 10,000 at the time the current pattern of variation was largely established. With $N = 10{,}000$, a new neutral mutation has a fixation probability of $1/20{,}000$, and the average time to fixation is $4N$ generations, which at a 20-year generation time is on the order of 800,000 years. That has a consequence for how we should read our own variation: the human population has grown enormously since agriculture began about 15,000 years ago, but 800,000 years is far longer than that, so most present-day common human variants are a mixture that was already present long before, when the population was still small.

Q+: What is a **founder effect**, and why does it matter for genetic counselling?
![A large original population with a rare red allele, a chance event reducing it to a small founder group containing one carrier, and a new population in which the red allele is common](assets/img/fig-4-77.png)
A: When a chance event greatly reduces population size, whichever alleles happen to be in the survivors become the whole basis of the new population, so an allele that was rare in the large population can end up at high frequency with no selective advantage at all, and even if it is mildly deleterious. The book's examples of communities descended from small groups of founders are the people of Iceland and the Jews of Eastern Europe. The practical implication is that the frequency of a disease variant in a particular community carries no information about how harmful it is: it may be common there for reasons that are purely historical.

Q: How far back can DNA actually be sequenced, and what did the Neanderthal genome show?

Q+: What route does Figure 4-78 lay out, and where do the Denisovans come in?

Q: For ancestors too old to yield DNA, how can their sequences be recovered, and what can then be done with them?

Q: How many new mutations does a person carry that neither parent had, and why does that not make common variants rare?
A: Every person is born with 50 to 100 de novo mutations in the sequences inherited from their parents, so new mutations are constantly accumulating in the population and the total number of variants among modern humans is huge. But the vast majority of those variants are extremely rare, precisely because they arose recently among a very large number of births. The common variants are a different population of a different age: they descend from the variation present in a comparatively tiny group of ancestors and have had hundreds of thousands of years to spread. So the number of distinct variants is dominated by recent rare ones while the variation any two people actually differ by is dominated by ancient common ones.

Q+: Define **variant**, **allele** and **SNV**, and say what happened to the term SNP.
A: A variant is a genomic location at which two or more versions of a sequence exist in the human population; an allele is the specific form the variant takes in a given person. Because we are diploid, an individual's genotype at a variant with alleles A and B is A/A, A/B or B/B. A single-nucleotide variant is a position where some haploid human genomes carry one nucleotide and others carry another, and SNVs are the vast majority of differences between humans, extensively characterized because they are abundant, relatively easy to detect and valuable for genetic studies. Historically "single-nucleotide polymorphism", or SNP, was reserved for SNVs present above 1% frequency in the population, but no precise threshold is used today to separate rare from common, which span everything from a private variation present in one haploid genome of one person up to 50%.

Q+: SNVs are not the whole story. What else varies between two human genomes?

These matter more than their small numbers suggest, because a single structural variant often affects tens or even hundreds of thousands of nucleotides, so structural variation is a large contributor to the total number of nucleotide-level differences between any two human genomes even though SNVs are far more numerous as events.

Q+: Comparing any one human genome with the reference finds 3 to 5 million variant locations. What fraction of those alleles are common, and what does that imply about their effects?
A: More than 95% of the variant alleles seen in any given person are relatively common, reflecting their ancient origin, and only a small fraction are rare. The implication is that most of what makes your genome different from the reference has at most a weak effect on your phenotype, because deleterious variants are selected against during evolution and therefore stay rare. The rule generalizes across all forms of variation, structural included: common structural variants also tend not to be deleterious, since if they were they would never have reached appreciable frequency. So allele frequency is the crucial quantity: common alleles tend to have weak effects and large-effect alleles tend to be rare. The converse does not hold, and the chapter is careful about it: a rare variant is not thereby important, and most rare variants have little or no effect either.

Q+: If common variants have weak effects, how do genome-wide association studies find anything?
A: By adding up the weak effects. GWAS associates human traits with a large set of alleles that are relatively common in the population, and frequent SNVs are ideal markers for it precisely because they are frequent. Individually most of those SNVs have little or no effect on fitness, which is why they are still common. But the combined effect of many common alleles can produce a large impact on phenotype, and that is what has let GWAS identify many associations between common traits, including common diseases, and common alleles in recent years.

Q: The chapter says no threshold separates a SNP from a SNV any more. Why did the distinction stop being useful, and what replaced it?
A: Because the technology stopped forcing the question. The 1% cutoff belonged to an era when variants were assayed on genotyping arrays: a site had to be chosen in advance and put on the chip, and only sites common enough to be worth the space made the cut, so "polymorphism" really meant "common enough for us to be looking at it". Sequencing an individual finds every variant they carry irrespective of frequency, so the category has nothing left to do. What replaced it is the number itself: instead of calling a variant a SNP you quote its allele frequency in a reference population database, and the frequency is what carries the meaning. In practice SNV is now the neutral term for the class of variant, and SNP survives mostly as a label for the common array-genotyped markers used in association studies, plus in the rs identifiers from dbSNP that everyone still uses as names.

Q: Table 4-3 puts indels at 1 to 49 nucleotide pairs. Where does that boundary come from, and why are indels harder to call than SNVs?
A: The boundary is a convention rather than a biological fact. Fifty nucleotide pairs is roughly where a difference stops fitting inside a single short sequencing read and its alignment, so below 50 it is called an **indel** by the ordinary variant caller and at 50 and above it is called a structural variant and needs different methods. The difficulty is that an indel has no single correct position. In a homopolymer run or a short tandem repeat, which is where indels overwhelmingly occur because replication slippage is what generates them, the same deleted nucleotide can be written at any of several positions and the alignment is genuinely ambiguous, so two pipelines will represent the identical variant differently unless the representation is normalised by left-alignment. On top of that, the polymerase error rate rises sharply in homopolymer runs, so a false indel is a much more likely artefact than a false substitution. They are nonetheless the second most numerous class of variation, at 0.4 to 0.5 million per genome against 3 to 4 million SNVs.

Q+: Why does a single nucleotide inserted into an exon usually matter far more than a single nucleotide substituted there?
A: Because the reading frame is shared by everything downstream. A substitution changes one codon, and most of the time the result is a synonymous change or a missense change that the protein tolerates. An insertion or deletion of one or two nucleotides shifts the frame, so every codon after it is read wrongly, and a premature stop codon typically turns up within a few dozen codons simply by chance. The transcript carrying that premature stop is then usually destroyed by nonsense-mediated decay rather than translated into a truncated protein, so the practical effect of a frameshift is a null allele: no product at all from that copy of the gene. Two qualifications are worth carrying: an indel of three nucleotides, or any multiple of three, stays in frame and removes or adds a residue instead, which is often far milder, and a premature stop in the last exon escapes nonsense-mediated decay and does yield a truncated protein, which can be worse than a null if that protein interferes with its normal partner.

Q: Structural variants are only tens of thousands per genome against millions of SNVs. Why do they matter so disproportionately in the clinic?
A: Two reasons, one arithmetic and one mechanistic. The arithmetic is that a single structural variant can move tens or hundreds of thousands of nucleotides, so a few tens of thousands of them contribute a comparable number of differing nucleotides to the millions of SNVs. The mechanism is that deleting or duplicating a segment changes gene dosage outright, which the chapter names as one of the three kinds of change with large effects on health, and a rearrangement can also break a gene in the middle or separate a gene from its regulatory DNA without touching either. They also recur at predictable places rather than falling randomly: where two segmental duplications flank a region, non-allelic homologous recombination between them deletes or duplicates the interval, which is why the recurrent genomic disorders keep reappearing at the same coordinates in unrelated patients, the 22q11.2 deletion behind DiGeorge syndrome being the standard example. So the same repeats that Figure 4-63 counts as genomic clutter are what make particular rearrangements happen over and over.

Q: The chapter mentions GWAS in two sentences and refers you to Chapter 8. What does the study design actually consist of?
A: You take a large group of people scored for a trait, either as cases and controls for a disease or as a measured quantity such as height or blood pressure, and you genotype every one of them at hundreds of thousands of common variants using an array. The genotypes are then extended by **imputation**: because nearby variants are inherited together in blocks, a reference panel of fully sequenced genomes lets you infer genotypes at millions of sites that were never on the chip. Then each variant is tested separately for association with the trait, usually under an additive model in which the effect is proportional to how many copies of the allele a person carries, by logistic regression for a disease or linear regression for a measurement, including covariates for age, sex and ancestry. The output is one effect size and one p-value per variant, millions of times over. Nothing about the design tests a hypothesis about any particular gene, which is the point of it: the whole genome is scanned without a prior candidate, which is why it found so much that candidate-gene studies had missed.

Q+: Testing millions of variants at once means a p-value of 0.05 is worthless. What threshold is used, and where does it come from?
A: Genome-wide significance is $p < 5 \times 10^{-8}$, and it is a Bonferroni correction: $0.05$ divided by roughly $10^6$, on the reasoning that although many millions of variants are tested, linkage disequilibrium makes neighbouring variants highly correlated, so the number of genuinely independent tests across the genome is on the order of a million. The number came originally from Risch and Merikangas in 1996 by a different route, assuming 100,000 genes with about five variants tested in each, and the original assumptions are long obsolete while the threshold has survived essentially untouched, in the practical sense that associations meeting it have very rarely failed to replicate. The standard display of the result is a **Manhattan plot**, with genomic position along the x-axis and $-\log_{10}(p)$ up the y-axis, so the threshold is a horizontal line at 7.3 and a real association appears as a tower of correlated variants rising through it rather than as a single point.

Q+: A GWAS names a specific variant. Why is that variant usually not the one doing anything?
A: Because association is inherited in blocks. Variants close together on a chromosome are in **linkage disequilibrium**, meaning they are correlated across the population because recombination has not yet had time to separate them, so a genotyped marker reports not only on itself but on everything it is correlated with. That is what makes the array design work at all, since a few hundred thousand well-chosen tag SNPs can capture most common variation, but it also means the strongest signal in a region is simply the variant most tightly correlated with the causal one, which is a matter of which markers were on the chip and how the local block is structured. Resolving this is **fine-mapping**: use the pattern of association across the whole block, plus the shorter blocks in populations with more historical recombination, to compute a credible set of variants that plausibly contains the causal one. Frequently the credible set still holds dozens of candidates, and the honest position for most GWAS loci is that the causal variant, and often the causal gene, is not yet identified.

Q+: Where in the genome do the hits actually land, and what does that tell you about how they work?
A: Overwhelmingly outside protein-coding sequence. In the first systematic survey of the catalogue, 45% of trait-associated variants were intronic and 43% intergenic, so about 88% were noncoding, and the picture has not changed as the catalogue has grown. The interpretation is that most common variants influencing common traits act by changing how much of a gene product is made, when, and in which cell type, rather than by changing a protein's sequence, which is what you would expect from the argument the chapter makes elsewhere: a variant altering a protein directly is more likely to be strongly deleterious and therefore rare, while a regulatory variant nudging expression can be tolerated and stay common. Following one up therefore means finding which regulatory element it sits in and which gene that element controls, typically by asking whether the same variant is associated with expression of a nearby gene, an expression quantitative trait locus, and whether the two association signals genuinely coincide rather than being two separate variants in the same block.

Q: What is **population stratification**, and why is it the failure mode a GWAS has to be designed against?
A: It is confounding by ancestry. Allele frequencies differ between populations for entirely historical reasons, as the chapter's account of bottlenecks and founder effects explains, and disease prevalence differs between populations too, for reasons that may be environmental or cultural. If your cases and controls are drawn in different proportions from two ancestral groups, then every variant whose frequency differs between those groups will be associated with the trait, and none of those associations mean anything causal. The teaching illustration is a study that would confidently identify a "gene for eating with chopsticks" in a mixed sample, since any variant more common in the group that uses chopsticks will track the behaviour perfectly. The correction is to measure ancestry from the genotype data itself, usually as the leading principal components of the genotype matrix, and fit those as covariates, or to use a linear mixed model that accounts for relatedness and ancestry together. Residual stratification is checked by asking whether test statistics are inflated across the whole genome rather than at particular loci.

Q: How large are the effects a GWAS finds, and how does that compare with the mutations behind rare inherited disease?
A: Small, and the contrast is the organising fact of human genetics. A typical common variant associated with a common disease carries an odds ratio around 1.1, meaning it shifts risk by roughly a tenth, and effects of that size are only detectable in cohorts of tens or hundreds of thousands of people. The mutations behind rare Mendelian disease sit at the other corner: allele frequencies far below 1 in 1000, effects large enough that a single variant is diagnostic in one family. Those two corners are the ones you can populate, and the reason is the chapter's own argument about allele frequency, since a variant with a large effect on fitness is kept rare by selection and cannot become common, so common variants of large effect are largely absent by construction rather than by our failure to find them. The corner that remains genuinely hard is rare variants of moderate effect, which are too rare for association testing and too weak to segregate cleanly in a pedigree.

Q: What is the **missing heritability** problem, and what happened to it?
A: Twin and family studies estimated that human height is about 80% heritable, but the first well-powered GWAS found around 40 variants that together explained about 5% of the variance in height. The gap between the two was named missing heritability, and it was for some years the standard objection to the whole enterprise. Most of the gap turned out to be a power problem rather than a missing mechanism: the trait is influenced by very large numbers of variants of very small effect, and each one only crosses the significance threshold when the sample is large enough. The 2022 height study is the demonstration, using 5.4 million people to identify 12,111 independent variants that account for essentially all the heritability attributable to common variants, and about 40% of the variance in populations of European ancestry. Those variants cluster in around 7200 genomic segments covering some 21% of the genome, which is the point where the word polygenic stops being an abstraction. The remainder of the gap is attributed to rare variants that arrays do not capture, and to the twin-study estimates themselves being somewhat inflated by shared environment.

Q+: If thousands of variants each contribute a little, they can be added up. What is a **polygenic risk score**, and what is wrong with it at the moment?
A: A polygenic risk score sums the risk alleles a person carries, each weighted by the effect size estimated from a GWAS, giving one number that places them in the distribution of genetic liability for a trait. For several common diseases the tail of that distribution now identifies people at a risk comparable to carrying a rare high-penetrance mutation, which is what makes it interesting for prevention rather than diagnosis. The problem is that the score does not transfer between ancestries. Weights are estimated in one population and applied in another, but allele frequencies and the linkage disequilibrium between the marker and the causal variant both differ, so the marker is a worse proxy elsewhere: prediction accuracy measured as $R^2$ is about 4.5 times lower in people of African ancestry than in Europeans. That is a direct consequence of who has been studied, since people of European ancestry are roughly 16% of the world population and have supplied around 79% of GWAS participants, so a technology sold as precision medicine would currently deliver its precision unevenly.

Q: When a patient's genome turns up a variant nobody has seen before, what is the first question asked of it, and what resource answers it?
A: How common is it in people without the disease. A variant proposed to cause a severe early-onset disorder cannot be sitting at appreciable frequency in the general population, so an allele frequency above what the disease prevalence and inheritance mode allow rules it out immediately, and this single filter removes most candidates before anything else is considered. The resource is a population frequency database, in practice gnomAD, whose version 4 aggregates 807,162 individuals, 730,947 by exome sequencing and 76,215 by genome sequencing. The reasoning is exactly the chapter's own: common variants are common because selection tolerated them, so frequency is evidence about effect. The care needed is that frequency must be read within the relevant ancestry group rather than globally, since a variant can be rare worldwide and common in one population, and the databases remain thinner for some populations than others, which makes the filter weaker precisely where it is most needed.

Q+: Frequency alone rarely settles it. How is the evidence formalised, and what is a **VUS**?
A: Under the ACMG and AMP framework a variant is placed in one of five tiers: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign, benign. The classification combines lines of evidence of different strengths, including population frequency, the type of change and whether loss of function is a known mechanism for that gene, segregation with disease in families, whether the variant arose *de novo*, functional assays, and computational prediction, each weighted and then combined by rules rather than by impression. A **variant of uncertain significance** is the verdict when the evidence does not reach either end, and it is the most common outcome for a novel missense change. It is not a weak diagnosis but the absence of one, and it is not actionable: acting on a VUS as though it were pathogenic is a recognised source of harm. VUS rates are substantially higher for patients from populations underrepresented in the reference databases, for the same reason polygenic scores travel badly, so the two problems have one root.

Q: Why do CACA repeats make such good forensic markers?
A: Because they mutate far faster than the rest of the genome. Human mutation rates are generally on the order of $10^{-8}$, roughly one mutation every 100 million base pairs, but segments with the motif $(CA)_n$ are replicated with relatively low fidelity because the template and the newly synthesized strand slip against each other during replication, so the value of $n$ varies considerably between genomes. The result is that most humans are heterozygous at each of these loci, having inherited one repeat length from their mother and a different one from their father. The balance is what makes them useful: $n$ changes rarely enough that most parent-to-child transmissions pass the repeat on faithfully, which is what a paternity test needs, yet often enough to maintain high heterozygosity in the population, which is what an identification needs. These and other highly variable simple repeats are the basis of DNA fingerprinting in crime investigations, paternity suits and other forensic applications.

Q: What kinds of genetic difference are known to have large effects on human health?
A: Three, of increasing subtlety. A single nucleotide change that alters one amino acid can cause serious disease, sickle-cell disease being the example, which is not even rare. Gene dosage matters: doubling or halving the copy number of some genes can profoundly affect development by altering the level of gene product. And a change in one of the many regulatory DNA sequences dispersed through the vast expanse of noncoding DNA can do the same by altering when and how much a gene is expressed. The challenge in modern human genetics is discriminating the differences with substantial effects from the majority that are of little consequence, and the nucleotide changes behind thousands of rare inherited traits and diseases are now known. That is the basis for precision medicine, in which prevention and treatment are tailored to individual genetic differences.

Q: Summarize what comparing human, chimpanzee and mouse genomes has established.
A: Human and chimpanzee are separated by about 12 million years of evolution, double the roughly 6 million years since they shared a common ancestor, and show fixed changes at only about 1% of base pairs. Not only are the protein-coding sequences highly similar, their order along each chromosome is almost identical, and even the positions of the transposable elements making up much of our noncoding DNA are mostly unchanged, despite a substantial number of segmental duplications and deletions. Human and mouse, separated by about 90 million years, show far more change: most exons and many regulatory regions have been highly conserved by selection, while huge fractions of the two genomes have been altered beyond any detectable similarity. Between 8 and 10% of the human genome has been conserved by purifying selection, and the function of the majority of that, tens of thousands of sequence elements conserved across mammalian evolution, remains mysterious.

Q: The chapter dismisses ENCODE's claim about junk DNA in a paragraph. What is the published version of that argument?
A: Graur and colleagues, "On the immortality of television sets: 'function' in the human genome according to the evolution-free gospel of ENCODE", *Genome Biology and Evolution* 2013. The central argument is a reductio: the fraction of the genome conserved by purifying selection is under 10%, so if 80% were functional then roughly 70% of the genome would have to be invulnerable to deleterious mutation, which is absurd. The paper's diagnosis is that ENCODE used a causal-role definition of function, under which anything that does something counts, and then applied it inconsistently across different biochemical assays. The title is the joke that carries the point: a television set that is never watched still consumes electricity and emits heat, and by a causal-role criterion that makes those activities its function. The chapter is siding with this position when it explains ENCODE's transcripts as background noise in gene expression, at levels of less than one RNA molecule per cell.

Q: The chapter says cryo-electron microscopy of carefully prepared nuclei suggests most chromatin is less regularly structured than the zigzag model. Which experiment is that?
A: ChromEMT, from Ou and colleagues in *Science* in 2017, which used a DNA-binding dye that catalyzes the deposition of a polymer visible by electron microscopy, then imaged whole nuclei by electron tomography. What it found in human interphase and mitotic cells in situ were disordered chromatin chains 5 to 24 nm in diameter, of varying particle arrangement and density, with no regular higher-order fibre anywhere. The control matters: the same method does detect 30 nm fibres in nuclei purified from hypotonically lysed chicken erythrocytes and treated with magnesium, so the classical fibre is real but is a product of the preparation rather than a feature of chromatin in a living nucleus. That is why Figure 4-62's tidy hierarchy of 11 nm to 30 nm to 700 nm should be read as a schematic of compaction ratios rather than as a series of structures.

Q: The chapter says position effect variegation was "first recognized in the fruit fly *Drosophila*" without saying by whom or when. Who, and how?
A: Hermann Muller, in 1930, as a by-product of the X-ray mutagenesis for which he had already established that radiation causes mutations. Among his irradiated flies were lines whose eyes carried patches of red pigmented cells intermingled with patches of white, rather than the uniform white of an ordinary *White* mutant. Six years later it was shown that each of those lines carried a chromosomal rearrangement, the best known being the inversion *In(1)wm4*, that brought pericentric heterochromatin next to the *White* locus. The coding sequence and regulatory elements of the gene were untouched in every case; what silenced it in a subset of eye progenitor cells was heterochromatin spreading across the breakpoint. So the phenomenon that opened the whole subject of epigenetic chromatin inheritance was found by someone looking for something else entirely, and it took six years to establish that the mutation was not in the gene at all.

Q: The chapter says of SMC rings that "it is still uncertain how they drive loop formation". Has anyone watched one do it?
A: Yes, in 2019, when two groups reconstituted the reaction and imaged single molecules. Davidson and colleagues and Kim and colleagues, both in *Science*, showed that a single human cohesin complex, given the NIPBL-MAU2 cofactor, extrudes a DNA loop in vitro at rates of up to about 2 kilobase pairs per second, using its own ATPase. Two details from those experiments bear on the chapter's account. Loop formation required ATPase activity and NIPBL-MAU2 but not topological entrapment of the DNA inside the ring, so the ring does not have to encircle the loop it is making. And extrusion was symmetric, with DNA reeled in from both sides, rather than the ring travelling one way along a static molecule as the inchworm cartoon suggests. Direct observation of the mechanism does not settle it: the chapter's caution stands, and the competing models on offer now include a swing-and-clamp cycle and a Brownian ratchet.

Q: True or false? Human females have 23 different chromosomes, whereas human males have 24.

Q: True or false? The four core histones are relatively small proteins with a very high proportion of positively charged amino acids, and the positive charge helps them bind tightly to DNA regardless of its nucleotide sequence.
A: True. The core histones are 102 to 135 amino acids, and more than a fifth of the residues in each is lysine or arginine, whose positive charges neutralize the negatively charged DNA backbone. Because that binding, along with most of the 142 hydrogen bonds per nucleosome, is to the sugar-phosphate backbone rather than to the bases, DNA of virtually any sequence can be wrapped. The one caveat worth attaching is that "regardless of sequence" is not quite absolute: bending the DNA compresses the minor groove, and AA, TT and TA dinucleotides are accommodated more easily where the minor groove faces inward, so some sequences do bind more tightly. That preference is weak enough that other factors, chiefly other bound proteins, usually decide where a nucleosome sits.

Q: True or false? Nucleosomes bind DNA so tightly that they cannot move from the positions where they are first assembled.
A: False, and it fails three times over. The DNA in an isolated nucleosome spontaneously unwraps from each end about four times a second and stays exposed for 10 to 50 milliseconds, so even without help most of it is intermittently available. Inside a cell, ATP-dependent chromatin-remodeling complexes actively slide nucleosomes along the DNA, one base pair per cycle of ATP hydrolysis, and there is roughly one such complex per five nucleosomes. Those complexes also exchange H2A-H2B dimers and remove entire octamers, so a typical nucleosome is replaced on the DNA every one or two hours. If the statement were true, transcription, replication and repair would all be impossible.

Q: True or false? The long linear DNA molecule in an interphase chromosome is organized into loops of chromatin that appear to emanate from a central axis.

Q: True or false? In a comparison between the DNAs of related organisms such as humans and mice, identifying the conserved DNA sequences facilitates the search for functionally important regions.
A: True, and it is the single most useful trick in the chapter. Humans and mice diverged about 90 million years ago, long enough for most nucleotides to have changed by random mutation, so anything still similar has been held there by purifying selection, meaning that changing it cost something. Adding more species sharpens the signal. It identifies about 4.5% of our genome as multispecies conserved sequence, and to the chapter's stated surprise only a quarter of that codes for protein. The limitation to keep alongside it is that conservation tells you a sequence matters without telling you what it does, and much of the conserved noncoding DNA still has no known function.

Q: True or false? Gene duplication and divergence are thought to have played a critical role in the evolution of increased biological complexity.
A: True. Comparing very different organisms rarely turns up genes in one with no homolog in the other; what differs is the number of members in each gene family. Duplication supplies a spare copy that is initially under no selection, and while the commonest fate is that one copy decays into a pseudogene, the alternative is that both stay active and diverge in sequence and expression pattern. The globin family is the worked example, from a single-chain globin through the α and β split about 500 million years ago to the fetal and adult clusters on two human chromosomes. The zebrafish gives the quantitative version: after an ancient whole-genome duplication, perhaps 30 to 50% of duplicate pairs have both remained active and diverged functionally, usually by being expressed in different tissues or at different stages.

Q: DNA from the bacterial virus M13 contains 25% A, 33% T, 22% C and 20% G. Should that strike you as peculiar?
A: It should strike you at first and then stop. In double-stranded DNA every A is paired with a T and every G with a C, so A must equal T and G must equal C when a whole molecule is analysed. Here A is 25% against T at 33%, and C is 22% against G at 20%, so the base equivalences fail badly and the DNA cannot be a normal duplex. The explanation is that the M13 genome is a single-stranded circle of DNA, and there is no reason whatever for a single strand to contain equal amounts of A and T. Chargaff's rules are a consequence of base-pairing between two strands, not a property of DNA as a substance, which is a distinction worth having straight.

Q: A segment from the interior of a single DNA strand is shown. Should it be written ACT or TCA?
![Three nucleotides drawn in a vertical chain, adenine at the top, cytosine in the middle and thymine at the bottom, joined through phosphates with the chain continuing at both ends](assets/img/fig-q4-01.png)
A: ACT, because by convention a nucleotide sequence is written from its 5′ end to its 3′ end. Work out which end is which from the structure rather than from the page: in each nucleotide the CH₂ group is the 5′ carbon, and here it is drawn above its sugar, with each phosphate joining the 3′ position of the sugar above it to the 5′ carbon of the sugar below. So reading down the page is reading 5′ to 3′, and top to bottom gives A, then C, then T. Written the other way round, TCA, the same three nucleotides would mean a different sequence, since a DNA strand is directional and a sequence quoted without its polarity is ambiguous.

Q: Human DNA contains 20% C on a molar basis. What are the mole percents of A, G and T?
A: G is 20%, and A and T are 30% each. Because human genomic DNA is double-stranded, G equals C, so G is 20% and the two together account for 40% of the bases. The remaining 60% is A plus T, and since A equals T they are 30% each. The check worth doing is that the answer must sum to 100 and must satisfy both equalities simultaneously, which is what distinguishes this from the M13 problem, where neither equality held.

Q: Histone acetylation always correlates with gene activation, but histone methylation can lead to either activation or repression. How can one modification mediate opposite outcomes?
A: Because "methylation" is not one mark. Which residue is methylated matters: H3K4me3 sits in highly accessible open chromatin with the gene on, while H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 mark the two classes of heterochromatin with the gene off. How many methyl groups are added matters too, since mono-, di- and trimethyl lysine are recognized by different binding proteins and can mean different things. And the meaning of any mark is set by whichever reader binds it, not by the chemistry: trimethylated H3K9 attracts HP1 and builds one kind of heterochromatin, trimethylated H3K27 attracts polycomb and builds another. Acetylation is uniform by comparison because it does something physical and general, removing the lysine's positive charge and loosening the grip on the DNA backbone, so it points the same way wherever it happens.

Q: Why is a chromosome with two centromeres unstable? Would a backup not be a good thing, giving two chances to attach to the spindle?
A: The problem is not attachment but agreement. A kinetochore at each centromere can be captured by microtubules from opposite poles, and then at anaphase the chromosome is pulled toward both ends of the cell at once. Either it is stretched until the DNA between the two centromeres breaks, producing two broken fragments that will be misrepaired or lost, or it lags and gets caught by the cleaving cell. The reason the backup intuition fails is that a centromere is not a redundant grip on a cargo, it is the instruction saying which way this molecule goes, and two instructions can conflict where one cannot. That is why chromosome evolution has to inactivate surplus centromeres: a fusion of two chromosomes is only viable once one of the two centromeres has been silenced, and the plasticity of centromere identity, which is protein-based rather than sequence-based in complex organisms, is what makes that possible.

Q: A yeast colony of 100,000 cells is mostly red because *Ade2* has been moved next to a telomere and silenced, but white sectors fan out from the middle to the rim. Why do the sectors form, and what do they say about inheritance of the transcriptional state?
![A white yeast colony with Ade2 at its normal location, and a red colony with white sectors where Ade2 has been moved near a telomere](assets/img/fig-q4-02.png)
A: A sector is a clone. *Ade2* is still at the telomere in both the red and white parts, so nothing has changed in the DNA; what changed is the chromatin. In one cell, at some point during the colony's growth, the telomeric heterochromatin failed to be re-established over the gene and *Ade2* switched on, and every descendant of that cell inherited the active state, producing a wedge of white cells expanding outward from wherever the ancestor sat. Two conclusions follow. The transcriptional state is heritable through many cell divisions, because otherwise the white cells would be scattered singly through a red colony rather than gathered into a sector. And it is not permanent, because the switch happened at all, so silencing is a stable but occasionally reversible state rather than a fixed one. A sector that reaches the rim from near the middle also dates the switching event, since the wider the sector, the earlier it happened. This is position effect variegation in yeast, the same phenomenon as the mottled fly eye.

Note that the two experiments run in opposite directions, one making loops smaller and one making them larger, which rules out the trivial explanation that injection damages chromatin or that one species' loops are simply dominant. Q: Transposable elements make up more than 40% of the human genome and insert more or less randomly, yet they are conspicuously rare across the four *Hox* clusters. Why?
![Two 1-megabase regions of human chromosomes, with exons projecting upward and transposable elements projecting downward as a nearly solid block everywhere except across the HoxD cluster](assets/img/fig-q4-03.png)
A: Because insertions there are removed by purifying selection rather than avoided in the first place. The elements insert more or less at random, so they land in *Hox* clusters as often as anywhere else; what differs is whether the resulting individual survives. Each cluster is about 100 kilobases holding 9 to 11 genes whose differential expression along the anteroposterior axis establishes the basic body plan, and that expression is controlled by regulatory elements distributed across the whole cluster, with the order of the genes along the DNA matching the order of the body regions they specify. Almost any insertion anywhere in the cluster therefore disrupts the regulation of one or more of those genes and produces a badly malformed embryo. The near-solid block of elements immediately outside the cluster is the control: it shows the insertion rate is normal there and it is retention, not insertion, that differs. The same argument explains the absence of transposons from the globin structural genes in Figure 4-70, on a smaller scale.

Q: Chromosome 3 in orangutans differs from human chromosome 3 by two inversions in the human lineage. Draw the intermediate and name the segments in each inversion.
![Orangutan chromosome 3 with a red short arm, then red, blue, yellow, green and red on the long arm, and human chromosome 3 with red, yellow, blue and red on the short arm and red, green and red on the long arm](assets/img/fig-q4-04.png)
A: Reading the orangutan from the top: a red short arm, the centromere, then red, blue, yellow, green and a red tip. The human reads red tip, yellow, blue, red, centromere, red, green, red tip. So the blue and yellow blocks have crossed to the other side of the centromere and their order down the page has swapped, while green has stayed put on the long arm.

The first inversion is pericentric, with one breakpoint inside the red of the short arm and the other at the junction between the red block below the centromere and the blue block. It flips the centromere together with the red on either side of it, so the intermediate reads: red tip, red, centromere, red, blue, yellow, green, red tip. This intermediate looks almost unchanged, because everything that moved is red.

The second inversion is also pericentric and starts from the same upper breakpoint, running down to the yellow-green junction. It inverts the whole block of red, centromere, red, blue, yellow, giving red tip, yellow, blue, red, centromere, red, green, red tip, which is the human chromosome. The reason the second inversion has to include the centromere is that blue and yellow have to end up on the other side of it, and the reason it has to be the second one rather than the first is that inverting them once more is what restores yellow above blue.

Q: Four sets of male twins have been shuffled in a maternity ward. You run a probe against simple sequence repeats such as $(CA)_n$ scattered through the genome. Which infants are brothers, and are they all identical twins?
![A DNA fingerprint gel with eight infant lanes, each showing a pattern of bands of differing intensity](assets/img/fig-q4-05.png)

Lanes 2 and 8 match band for band, and so do lanes 3 and 6, which is only possible if each of those pairs came from a single zygote: two different individuals cannot share every allele at a dozen highly variable loci. Lanes 1 and 7, and lanes 4 and 5, share roughly half their bands and differ in the rest, which is what ordinary siblings do, so those are fraternal twins. Q+: How would you match each pair of brothers to the right parents?
A: By running the same fingerprint on the parents and applying the inheritance rule, which is that every band in a child must be present in one of its two parents. Each $(CA)_n$ locus gives a child one allele from the mother and one from the father, so a child's set of bands is a union of a subset of the mother's and a subset of the father's, and a couple who lack a band that the infants carry cannot be their parents. With four couples and four pairs, one round of comparison assigns them all, and the exclusion is what does the work: it takes only a single band with no possible parental source to rule a couple out, whereas matching bands accumulate as positive evidence. This is exactly how the technique is used in paternity testing, and it works because $(CA)_n$ repeats mutate often enough that almost everyone is heterozygous at each locus, while still passing faithfully from parent to child in the great majority of transmissions.
