Q: Until the late nineteenth century, animals were believed to contain a Vital Force, an "animus", responsible for their distinctive properties. What replaced that idea, and what is genuinely special about the chemistry of life?
A: Nothing in a living organism disobeys chemical or physical law, so no extra force is needed. What remains special is the kind of chemistry involved. It runs in aqueous solution and is built overwhelmingly on carbon compounds. Most of its carbon atoms sit in enormous polymers rather than small molecules, and the properties of those macromolecules are what let cells grow and reproduce. Above all it is complex: even the simplest cell is vastly more complicated chemically than any other chemical system known, complicated enough that its networks of reactions show emergent properties.

---

Q: Of the 92 naturally occurring elements, how few does life use?
![Periodic table with the four main elements of cells highlighted in red, seven minor elements in blue, and trace elements in green](assets/img/fig-2-01.png)
A: A small selection. Carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen and oxygen alone make up 96.5% of an organism's weight and 99% of the total number of atoms in the human body. A further seven elements account for about 0.9% of our atoms, and others are needed only in trace amounts. The chemistry of life is predominantly the chemistry of the lighter elements.

---

Q: How much stronger is a covalent bond than the thermal energies inside a cell, and why does that matter?
A: Typically about 100 times stronger. That margin is what stops thermal motion from pulling molecules apart at random, so covalent bonds are normally broken only during biologically catalysed reactions that the cell has a use for. Noncovalent bonds are far weaker, but sets of them let molecules recognize each other and associate reversibly, which is what most biological function depends on.

---

Q: How is bond strength measured, and what are the two units in use?
![Logarithmic scale of energies important to cells, from average thermal motions through noncovalent bond breakage to ATP hydrolysis and complete glucose oxidation](assets/img/fig-2-02.png)
A: By the energy that must be supplied to break the bond, per mole. If 100 kJ is needed to break one mole of a bond, that is $6 \times 10^{23}$ bonds, the bond strength is 100 kJ/mole. Physical scientists use the kilojoule, where one joule is the energy needed to move an object one meter against a force of one newton. Cell biologists often use the kilocalorie instead, where one calorie raises the temperature of one gram of water by 1°C. One kilojoule equals 0.239 kcal.

---

Q: What fraction of a cell's weight is water, and why is that a fact about chemistry rather than plumbing?
A: About 70%. It means the reactions that make life possible all happen in an aqueous environment, and the properties of water therefore constrain every one of them. Life is thought to have begun in shallow bodies of water that concentrated essential molecules, and the conditions of that primeval environment have left a permanent stamp on the chemistry of all living things.

---

Q: What is a hydrogen bond, and why is it directional when a plain electrostatic attraction is not?
![A hydrogen bond forming between two water molecules, and the general donor and acceptor arrangement between molecules containing oxygen or nitrogen](assets/img/fig-2-03.png)
A: It is a special form of polar interaction in which an electropositive hydrogen atom is shared between two electronegative atoms. The hydrogen can be viewed as a proton partly dissociated from a donor atom, which allows a second acceptor atom to share it. Because the sharing depends on the geometry of that arrangement, the bond is strongest when a straight line can be drawn through all three atoms involved, which an ordinary electrostatic attraction between charges does not require.

---

Q+: Why is water a liquid at room temperature rather than a gas?
A: Because of hydrogen bonding between water molecules. Each water molecule can form hydrogen bonds through its two hydrogen atoms to two others, producing a network that is continually broken and re-formed. Any single bond is weak and short-lived, easily broken by thermal motion, but the combined effect of many of them gives water its high boiling point and high surface tension.

---

Q: Distinguish **hydrophilic** from **hydrophobic** molecules in terms of what they can do with water.
A: Hydrophilic molecules either carry charges or contain polar bonds that let them form hydrogen bonds with water, so they dissolve readily. Sugars, DNA, RNA and most proteins are hydrophilic, as they must be to work in a cell. Hydrophobic molecules are uncharged and form few or no hydrogen bonds, so they do not dissolve. Hydrocarbons are the important example: every hydrogen is joined to carbon by a largely nonpolar bond, so the molecule cannot form effective hydrogen bonds, and cells exploit that by building membranes from molecules with long hydrocarbon tails.

---

Q: Four noncovalent forces bring molecules together in cells. Which are they?
A: Hydrogen bonds, electrostatic attractions (ionic bonds), van der Waals attractions, and the hydrophobic force. The fourth is not strictly a bond at all.

---

Q+: What actually causes the hydrophobic force?
A: A pushing rather than a pulling. Nonpolar surfaces interfere with the highly favourable hydrogen-bonded network among water molecules, so they get forced out of it. Bringing any two nonpolar surfaces together reduces their contact with water, which is why the effect is entirely nonspecific about which surfaces they are. It is central to the proper folding of proteins.

---

Q+: What gives rise to a van der Waals attraction?
A: Fluctuations in the distribution of electrons within every atom, which generate a transient attraction when two atoms come very close. They are weak and nonspecific, and unlike the other forces they occur between all types of molecules, including entirely nonpolar ones.

---

Q: Both electrostatic attractions and hydrogen bonds are considerably weaker inside a cell than they would be in a vacuum. Why?
A: Because water competes. Water forms its own interactions with the very groups that would otherwise attract each other, so the net strength of both electrostatic attractions and hydrogen bonds is greatly reduced in aqueous surroundings.

---

Q: A single noncovalent bond has less than one twentieth the strength of a covalent bond, and would be no match for thermal motion. How do such bonds hold two macromolecules together?
![Two macromolecules with complementary surfaces binding through many simultaneous noncovalent interactions](assets/img/fig-2-04.png)
A: By acting in large numbers at once. Individually they are far too weak to matter, but their energies sum, so complementary surfaces that can form many of them simultaneously bind tightly. This is why binding depends on shape: only surfaces that fit can bring enough groups into position at the same time. In practice all four noncovalent forces usually contribute together.

---

Q: What is an **acid**, in the sense that matters in a cell?
![A molecule of acetic acid dissolving in water to give acetate and hydronium, and water molecules exchanging protons to form hydronium and hydroxyl ions](assets/img/fig-2-05.png)
A: A substance that releases protons when it dissolves in water, forming hydronium ions (H₃O⁺). A highly polar bond between hydrogen and another atom leaves the hydrogen as an almost naked proton, which is readily attracted to the partial negative charge on the oxygen of a neighbouring water molecule and transfers to it. The higher the resulting H₃O⁺ concentration, the more acidic the solution.

---

Q+: Why is the concentration of H₃O⁺ usually written as the H⁺ concentration, and what is it in pure water?
A: Purely by convention, even though most protons in aqueous solution are actually present as H₃O⁺ rather than free. In pure water the concentration is $10^{-7}$ M, produced by protons moving from one water molecule to another, which corresponds to pH 7.0.

---

Q: What distinguishes a **strong** acid from a **weak** one, and which kind matters most in cells?
A: How readily it gives up its proton to water. Hydrochloric acid loses its proton easily and is strong; acetic acid holds on to its proton more tightly and is weak. Most of the acids that matter in the cell are weak ones, including the many molecules containing a carboxyl (COOH) group.

---

Q+: Weak acids do not behave the same way in all conditions. What changes their behaviour?
A: The surrounding concentration of H₃O⁺. A weak acid gives up its proton more readily when the concentration in solution is low, and tends to take protons back when it is high. Since a proton passed to a molecule in the cell can alter that molecule's character, the acidity inside a cell has to be closely regulated.

---

Q: What is a **base**, and what makes weak bases the biologically interesting ones?
A: Any molecule capable of accepting a proton from a water molecule. Sodium hydroxide is a strong base because it dissociates readily to give Na⁺ and OH⁻. More important in living cells are weak bases, which accept a proton from water reversibly and only weakly. Many biologically important molecules contain an amino (NH₂) group, which generates OH⁻ by taking a proton from water.

---

Q: If the concentration of OH⁻ in a solution rises, what necessarily happens to H₃O⁺?
A: It falls. An OH⁻ ion combines with an H₃O⁺ ion to give two water molecules, so the two concentrations are tied together: the product $[\text{OH}^-] \times [\text{H}_3\text{O}^+]$ is always $10^{-14}$ (moles/liter)². Pure water holds an equal $10^{-7}$ M of each, which is what makes it neutral.

---

Q: What keeps the interior of a cell close to neutral pH?
A: **Buffers**: weak acids and bases that can release or take up protons near pH 7. By absorbing added protons or supplying them as needed, they hold the cell's environment relatively constant across a range of conditions.

---

Q: Carbon is outstanding among the elements for building large molecules. What about the atom makes that so, and what comes second?
A: It is small, and has four electrons and four vacancies in its outermost shell, so it can form four covalent bonds. What matters most is that carbon atoms join to each other through highly stable C-C bonds, producing chains and rings with no obvious upper limit to their size. Silicon is a poor second.

---

Q+: What distinguishes an **organic** molecule from an **inorganic** one?
A: Organic molecules are the carbon compounds made by cells. All other molecules, water included, are inorganic. Disregarding water and inorganic ions such as potassium, nearly every molecule in a cell is carbon-based.

---

Q: Which chemical groups recur again and again in the molecules cells make?
![Panel showing carbon skeletons, covalent bonds, and the common chemical groups found in biological molecules](assets/img/panel-2-01.png)
A: Methyl (-CH₃), hydroxyl (-OH), carboxyl (-COOH), carbonyl (-C=O), phosphate (-PO₃²⁻), sulfhydryl (-SH) and amino (-NH₂). Each has distinct chemical and physical properties that shape the behaviour of whatever molecule it sits in, which is why recognizing them tells you a great deal about how a molecule will act.

---

Q: What counts as a **small organic molecule** in a cell, and how many kinds are there?
A: A carbon-based compound of roughly 100 to 1000 daltons containing up to about 30 carbon atoms, usually free in solution. They make up only about one tenth of the total mass of organic matter in a cell but are very diverse: nearly 4000 different kinds have been detected in *E. coli* alone.

---

Q+: What happens to them?
A: They have several fates, and many molecules have more than one. Some serve as monomer subunits for the giant polymers that make up most of the cell's mass. Others are energy sources, broken down and transformed through metabolic pathways. A single small molecule can act both as a potential subunit for a macromolecule and as an energy source.

---

Q: Which four families do most of a cell's small organic molecules fall into?
![The four main families of small organic molecules in cells: sugars, fatty acids, amino acids and nucleotides, with the macromolecules each forms](assets/img/fig-2-06.png)
A: The sugars, the fatty acids, the nucleotides and the amino acids. Many compounds in cells fit none of these categories, but these four families, together with the macromolecules made by linking them into chains, account for a large fraction of the cell's mass. All organic molecules are built from and broken down into the same set of simple compounds, which is why the contents of a cell are chemically related rather than arbitrary.

---

Q: By weight, what is a bacterial cell actually made of?
![Chart of the approximate composition of a bacterial cell by weight, showing water, inorganic ions, small molecules and the macromolecules](assets/img/fig-2-07.png)
A: About 70% water and 30% chemicals, and of the chemicals the macromolecules dominate: protein 15%, RNA 6%, phospholipid 2%, DNA 1% and polysaccharide 2%, with small molecules at 3% and inorganic ions at 1%. The composition of an animal cell is similar despite its volume being roughly 1000 times greater. The major inorganic ions are Na⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻.

---

Q: How does a polymer chain grow, and what is the reverse reaction called?
![Condensation joins two subunits with the loss of a water molecule, while hydrolysis breaks the bond by adding water back](assets/img/fig-2-09.png)
A: By **condensation**: a monomer adds to the end of the growing chain and one molecule of water is lost with each subunit added. The reverse is **hydrolysis**, which breaks the bond by adding water back. Condensation reactions are all energetically unfavourable, so building a polymer requires an input of energy.

---

Q+: Why is stepwise polymerization such a convenient way to build something large?
A: Because the same reaction is performed over and over by the same set of enzymes. A large, complex molecule can be manufactured without needing a distinct chemical step for each part of it, which is what makes a single machinery capable of producing an unlimited variety of products.

---

Q: Most macromolecules are built from a small set of similar monomers, such as the 20 amino acids. What is the critical extra requirement?
A: That the chain is not assembled at random from them. The subunits have to be added in a precise order, and that sequence is what carries the information. A polymer of the right composition but the wrong sequence is not the same molecule in any functional sense.

---

Q: Proteins are called spectacularly versatile. What kinds of job do they do?
A: They act as enzymes, catalysing the bond-making and bond-breaking the cell needs, including every reaction by which energy is extracted from food. They build structural components, as tubulin does in self-assembling into microtubules and histones do in compacting DNA into chromosomes. They serve as signalling devices wired into networks that control cell function. And they act as molecular motors producing force and movement, as myosin does in muscle.

---

Q: The covalent bonds in a polymer backbone allow rotation, so in principle a macromolecule could take an almost unlimited number of conformations. Why does it not?
![A folded protein or RNA molecule losing its defined conformation and becoming a flexible chain when noncovalent bonds are disrupted](assets/img/fig-2-10.png)
A: Because many weak noncovalent bonds form between different parts of the same molecule. Formed in sufficient numbers, they make the chain strongly prefer one particular conformation, and which conformation that is follows from the linear sequence of monomers. Disrupt those noncovalent bonds and the molecule reverts to a flexible chain and loses its biological activity, which shows that the shape rather than the chain itself is what carries the function.

---

Q: Noncovalent binding between macromolecules has two properties that would be hard to get any other way. What are they?
A: Specificity and tunable strength. Strong binding requires close multipoint contact, so a macromolecule can pick out just one of the many thousands of molecule types present in a cell by shape alone. And because the strength depends on how many noncovalent bonds form, interactions of almost any affinity are possible, including deliberately weak ones that allow rapid dissociation when that is what the job needs.

---

Q: Roughly how many macromolecules make up a ribosome?
![Small molecules, proteins and a ribosome drawn approximately to scale to show how covalent and noncovalent assembly build up in size](assets/img/fig-2-11.png)
A: About 90 protein and RNA molecules, held together as a complex by noncovalent interactions. It is an example of the general pattern: small molecules are covalently linked into macromolecules, and macromolecules then assemble noncovalently into intricate machines with multiple moving parts, capable of tasks such as DNA replication and protein synthesis.

---

Q: What is an **enzyme**, and how does a metabolic pathway arise from a set of them?
![A set of enzymes acting in series, each catalysing one reaction, converting molecule A through to molecule F](assets/img/fig-2-13.png)
A: A biological catalyst, almost always a protein, that accelerates just one of the many reactions a given molecule might undergo, and is left unchanged by it. Reactions get linked in series when the product of one becomes the starting material, or **substrate**, for the next, and a chain of such steps is a metabolic pathway. Long linear pathways are then linked to one another into a maze of interconnected reactions. RNA catalysts, the ribozymes, also exist.

---

Q+: Why does the cell need catalysts at all, rather than letting the reactions happen?
A: Because at the temperatures inside a cell those reactions would proceed at an appreciable rate only if it were far hotter. Each reaction needs a specific boost in chemical reactivity, and that requirement is what hands the cell control: since nothing significant happens without a catalyst, deciding which catalysts are present decides which chemistry occurs.

---

Q: Distinguish **catabolic** from **anabolic** pathways.
![Catabolic pathways breaking food molecules down to yield building blocks and useful energy, feeding anabolic pathways that build the molecules of the cell](assets/img/fig-2-14.png)
A: Catabolic pathways break foodstuffs down into smaller molecules, generating both a useful form of energy and some of the small molecules the cell needs as building blocks. Anabolic, or biosynthetic, pathways use those small molecules plus the harnessed energy to build the many other molecules that form the cell. Together the two opposing streams make up the cell's **metabolism**.

---

Q+: Why does an organism need to consume so much more mass in food than it produces in new material?
A: Because a major portion of the energy stored in the chemical bonds of food is dissipated as heat rather than captured. Catabolism has to supply both the building blocks and the energy for biosynthesis, and the losses along the way mean the mass of food required is much greater than the mass of molecules that anabolism can produce from it.

---

Q: State the **second law of thermodynamics** in the two forms the book uses.
A: In the universe, or in any isolated system, the degree of disorder always increases. Equivalently, in terms of probability, systems change spontaneously toward the arrangements that have the greatest probability.

---

Q+: Why does a box of 100 coins, all heads-up, tend toward 50 heads and 50 tails when shaken?
A: Because there is an enormous number of distinct arrangements of individual coins that produce a 50-50 result, and exactly one that keeps every coin heads-up. The mixture is overwhelmingly the more probable outcome, and that is precisely what we mean by calling it more disordered. Nothing pushes the coins toward the mixture; it is just where the counting leads.

---

Q: What is **entropy**?
A: A quantified measure of the amount of disorder in a system: the greater the disorder, the greater the entropy. It lets the second law be stated as systems changing spontaneously toward arrangements of greater entropy.

---

Q: Living cells generate order, and the second law says disorder must always increase. How is that not a contradiction?
![A cell taking in energy and releasing heat, ordering its own molecules while increasing the disorder of the surrounding sea of matter](assets/img/fig-2-16.png)
A: Because a cell is not an isolated system, and the second law applies to the cell plus its surroundings. The cell takes in energy from its environment, as chemical bonds in food, as photons from the Sun, or in some chemosynthetic bacteria from inorganic molecules alone, and uses it to generate internal order. While doing so it converts part of that energy into heat and discharges it. Heat is energy in its most disordered form, the random jostling of molecules, so releasing it increases the disorder of the surroundings by more than the order gained inside. Total entropy still rises.

---

Q+: Tidying a room reverses the drive toward disorder locally. What does the second law guarantee about the cost?
![A room shown before and after becoming disordered, illustrating that reversing the tendency requires an input of energy](assets/img/fig-2-15.png)
A: That the human effort involved releases enough heat to the environment to more than compensate for the reordering of the objects in the room. Local order is always available; it is never free.

---

Q: State the **first law of thermodynamics**, and give an example of energy changing form in a cell.
![Interconversions between forms of energy: a falling brick, formation of water from hydrogen and oxygen, a motor driven by chemical energy, and photosynthesis](assets/img/fig-2-17.png)
A: Energy can be converted from one form to another but cannot be created or destroyed, so the total amount is always the same. An animal cell takes in food and converts some of the chemical-bond energy in it into the random thermal motion of molecules, which is the heat that keeps our bodies warm. Cells also convert chemical-bond energy directly into the kinetic energy of motor proteins, without the intermediate conversion to electrical energy that a man-made appliance needs.

---

Q: What distinguishes a cell's metabolism from a fire, given that both release heat by oxidizing organic molecules?
A: Coupling. A cell derives no benefit from heat as such, so the heat-generating reactions have to be directly linked to the processes that generate molecular order. That tight coupling of controlled burning to the creation of order is exactly what a fire lacks, and it is what allows cells to maintain an island of order in a universe tending toward chaos.

---

Q: Why is CO₂ and H₂O what a cell ends up with when it extracts energy from food?
A: Because in the presence of oxygen those are the most energetically stable forms of carbon and hydrogen. Earth's atmosphere contains a great deal of oxygen, so a cell can obtain energy from sugars and other organic molecules by letting their carbon and hydrogen atoms combine with oxygen, a process called **aerobic respiration**. The energy comes from moving downhill toward the stable forms.

---

Q: In what sense are photosynthesis and respiration complementary?
![Photosynthesis converting sunlight into chemical bond energy in sugars while releasing oxygen, and respiration using that oxygen to release the energy again](assets/img/fig-2-18.png)
A: They run the same transaction in opposite directions. Photosynthesis converts electromagnetic energy in sunlight into chemical-bond energy in sugars, taking carbon from atmospheric CO₂ and hydrogen from water and releasing O₂ as a by-product. Aerobic respiration uses that O₂ to make CO₂ again from the same carbon atoms, releasing the chemical-bond energy for the organism that respires. The traffic runs both ways: CO₂ fixed by a leaf today may have been released yesterday by an animal, a fungus or a bacterium decomposing dead matter.

---

Q+: Which came first on Earth, photosynthesis or respiration?
A: Photosynthesis. The first cells are thought to have had neither, but photosynthesis must have preceded respiration because strong evidence indicates that billions of years of photosynthesis were needed before O₂ had accumulated enough to create an oxygen-rich atmosphere. Earth's atmosphere currently contains 21% O₂.

---

Q: Carbon atoms cycle through the biosphere rather than being consumed. Trace the loop.
![Carbon cycling through the biosphere: fixed by photosynthesis, passed along food chains, and returned to the atmosphere by respiration and by burning fossil fuels](assets/img/fig-2-19.png)
A: Photosynthesis in bacteria, algae and plants incorporates atmospheric carbon into organic molecules. Those pass to animals, to microorganisms, and into organic material in soil and oceans. CO₂ returns to the atmosphere when organic molecules are oxidized during respiration, or when humans burn fossil fuels. Nitrogen, phosphorus and sulfur move between the living and nonliving worlds in comparable cycles.

---

Q: What do **oxidation** and **reduction** actually mean?
![Formation of a polar covalent bond in which one atom gains a greater share of the electrons, and the ladder from methane through methanol, formaldehyde and formic acid to carbon dioxide](assets/img/fig-2-20.png)
A: Oxidation means the removal of electrons and reduction means their addition, regardless of whether any oxygen is involved. Fe²⁺ is oxidized when it loses an electron to become Fe³⁺, and a chlorine atom is reduced when it gains one to become Cl⁻. Because electrons are neither created nor destroyed in a reaction, the two always happen together: if one molecule is reduced, another is oxidized in the same step.

---

Q+: Two atoms form a polar covalent bond, and atom 2 ends up with the greater share of the electrons. Which atom has been oxidized?
- [x] Atom 1, which acquires a partial positive charge
- [ ] Atom 2, which acquires a partial negative charge
- [ ] Both, since they are now bonded
- [ ] Neither, because no electron was fully transferred
A: Atom 1. It gave up more than its equal share of the electrons, so the positive charge of its nucleus is no longer fully balanced and it carries a partial positive charge (δ⁺): that is oxidation. Atom 2 gained the greater share, carries a partial negative charge (δ⁻), and is reduced. The terms apply to partial shifts of electrons within a covalent bond, not only to complete transfers.

---

Q+: Going down the ladder from methane to methanol to formaldehyde to formic acid to carbon dioxide, is the carbon being oxidized or reduced, and how can you tell at a glance?
A: Oxidized, progressively, as each covalently bonded hydrogen is replaced by oxygen and electrons shift away from the carbon. The quick test for any organic molecule is to count C-H bonds: reduction is happening if the number of C-H bonds increases, and oxidation if it decreases. Every step down this particular ladder is energetically favourable under the conditions inside a cell.

---

Q: Why is a **gain** of electrons called "reduction", which sounds like the opposite?
A: The word predates any knowledge of electrons. Reduction reactions were originally ones that liberated oxygen, as when metals are extracted from their ores by heating, and that made the sample lighter. The name records the loss of mass, and it stuck after the underlying electron transfer was understood.

---

Q: When a molecule in a cell picks up an electron it usually picks up something else at the same time. What, and why?
A: A proton, since protons are freely available in water. The net effect of $\text{A} + e^- + \text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{AH}$ is to add a whole hydrogen atom. Such hydrogenation reactions still count as reductions, and the reverse dehydrogenation reactions as oxidations, even though a proton is involved rather than an electron alone.

---

Q: A book does not burst into flames even though CO₂ and H₂O are the more stable forms of its carbon and hydrogen. What is stopping it?
![Energy diagram showing the activation energy barrier between a reactant and a lower-energy product, and how a catalyst lowers that barrier](assets/img/fig-2-21.png)
A: An activation-energy barrier. The molecules sit in a relatively stable state and cannot reach the lower-energy state without first being kicked over a barrier, and that kick costs energy even though the overall reaction releases it. For a burning book the kick comes from a lighted match; for molecules in the watery interior of a cell it comes from an unusually energetic random collision with surrounding molecules.

---

Q+: Why does the reverse reaction, from product back to reactant, happen so much more rarely?
A: Because its activation energy is much larger. Starting from the lower-energy product, the barrier to be climbed is the whole gap up to the transition state rather than the smaller step up from the reactant. Activation energies are always positive in both directions, but the total energy change for the favourable direction is negative, which is what makes it the spontaneous one.

---

Q: What does it mean for a reaction to be **energetically favourable**, and what does burning paper lose?
A: Reactions proceed spontaneously only in the direction that leads to a loss of **free energy**, meaning energy that can be harnessed to do work or drive other reactions. That is the downhill direction. When paper burns, its chemical energy is not lost from the universe, since energy cannot be destroyed, but it is irretrievably dispersed into the chaotic thermal motion of molecules, and the atoms of the paper are scattered and disordered. What has been lost is the orderliness with which the energy and the molecules were stored.

---

Q: How does an enzyme make a reaction go faster?
![Graph of the range of energies in a population of substrate molecules, with the activation energy barriers for the uncatalysed and enzyme-catalysed reactions marked](assets/img/fig-2-22.png)
A: It binds one or more substrate molecules and holds them in a way that greatly reduces the activation energy of one particular reaction they can undergo. At any instant a population of identical substrate molecules has a spread of energies, coming from collisions that make them jiggle, vibrate and spin, and only those above the barrier can react. Lowering the barrier means a much larger proportion of ordinary random collisions is now enough to push a substrate over it.

---

Q+: Raising the temperature also increases the number of molecules with enough energy. Why is that not equivalent?
A: Because heat is indiscriminate. Raising the temperature speeds up every reaction at once, whereas an enzyme lowers the barrier for one specific reaction and leaves the rest alone. Selectivity, not speed by itself, is what lets a cell control its chemistry.

---

Q: Alberts notes that some enzymes speed up reactions by factors of $10^{14}$ or more, without saying which. Which enzymes actually reach that, and what is the record?
A: The classic measurements are Radzicka and Wolfenden's, comparing the catalysed rate with the uncatalysed rate for the same reaction.

| Enzyme | Rate enhancement |
|---|---:|
| OMP decarboxylase | $1.4 \times 10^{17}$ |
| Staphylococcal nuclease | $5.6 \times 10^{14}$ |
| Adenosine deaminase | $2.1 \times 10^{12}$ |
| Carbonic anhydrase | $7.7 \times 10^{6}$ |

Staphylococcal nuclease is the one sitting right at Alberts' $10^{14}$. The record holder is orotidine 5'-monophosphate decarboxylase, a step in pyrimidine synthesis, and the comparison there is worth stating plainly: the uncatalysed reaction has an estimated half-life of 78 million years, and the enzyme does it in milliseconds. It manages that with no metal ion and no cofactor, purely through contacts in the active site.

Q: Can an enzyme change which way a reaction goes?
![Diagram showing that catalysed and uncatalysed versions of a reaction reach the same equilibrium point, with the catalysed one arriving faster](assets/img/fig-2-23.png)
A: No. Lowering the activation energy for Y → X necessarily lowers it for X → Y by exactly the same amount, so both directions are accelerated by the same factor and the equilibrium point is untouched. A catalysed and an uncatalysed reaction reach exactly the same equilibrium; the catalysed one just gets there much faster. No matter how much an enzyme speeds a reaction, it cannot change its direction.

---

Q: If enzymes cannot change direction or equilibrium, how do they end up determining what happens in a cell?
![A substrate converted step by step along one specific route, where several reactions are energetically favourable at each step but only one is catalysed](assets/img/fig-2-24.png)
A: By selection among the possibilities. At each step, several different reactions may be energetically favourable, but each enzyme selectively lowers the activation energy of only one of them. Since nothing much happens without catalysis, the set of enzymes present decides which of the available downhill paths each molecule actually takes. Sets of enzymes thereby direct every molecule in the cell along a particular reaction pathway.

---

Q: What is an **active site**, and what is the cycle an enzyme goes through?
![An enzyme binding substrate at its active site to form an enzyme-substrate complex, converting it to an enzyme-product complex, and releasing the product](assets/img/fig-2-25.png)
A: A pocket or groove in the enzyme's unique shape into which only particular substrates fit. Substrate binds there to form an enzyme-substrate complex, the reaction occurs, giving an enzyme-product complex, and the product is then released. The enzyme itself is unchanged and can immediately bind more substrate, so it works over and over again.

---

Q: An enzyme may process thousands of substrate molecules a second, which means binding a new one within a fraction of a millisecond, yet both are present in small numbers. How do they find each other so fast?
A: Because molecular motion driven by heat is enormously fast at this scale. It comes in three kinds: translational motion from place to place, vibration of covalently linked atoms back and forth, and rotation. All three help bring interacting surfaces together. A large globular protein tumbles about its axis roughly a million times per second, and translational motion makes molecules explore the space inside the cell efficiently by diffusion, so every molecule collides with a huge number of others each second.

---

Q: In a random walk, how does the distance covered relate to the time taken?
![A molecule following a random walk through solution as a result of continual collisions](assets/img/fig-2-26.png)
A: The average net distance from the starting point, as the crow flies, is proportional to the **square root** of the time. If a molecule covers 1 μm in 1 second on average, it needs 4 seconds for 2 μm and 100 seconds for 10 μm. Distance does not accumulate in proportion to time, because each step is as likely to undo progress as to add to it.

---

Q: The interior of a cell is extremely crowded. Does that stop small molecules getting about?
![The crowded interior of a cell showing only the macromolecules, drawn to scale](assets/img/fig-2-27.png)
A: Barely. Experiments injecting fluorescent dyes and other labelled molecules show that small organic molecules diffuse through the watery gel of the cytosol nearly as fast as through plain water: about one fifth of a second on average to cover 10 μm. Since a typical animal cell is around 15 μm across, diffusion is an efficient way for small molecules to cross it. Macromolecules are slowed considerably more, partly because they interact with the many other macromolecules around them.

---

Q: An abundant substrate might sit at 0.5 mM, which is one molecule for every $10^5$ water molecules. How often does an enzyme's active site meet one?
A: About 500,000 times a second. Pure water is 55.5 M, so the substrate really is heavily outnumbered, and yet the active site is bombarded by roughly half a million random collisions with it every second. At a tenfold lower substrate concentration the rate drops to 50,000 per second. A collision between the active site and a matching substrate surface often forms the enzyme-substrate complex immediately, and bond making or breaking then follows extremely rapidly, which is why observed catalytic rates are less astonishing than they first appear.

---

Q: What stops an enzyme from binding the wrong molecules, given that it collides with everything?
A: The difference in how long the association lasts. Two molecules held by noncovalent bonds stay together until random thermal motion pulls them apart, and the stronger the binding the slower that dissociation. When two colliding molecules have poorly matching surfaces they form few noncovalent bonds, so the total energy of association is negligible against thermal motion and they separate as fast as they met. Incorrect pairings are not prevented from forming, they are simply not sustained.

---

Q: What is **free energy**, and what does its change tell you?
![Diagram distinguishing an energetically favourable reaction with negative delta G from an unfavourable one with positive delta G](assets/img/fig-2-28.png)
A: Free energy, $G$, is the energy available to do work, such as driving chemical reactions. Only its change matters, and the free-energy change $\Delta G$ is a direct measure of the amount of disorder created in the universe when a reaction takes place. Energetically favourable reactions are by definition those that decrease free energy, so they have a negative $\Delta G$ and increase the disorder of the universe. A compressed spring relaxing and releasing its stored elastic energy as heat is a macroscopic example; salt dissolving in water is a microscopic one.

---

Q+: Joining two amino acids into a peptide bond has a positive $\Delta G$. How does a cell manage it?
![An energetically unfavourable reaction driven by being coupled to a favourable one, so that the net free-energy change is negative](assets/img/fig-2-29.png)
A: By coupling. An unfavourable reaction creates order in the universe and cannot proceed on its own, but it can be joined to a second reaction whose $\Delta G$ is negative enough that the net change for the pair is negative. Enzymes cannot make water run uphill by themselves; what they can do is directly couple a reaction that releases energy to one that produces biological order.

---

Q: $\Delta G$ depends on more than the energy stored in the individual molecules. What else, and why?
A: The concentrations of the molecules in the mixture. $\Delta G$ measures how far a reaction moves the universe toward a more probable state, and that depends on where you start. Returning to the coins: a jiggling box holding 90 heads and 10 tails will very probably flip a head to a tail, while a box with 10 heads and 90 tails is much less likely to. In the same way, a large excess of Y over X drives Y → X, and as the ratio of Y to X rises, $\Delta G$ for that direction becomes more negative.

---

Q: Why is a **standard** free-energy change, $\Delta G°$, needed at all, and how does it relate to $\Delta G$?
A: Because $\Delta G$ shifts with the concentrations present at the time, which makes it useless for comparing different kinds of reaction. $\Delta G°$ is the free-energy change under a defined standard condition, with the concentration of every reactant set to 1 mole/liter, so it depends only on the intrinsic character of the molecules involved. For the reaction Y → X:
$$\Delta G = \Delta G° + RT \ln \frac{[X]}{[Y]}$$
where concentrations are in moles/liter and $RT$ is the gas constant times absolute temperature. At 37°C, $RT = 2.58$ kJ mol⁻¹, and a mole is $6 \times 10^{23}$ molecules. Tabulated $\Delta G°$ values for the important metabolic reactions, combined with measured metabolite concentrations, make it possible to predict the course of most biological reactions quantitatively.
Tags: thermodynamics

---

Q: What is happening at chemical **equilibrium**, and what is $\Delta G$ there?
![Diagram of a reaction reaching equilibrium, where forward and backward fluxes of molecules become equal and opposite](assets/img/fig-2-30.png)
A: The forward and backward fluxes of reacting molecules are equal and opposite, so there is no net change in the ratio of reactant to product, and $\Delta G$ is zero in both directions. It is not that the reaction has stopped. Individual molecules keep converting both ways; the two rates have simply come into balance.

---

Q+: Why does a favourable reaction slow to a halt rather than running to completion?
A: Because the concentrations shift as it proceeds. Product accumulates and substrate is consumed, so $[X]/[Y]$ grows, and the $RT \ln([X]/[Y])$ term becomes increasingly positive, making the initially negative $\Delta G$ less and less negative. Eventually the concentration effect exactly balances the push given by $\Delta G°$, $\Delta G$ reaches zero, and equilibrium is attained.

---

Q: How are the equilibrium constant $K$ and the standard free-energy change related?
A: For Y → X, $K = [X]/[Y]$ at equilibrium. Setting $\Delta G = 0$ in the free-energy equation gives
$$\Delta G° = -RT \ln K$$
which at 37°C becomes $\Delta G° = -2.58 \ln K$, or in base 10 logarithms $\Delta G° = -5.94 \log K$. The more energetically favourable a reaction, the more product accumulates before it reaches equilibrium.
Tags: thermodynamics

---

Q+: How much free energy does it take to change the equilibrium constant by a factor of 10, and why is that number worth remembering?
A: 5.94 kJ/mole at 37°C. It is worth remembering because it is roughly the free energy available from a **single hydrogen bond**, which puts the whole scale in perspective: one hydrogen bond's worth of binding energy shifts an equilibrium tenfold. A standard free-energy change of $-17.8$ kJ/mole gives $K = 1000$, so a thousandfold excess of product at equilibrium.
Tags: thermodynamics

---

Q: For a reaction with two reactants and two products, A + B → C + D, what is the equilibrium constant?
A: $$K = \frac{[C][D]}{[A][B]}$$
The concentrations are multiplied rather than added because the rate of the forward reaction depends on A and B colliding, and the rate of the backward reaction on C and D colliding.
Tags: thermodynamics

---

Q: Two sequential reactions have $\Delta G°$ values of $+5$ and $-13$ kJ/mole. What is the $\Delta G°$ of the pair, and what does that make possible?
A: $-8$ kJ/mole, because the free-energy changes of coupled reactions add. Under appropriate conditions the unfavourable first step can therefore be driven by the favourable second one, provided the second follows the first. This is not a special case: several reactions in the long pathway converting sugars to CO₂ and H₂O have positive $\Delta G°$ values, and the pathway runs anyway because the total for the sequence is large and negative.
Tags: thermodynamics

---

Q+: Why is a sequential pathway not a general enough solution?
A: Because often the cell wants X → Y and nothing further, not X → Y → Z. Chaining reactions works only when you are content for the intermediate to be consumed by the next step. A more general coupling mechanism is needed, and that is what activated carrier molecules provide.

---

Q: What is an **activated carrier molecule**, and why does the cell need them?
![Activated carriers acting as shuttles that link catabolism to the energy-requiring reactions of anabolism](assets/img/fig-2-31.png)
A: A small molecule containing one or more energy-rich covalent bonds, used to store the energy released by oxidizing food until it can be spent. They are needed because energy release and energy use happen at different places and times. Carriers diffuse rapidly through the cell, carrying bond energy from where it is generated to where biosynthesis needs it, storing it either as a readily transferable chemical group or as electrons held at high energy. They can therefore serve as a source of both energy and chemical groups. For historical reasons they are also called coenzymes.

---

Q+: Which are the most important activated carriers?
A: ATP, and the two closely related molecules NADH and NADPH.

---

Q: Rocks falling from a cliff normally waste all their energy as heat. What does the paddle-wheel version of that picture illustrate?
![A mechanical model in which falling rocks either produce heat alone, or turn a paddle wheel that lifts a bucket of water whose stored energy can drive machines](assets/img/fig-2-32.png)
A: Coupling. Falling rocks are an analogy for a spontaneous reaction such as direct oxidation of glucose to CO₂ and H₂O, which yields heat and nothing else. Arrange things so the rocks can only reach the ground by turning a paddle wheel that lifts a bucket of water, and part of the energy is captured in a reusable form that can drive many different machines. The rocks now hit the ground more slowly and correspondingly less energy is wasted as heat. In the cell, enzymes play the part of the paddle wheel, and the raised bucket is the activated carrier molecule.

---

Q+: When an enzyme couples oxidation of food to making an activated carrier, what happens to the heat?
A: The heat released by the oxidation is reduced by exactly the amount of energy stored in the energy-rich bonds of the carrier. Nothing is created: energy that would have been dissipated is diverted into the carrier instead, which picks up a packet of energy the right size to power a reaction elsewhere in the cell.

---

Q: How is ATP made and spent?
![ATP synthesis by phosphorylation of ADP and its hydrolysis back to ADP and inorganic phosphate, showing the phosphoanhydride bonds](assets/img/fig-2-33.png)
A: ATP is synthesized by coupling a highly favourable reaction to the unfavourable phosphorylation of ADP, adding a phosphate group. When energy is needed, ATP gives up its packet through the favourable hydrolysis back to ADP and inorganic phosphate, and the regenerated ADP is available for another round. It works as a currency: a convenient, versatile store that can drive a huge variety of reactions, in the way a raised bucket of water can drive many different hydraulic machines.

---

Q+: The two outermost phosphates of ATP are held by "high-energy" bonds. What actually makes their hydrolysis release so much free energy?
A: Several things at once. Removing the terminal phosphate relieves an unfavourable repulsion between adjacent negative charges. The inorganic phosphate ion that is released is then stabilized by resonance, and further stabilized by favourable hydrogen bonding with water. The phrase "high-energy bond" is really shorthand for the whole balance being strongly downhill, rather than for unusual energy sitting in the bond itself.

---

Q: How much usable energy does hydrolysing the terminal phosphate of ATP actually yield in a cell?
A: Between 46 and 54 kJ/mole, depending on intracellular conditions. That is noticeably more than the standard value of $\Delta G° = -30.5$ kJ/mole, and the reason is instructive: the cell keeps the ratio of ATP to its products ADP and phosphate very high, which pushes $\Delta G$ well below $\Delta G°$. The quoted standard number understates what ATP is worth in a living cell.
Tags: thermodynamics

---

Q: Many coupled reactions work by transferring the terminal phosphate of ATP onto another molecule. Why is that transfer favourable?
![A phosphate group transferred from ATP to a hydroxyl group on another molecule, converting a phosphoanhydride bond into a phosphoester bond](assets/img/fig-2-34.png)
A: Because an energy-rich phosphoanhydride bond in ATP is exchanged for a phosphoester bond in the product, which is a step downhill, giving the reaction a large negative $\Delta G$. Reactions of this type appear in phospholipid synthesis, in the first steps of sugar catabolism, and in many other pathways.

---

Q+: Beyond biosynthesis, what else does ATP pay for?
A: It supplies the energy for many of the pumps that transport substances into and out of the cell, and it powers the molecular motors that let muscle cells contract and let nerve cells move materials from one end of a long axon to the other.

---

Q: A typical biosynthetic condensation, B-H + A-OH → A-B + H₂O, is energetically unfavourable. How does ATP hydrolysis force it through?
![The two-step activation and condensation mechanism, and the synthesis of glutamine from glutamic acid and ammonia by glutamine synthetase](assets/img/fig-2-35.png)
A: In two steps, with a high-energy intermediate in between.
1. **Activation**: A-OH + ATP → A-O-PO₃ + ADP
2. **Condensation**: B-H + A-O-PO₃ → A-B + phosphate

Net: A-OH + ATP + B-H → A-B + ADP + phosphate. The point is that the unfavourable condensation is never attempted directly; ATP first raises A-OH to a higher-energy form that will react with B-H willingly.

---

Q+: Work the glutamine example through with the numbers.
A: Glutamic acid takes the part of A-OH and is phosphorylated to a high-energy intermediate, which then reacts with ammonia in the part of B-H to give glutamine. Both steps happen on the surface of the same enzyme, glutamine synthetase. Synthesizing glutamine from glutamic acid plus NH₃ has $\Delta G°$ of $+14.2$ kJ/mole, while ATP hydrolysis supplies $-30.5$ kJ/mole, and because the favourable change is the larger in magnitude the coupled pathway runs.

---

Q: What do NAD⁺ and NADP⁺ carry, and in what form?
![NADPH being produced as two hydrogen atoms are removed from a substrate, with the structures of NADP+ and NADPH](assets/img/fig-2-36.png)
A: Electrons held at a high energy level, together with hydrogen. In an enzyme-catalysed oxidation, two hydrogen atoms are removed from a substrate; both electrons plus just one of the hydrogens are added to the nicotinamide ring, and the second hydrogen leaves as a proton in solution. So each carrier picks up a **hydride ion**, written H⁻, meaning one proton and two electrons, becoming NADH or NADPH. The substrate is oxidized and the carrier reduced.

---

Q+: Why does NADPH give up its hydride ion so readily?
A: Because the nicotinamide ring can reach a more stable arrangement of electrons without it, so the transfer comes with a large negative free-energy change. It is an effective hydride donor for the same reason ATP is an effective phosphate donor: in both cases handing the group on is downhill.

---

Q: NADPH and NADH differ by a single phosphate group that sits far from the site of electron transfer. If it does not change the chemistry, what is it for?
![Cholesterol biosynthesis using NADPH as a reducing agent, and a comparison of the redox behaviour of the NADPH and NADH pairs](assets/img/fig-2-37.png)
A: It changes the shape, which is enough for NADPH and NADH to bind as substrates to completely different sets of enzymes. That separation is the point: it lets the cell run two sets of electron-transfer reactions independently. NADPH works mainly with anabolic enzymes, supplying high-energy electrons for building energy-rich molecules, while NADH acts as an intermediate in the catabolic reactions that generate ATP by oxidizing food.

---

Q+: The cell holds the NAD⁺:NADH ratio high and the NADP⁺:NADPH ratio low. What does that accomplish?
A: It supplies plenty of NAD⁺ to act as an oxidizing agent and plenty of NADPH to act as a reducing agent, which is exactly what their respective roles in catabolism and anabolism require. Because the ratios differ, the redox potential of each pair differs too, making NADPH a much stronger electron donor than NADH, and NAD⁺ a much better electron acceptor than NADP⁺. The two are generated by different pathways and regulated independently.

---

Q: What does **acetyl CoA** carry, and how?
![The structure of acetyl CoA, with the sulfur atom forming a thioester bond to acetate](assets/img/fig-2-38.png)
A: A readily transferable acetyl group, held in a thioester linkage to a sulfur atom. Because that is a high-energy linkage, hydrolysing it releases a large amount of free energy and the acetate can be handed on easily, which makes acetyl CoA the standard way of adding two-carbon units in the biosynthesis of larger molecules.

---

Q: In activated carriers the transferable group is only a small part of the molecule. What is the rest of it doing, and why is it suspicious?
A: The bulk of the molecule is a handle that lets specific enzymes recognize the carrier. What is striking is that this handle very often contains a nucleotide derivative, usually adenosine diphosphate, even though nothing about carrying an acetyl group or a hydride ion requires a nucleotide. The suspicion is that these carriers are relics of the RNA world: if the main catalysts of early life were RNA molecules, a nucleotide portion would have been useful for binding the carrier to a ribozyme, and the handle simply stayed once proteins took over catalysis.

---

Q: Which group does each of the major activated carriers transfer?
A: ATP transfers phosphate. NADH, NADPH and FADH₂ transfer electrons and hydrogen. Acetyl CoA transfers two-carbon acetyl groups. Other carriers transfer methyl, carboxyl or glucose groups for biosynthesis.
![The structure of FADH2 with its hydrogen-carrying atoms highlighted, and its formation from FAD](assets/img/fig-2-39.png)

---

Q+: Where does the energy in all these carriers ultimately come from?
A: From the catabolic reactions that generate ATP. The activated carriers are themselves generated in reactions coupled to ATP hydrolysis, so whatever group they carry, the energy that makes it available for biosynthesis traces back to the oxidation of food.

---

Q: Biotin is a B-complex vitamin that acts as an activated carrier. What does it carry, and to what?
![Carboxylated biotin transferring a carboxyl group to pyruvate to produce oxaloacetate, in a reaction catalysed by pyruvate carboxylase](assets/img/fig-2-40.png)
A: A carboxyl group. Carboxylated biotin is used by pyruvate carboxylase to transfer that group onto pyruvate, producing oxaloacetate, which the citric acid cycle needs. Other enzymes use biotin to carry carboxyl groups to different acceptors. Making carboxylated biotin in the first place requires energy derived from ATP, which is the general pattern for activated carriers.

---

Q: Nucleic acids, proteins and polysaccharides are all built by condensation driven by nucleoside triphosphate hydrolysis. Why is there no phosphate left in most of the products?
![Synthesis of a polysaccharide, a nucleic acid and a protein, each by a condensation reaction in which water is lost](assets/img/fig-2-41.png)
A: Because the phosphate is spent on activation rather than incorporated. The hydroxyl group that will be removed in the condensation is first put into a high-energy linkage with another molecule, exactly as in glutamine synthesis, and the phosphate leaves again when the condensation happens. Only in the nucleic acids does phosphate remain, because there it is part of the backbone. For proteins and polysaccharides the mechanisms are more elaborate, needing more than one high-energy intermediate to generate the bond that is finally broken in the condensation step.

---

Q: ATP hydrolysis in a cell provides between $-46$ and $-54$ kJ/mole. What does the cell do when a reaction needs more than that?
![The alternative route of ATP hydrolysis, forming AMP and pyrophosphate, which is then itself hydrolysed](assets/img/fig-2-42.png)
A: It changes the route of hydrolysis. Instead of going to ADP and phosphate, ATP is cleaved to AMP and pyrophosphate, and the pyrophosphate is then hydrolysed in a second step. The two steps together make about $-100$ kJ/mole available, roughly twice the usual amount. Synthesis of nucleic acids from nucleoside triphosphates is an important reaction driven this way.

---

Q+: In polynucleotide synthesis, what role does the pyrophosphate play?
![Synthesis of a polynucleotide, in which an activated nucleoside triphosphate reacts with the growing chain and releases pyrophosphate](assets/img/fig-2-43.png)
A: It is the leaving group whose subsequent destruction makes the reaction hard to reverse. A nucleotide is activated by transfer of terminal phosphates from two ATP molecules, giving a nucleoside triphosphate that exists free in solution until it reacts with the growing end of the chain, releasing pyrophosphate. Hydrolysing that pyrophosphate to inorganic phosphate is itself highly favourable, and it is that second step which helps drive the overall reaction toward synthesis.

---

Q: Repetitive condensation can be arranged in two ways depending on where the high-energy bond sits. What are they, and which macromolecules use each?
![Polymer-end activation compared with direct-monomer activation for the condensation reactions that build biological polymers](assets/img/fig-2-44.png)
A: In **polymer-end activation** the reactive bond is carried on the end of the growing polymer and has to be regenerated every time a monomer is added, so each monomer brings with it the bond that will be used to add the *next* one. In **direct-monomer activation** each monomer carries the reactive bond used for its own addition. DNA, RNA and some simple polysaccharides are made by direct-monomer activation; proteins are made by polymer-end activation.

---

Q+: Which mechanism does protein synthesis use?
- [ ] Direct-monomer activation, as for DNA and RNA
- [x] Polymer-end activation
- [ ] Neither, since peptide bonds form without activation
- [ ] Both, alternating between them
A: Polymer-end activation. The reactive bond sits at the growing end of the chain, so each amino acid added brings the bond that will attach the following one.

---

Q: Before food can be used, it has to be taken apart. What does digestion produce from each class of food molecule?
A: Proteins are broken down into amino acids, polysaccharides into sugars, and fats into fatty acids and glycerol. Those small molecules then enter the cytosol, where their gradual oxidation begins.

---

Q: Why does a cell oxidize sugar in many small steps rather than all at once?
![Direct burning of sugar in a nonliving system compared with the stepwise enzyme-catalysed oxidation of sugar in a cell](assets/img/fig-2-45.png)
A: Because a single step would release far more energy at once than could be captured for anything useful, exactly as burning does. Splitting the oxidation into many enzyme-catalysed steps transfers the free energy in conveniently sized packets to carrier molecules, most often ATP and NADH. Heat is still released, enough to disorder the universe sufficiently to keep the whole pathway favourable.

---

Q+: How much total free energy does complete oxidation of glucose to CO₂ and H₂O release, and does the stepwise route change it?
A: 2880 kJ/mole, and no. The total is exactly the same whether the sugar is burned in a fire or oxidized through the cell's pathway. What the stepwise route changes is how much of that energy is captured in a useful form rather than dissipated immediately as heat.

---

Q: What does **glycolysis** accomplish, and what is notable about its requirements?
A: It converts one glucose molecule of six carbons into two molecules of pyruvate of three carbons each, producing ATP without any involvement of molecular oxygen. It runs in the cytosol of most cells, including many anaerobic microorganisms, and it probably evolved early in the history of life, before photosynthetic organisms had put oxygen into the atmosphere.
Tags: glycolysis

---

Q: Walk through the shape of glycolysis. How many steps, and what is the net yield?
![Outline of the ten steps of glycolysis, showing the energy-investment phase, the cleavage of a six-carbon sugar into two three-carbon sugars, and the energy-generation phase](assets/img/fig-2-46.png)
A: Ten separate reactions, each producing a different sugar intermediate and each catalysed by a different enzyme. Two ATP are hydrolysed in the early energy-investment phase and four are produced later, so the net gain is **2 ATP** and **2 NADH** per glucose. Step 4 cleaves the six-carbon sugar into two three-carbon sugars, which is why the number of molecules doubles from that point on, and step 5 isomerizes one of those products into a second molecule of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate. Step 6 begins the energy-generation phase. It is also known as the Embden-Meyerhof pathway.
Tags: glycolysis

---

Q+: Piecing the pathway together in the 1930s was a major triumph of biochemistry. What convention in the enzyme names helps you read it?
A: Most enzymes are named with the suffix "-ase" attached to the type of reaction they catalyse, as in isomerase and dehydrogenase. The names tell you what chemistry is happening at each step rather than what the substrate is.
Tags: glycolysis

---

Q: Where does the oxidation in glycolysis actually happen, and where does the energy go?
A: Electrons are removed by NAD⁺ from some of the carbons derived from glucose, producing NADH. Some of the energy released by that oxidation drives direct synthesis of ATP from ADP and phosphate, and some stays with the electrons in NADH. In aerobic organisms the NADH then donates its electrons to the electron-transport chain, which regenerates the NAD⁺ that glycolysis needs to keep running.
Tags: glycolysis

---

Q: Steps 6 and 7 of glycolysis are the clearest example in the chapter of coupling. What overall chemical change do they achieve?
![The oxidation of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate coupled to the formation of ATP and NADH in steps 6 and 7 of glycolysis](assets/img/fig-2-47.png)
A: They oxidize an aldehyde to a carboxylic acid, converting glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate into 3-phosphoglycerate. That single oxidation releases enough free energy to make one ATP from ADP, to transfer two electrons and a proton to NAD⁺ to form NADH, and still to release enough heat for the overall reaction to be favourable, with $\Delta G°$ of $-12.5$ kJ/mole.
Tags: glycolysis

---

Q+: Trace the mechanism. How do two enzymes pull that off?
A: Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase first forms a covalent bond between the sugar's aldehyde group and a reactive -SH group on a cysteine side chain of the enzyme, binding NAD⁺ noncovalently alongside. It then oxidizes the aldehyde, transferring a hydride ion to NAD⁺ to give NADH, with part of the released energy stored in NADH and part in the high-energy thioester bond now linking the sugar to the enzyme. An inorganic phosphate ion displaces that thioester bond, producing 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate with a high-energy phosphate bond. Phosphoglycerate kinase then transfers that phosphate to ADP, forming ATP and leaving a free carboxylic acid group.
Tags: glycolysis

---

Q+: What is **substrate-level phosphorylation**?
![Diagram of how a favourable oxidation drives the formation of NADH and a high-energy phosphate bond, which then drives ATP formation](assets/img/fig-2-48.png)
A: The formation of ATP by direct transfer of a phosphate group from a high-energy substrate intermediate, rather than by a membrane-driven process. Both the creation of 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate from inorganic phosphate and the handing of that phosphate to ADP are examples. Steps 6 and 7 are the only reactions in glycolysis that create a high-energy phosphate linkage directly from inorganic phosphate, which is why they account for the entire net yield of the pathway.
Tags: glycolysis

---

Q: Phosphate bonds are not all equally energetic. How do you tell whether a phosphate group will transfer from one molecule to another?
![Molecules formed during glycolysis containing different types of phosphate bonds, ranked by the standard free energy of hydrolysis](assets/img/fig-2-49.png)
A: Compare the standard free-energy change for hydrolysis of the phosphate bond in each, treating hydrolysis as transfer of the phosphate to water. The transfer is favourable when $\Delta G°$ of hydrolysis is more negative for the donor than for the acceptor. That is why a phosphate moves readily from 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate to ADP to form ATP: the donor bond sits higher on the scale than ATP's terminal phosphoanhydride bond.
Tags: glycolysis, thermodynamics

---

Q: What is a **fermentation**, and why does the cell bother?
![Fermentation in a vigorously active muscle cell producing lactate, and fermentation in yeast producing ethanol and carbon dioxide](assets/img/fig-2-50.png)
A: An energy-yielding pathway in which pyruvate and the NADH electrons stay in the cytosol and the pyruvate is converted into a product that is excreted, without oxygen being involved. Yeast used in brewing and breadmaking makes ethanol and CO₂; muscle makes lactate. The reason it matters is not the product but the NADH: giving up its electrons converts it back to NAD⁺, and regenerating NAD⁺ is essential, because without it glycolysis would stall for want of an oxidizing agent.
Tags: glycolysis

---

Q+: For which cells is glycolysis the whole story rather than a prelude?
A: For many anaerobic organisms, which do not use molecular oxygen and can grow and divide without it, glycolysis is the principal source of ATP. Certain animal tissues manage temporarily too: skeletal muscle keeps functioning when oxygen is limited. In most animal and plant cells, by contrast, glycolysis is only the opening stage, and the pyruvate is carried into mitochondria to be converted to CO₂ and acetyl CoA and oxidized fully.
Tags: glycolysis

---

Q: In glycolysis, one molecule of glucose is converted into two molecules of {{pyruvate}}, for a net yield of {{2}} ATP and {{2}} NADH.
A: The gross production is four ATP, but two are spent in the early energy-investment phase, leaving a net of two.
Tags: glycolysis

---

Q: Animals eat periodically and plants cannot photosynthesize at night, yet both need a high ATP/ADP ratio at all times. How do they bridge the gap?
![Fat droplets stained red in the cytoplasm of developing adipocytes, and lipid droplets in yeast cells](assets/img/fig-2-51.png)
A: By converting sugars and fats into storage forms. Animals store fatty acids as water-insoluble **triacylglycerols**, also called triglycerides, in fat droplets held mostly in the cytoplasm of specialized fat cells, the adipocytes. For shorter-term storage they keep glucose subunits in the large branched polysaccharide **glycogen**, as small granules in the cytoplasm of many cells, with the largest stores in liver and muscle.

---

Q: How does a cell get glucose back out of glycogen, and why do liver and muscle behave differently?
![The branched structure of glycogen, an electron micrograph of glycogen granules in a liver cell, and the action of glycogen phosphorylase](assets/img/fig-2-52.png)
A: The enzyme glycogen phosphorylase breaks glycogen down to glucose 1-phosphate, which is rapidly converted to glucose 6-phosphate and fed into glycolysis. Each granule contains both glycogen and the enzymes for making and breaking it, and synthesis and degradation are regulated rapidly according to need. During fasting, liver cells release the resulting glucose into the bloodstream for other cells to use, while muscle cells keep their supplies for themselves.

---

Q: Fat is far more important than glycogen as an energy store in animals. Put numbers on why.
A: Oxidizing a gram of fat releases about twice as much energy as oxidizing a gram of glycogen. On top of that, glycogen binds a great deal of water while fat does not, which produces a sixfold difference in the actual mass needed to store the same energy. The consequence for a person is stark: an average adult stores enough glycogen for about one day of normal activity but enough fat for nearly a month, and carrying that fuel as glycogen instead would add roughly 60 pounds of body weight.

---

Q: Plants make abundant ATP and NADPH by photosynthesis in their chloroplasts. Why can they not simply use it where it is made?
A: Because the chloroplast is isolated from the rest of the cell by a membrane impermeable to both of those carriers, and because many plant cells, those in the roots for instance, have no chloroplasts at all. So sugars are exported from chloroplasts instead, and most of the ATP for general plant metabolism is made in mitochondria, using exactly the same oxidative pathways as in nonphotosynthetic organisms.

---

Q+: What do chloroplasts do with the surplus during a good day, and where does it go?
![Electron micrograph of a chloroplast showing starch granules and fat droplets synthesized inside the organelle](assets/img/fig-2-53.png)
A: They convert some of the sugars they make into fats and into **starch**, a polymer of glucose analogous to animal glycogen but with many fewer branch points. Both are stored inside the chloroplast until darkness, when they are oxidized for energy.

---

Q+: Why are seeds such a rich food source for animals?
![Corn, nuts and peas, seeds containing rich stores of starch and fat](assets/img/fig-2-54.png)
A: Because the embryo inside has to live on stored energy for a prolonged period until it germinates and grows leaves that can harvest sunlight. Seeds therefore often contain especially large amounts of fat and starch, which is exactly what makes corn, nuts and peas valuable to us.

---

Q: What happens when blood glucose falls, and how do fatty acids travel to the cells that need them?
![Low blood glucose triggering hydrolysis of triacylglycerols to fatty acids, which bind serum albumin in the bloodstream and are taken into cells by fatty acid transporters](assets/img/fig-2-55.png)
A: Low blood glucose triggers hydrolysis of the triacylglycerols in fat droplets into free fatty acids and glycerol. The fatty acids enter the bloodstream, where they bind to the abundant blood protein serum albumin, since they are not soluble in water on their own. Special fatty acid transporters in the plasma membrane of cells that oxidize them, such as muscle cells, pass them into the cytosol and from there they are moved into mitochondria.

---

Q+: After an overnight fast, which fuel is generating most of your ATP?
A: Fatty acids. The liver first releases glucose from glycogen to maintain circulating levels, but by the morning after an overnight fast the glycogen reserves are exhausted and fatty acid oxidation is generating most of the ATP.

---

Q: The brain cannot use fatty acids well. What does it burn during prolonged fasting, and where does that fuel come from?
A: **Ketone bodies**: acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate. During prolonged fasting the liver acts as a central metabolic hub, converting fatty acids into these energy-rich molecules and releasing them into the bloodstream, where heart and brain cells oxidize them through the citric acid cycle to make ATP. Since circulating glucose during a long fast is largely made from amino acids released by breaking down muscle protein, meeting the brain's needs this way partially spares that protein.

---

Q+: Where does the characteristic bad breath of fasting or a ketogenic diet come from?
A: From acetoacetate breaking down spontaneously to acetone, which is volatile and is exhaled. A diet very low in carbohydrate produces ketone bodies for the same reason a prolonged fast does, and can enable weight loss in most individuals.

---

Q: How is a fatty acid oxidized, and what does each turn of the cycle yield?
![Triacylglycerol hydrolysed to fatty acids, activated as fatty acyl CoA, and shortened two carbons at a time in a cycle producing acetyl CoA, NADH and FADH2](assets/img/fig-2-57.png)
A: Three fatty acid chains are linked to glycerol by ester bonds, which enzymes called **lipases** hydrolyse to release the fatty acids. Once inside a mitochondrion each is coupled to coenzyme A in a reaction requiring ATP, giving fatty acyl CoA, which then goes round a cycle of four enzymes that trims two carbons from the carboxyl end each turn. Every turn produces one molecule each of acetyl CoA, NADH and FADH₂, and the cycle repeats until the fatty acid is completely consumed.

---

Q: How does pyruvate from glycolysis become acetyl CoA?
![Pathways converting both sugars and fats to acetyl CoA inside the mitochondrion](assets/img/fig-2-56.png)
A: It is transported into the mitochondrion and rapidly decarboxylated by the **pyruvate dehydrogenase complex**, a giant assembly of three enzymes. The products are CO₂ as a waste product, a molecule of NADH, and acetyl CoA. Amino acids can also enter mitochondria to be converted into acetyl CoA or another citric acid cycle intermediate.

---

Q+: Why is the mitochondrion described as the place where most of the cell's oxidation happens?
A: Because acetyl CoA is produced there from both major classes of food molecule, sugars by way of pyruvate and fats by way of fatty acyl CoA. Most of the useful energy extractable from either source remains stored in that acetyl CoA, so the organelle that makes it is where the oxidation reactions, and most of the ATP production, are concentrated.

---

Q: When was the citric acid cycle discovered, what else is it called, and how much of the cell's oxidation does it account for?
A: It was discovered in 1937, following nineteenth-century observations that cells deprived of air produce lactic acid or ethanol while cells with air consume O₂ and produce CO₂ and H₂O. It is also known as the tricarboxylic acid cycle or the Krebs cycle, and it accounts for about two thirds of the total oxidation of carbon compounds in most cells. Its major end products are CO₂, released as waste, and high-energy electrons carried as NADH.
Tags: citric-acid-cycle

---

Q: What goes into and comes out of one turn of the citric acid cycle?
![Overview of the citric acid cycle, with acetyl CoA joining oxaloacetate to form citrate and the cycle regenerating oxaloacetate](assets/img/fig-2-58.png)
A: The acetyl group is transferred from acetyl CoA onto the four-carbon molecule **oxaloacetate**, forming the six-carbon tricarboxylic acid **citrate**, which gives the cycle its name. Citrate is then gradually oxidized through a chain of eight reactions that ends by regenerating oxaloacetate, ready for another turn. Each turn releases two molecules of CO₂ as waste and produces three NADH, one FADH₂ and one GTP.
Tags: citric-acid-cycle

---

Q+: What is GTP, and what happens to it?
![The structure of GTP, guanosine triphosphate](assets/img/fig-2-59.png)
A: Guanosine triphosphate, a close relative of ATP, with GDP standing in the same relation to it as ADP does to ATP. Transferring its terminal phosphate group to ADP yields one ATP molecule for each turn of the cycle.
Tags: citric-acid-cycle

---

Q: The citric acid cycle does not use gaseous O₂, yet it stops without oxygen. Explain the apparent contradiction.
A: The oxygen atoms the cycle needs to build CO₂ come from water, not from O₂: three molecules of water are taken up in each turn. What requires oxygen is downstream. The cycle produces NADH, and there is no other efficient way for NADH to unload its electrons and regenerate the NAD⁺ that the cycle depends on. Cut off the oxygen and NAD⁺ stops being replenished, so the cycle halts even though it never touched O₂ directly.
Tags: citric-acid-cycle

---

Q: What happens in the electron-transport chain, and what is the gradient it builds used for?
A: NADH and FADH₂ hand the electrons they collected from food to a chain of specialized acceptor and donor molecules embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Passing along it, the electrons fall to successively lower energy states and are finally given to O₂ that has diffused into the mitochondrion, reducing it to water. The energy released along the way pumps H⁺ ions from the innermost compartment, the matrix, out to the intermembrane space. The resulting electrochemical proton gradient across the inner membrane is tapped like a battery to drive energy-requiring reactions, above all the phosphorylation of ADP to ATP in **oxidative phosphorylation**.

---

Q+: Which step in the oxidative catabolism of food actually requires gaseous oxygen from the atmosphere?
- [ ] Glycolysis
- [ ] Conversion of pyruvate to acetyl CoA
- [ ] The citric acid cycle
- [x] Oxidative phosphorylation
A: Only oxidative phosphorylation. Everything upstream of it works without O₂ and would continue to do so if NAD⁺ could be regenerated some other way, which is exactly what fermentation arranges. In the eukaryotic cell the mitochondrion is the centre toward which all energy-yielding processes lead, whether they start from sugars, fats or proteins.

---

Q: Catabolic pathways are not only for energy. What else do glycolysis and the citric acid cycle supply?
![Glycolysis and the citric acid cycle shown feeding branches that lead to nucleotides, amino acids, lipids, sugars and other biosynthetic products](assets/img/fig-2-60.png)
A: The building blocks for biosynthesis. Many of their intermediates are siphoned off into anabolic pathways and converted into amino acids, nucleotides, lipids and other small molecules. Oxaloacetate and α-ketoglutarate from the citric acid cycle, for example, are carried from the mitochondrial matrix back to the cytosol, where they are the precursors of the amino acids aspartate and glutamate respectively.

---

Q: Nitrogen makes up nearly 80% of the atmosphere. Why is that not much use to most organisms?
A: Because N₂ is chemically unreactive as a gas, and only a few species can incorporate it into organic molecules, a process called **nitrogen fixation**. It happens in certain microorganisms and through geophysical processes such as lightning discharge, and the biosphere could not exist without it.

---

Q+: If fixation is that essential, why is only a small fraction of today's organic nitrogen freshly fixed?
A: Because most organic nitrogen has been in circulation for a long time, passing from one organism to another. Present-day fixation is better described as topping up the total nitrogen supply than as supplying it. Vertebrates in particular get virtually all their nitrogen from dietary protein and nucleic acids, which are broken down to amino acids and nucleotide components and rebuilt.

---

Q: Which amino acids are **essential** for humans, and what does "essential" mean here?
![The nine essential amino acids: threonine, methionine, lysine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, histidine, phenylalanine and tryptophan](assets/img/fig-2-61.png)
A: Nine of the twenty: threonine, methionine, lysine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, histidine, phenylalanine and tryptophan. Essential means they cannot be synthesized from other components of the diet and must be supplied ready-made. The others can be made, using raw materials that include citric acid cycle intermediates. Plants and other organisms make the essential ones through long and energetically expensive pathways that vertebrates lost in the course of evolution.

---

Q+: Are there essential *nucleotides* that must come from the diet?
- [ ] Yes, the purines
- [ ] Yes, the pyrimidines
- [x] No, there are none
- [ ] Only during rapid growth
A: None. All the nitrogens in the purine and pyrimidine bases, and some of the carbons, come from the plentiful amino acids glutamine, aspartic acid and glycine, while the ribose and deoxyribose sugars come from glucose. The cell has specialized biosynthetic pathways for the whole job.

---

Q: What happens to dietary amino acids that are not used for biosynthesis?
A: They are oxidized for metabolic energy. Most of their carbon and hydrogen ends up as CO₂ or H₂O, while their nitrogen is shuttled through various forms and eventually excreted as **urea**. Each amino acid is processed differently, so a whole constellation of enzymatic reactions exists for their catabolism.

---

Q: Sulfur is abundant on Earth as sulfate. Why can humans not use it directly?
A: Because sulfate (SO₄²⁻) is the fully oxidized form, and life needs sulfur in the reduced sulfide (S²⁻) state to build methionine, cysteine, coenzyme A and the iron-sulfur centres essential for electron transport. Reduction requires a sulfate assimilation pathway using ATP and reducing power, and that pathway exists in bacteria, fungi and plants but not in humans or other animals, so we have to obtain our sulfur already reduced, in food.

---

Q: A diagram of human metabolism shows some 2000 reactions. What does that scale imply about how molecules are handled?
![Some 2000 metabolic reactions of a human cell shown schematically, with glycolysis and the citric acid cycle highlighted at the centre](assets/img/fig-2-62.png)
A: That the same molecule is usually contested by several pathways at once. All of it happens inside a cell less than 0.1 mm across, and each reaction needs a different enzyme. Pyruvate alone is a substrate for half a dozen or more enzymes: one converts it to acetyl CoA, another to oxaloacetate, a third to the amino acid alanine, a fourth to lactate. All those pathways compete for the same pyruvate molecules, and similar competitions run simultaneously for thousands of other small molecules.

---

Q+: Given that complexity, why is a temporary change in diet not disastrous?
A: Because the metabolic balance of a cell is remarkably stable and self-correcting. When it is perturbed the cell reacts so as to restore the original state, and it can adapt and keep functioning through starvation or disease. Mutations can damage or even eliminate whole reaction pathways and the cell will still survive provided certain minimum requirements are met. What makes that possible is an elaborate network of control mechanisms that regulates and coordinates the rates of all its reactions, resting ultimately on the ability of proteins to change shape and chemistry in response to their immediate surroundings.

---

Q+: In a multicellular organism, is metabolism the same everywhere?
A: No. Different cell types need somewhat different sets of enzymes, and different tissues make distinct contributions to the chemistry of the organism as a whole. Each type has its own metabolic traits, and they cooperate extensively both in the normal state and in response to stress and starvation.

---

Q: The citric acid cycle begins when the acetyl group of acetyl CoA is transferred to {{oxaloacetate}}, forming the six-carbon molecule {{citrate}}.
A: The cycle closes because its final reaction regenerates oxaloacetate, so the same four-carbon acceptor is available for the next acetyl group.

Q: What is the general formula of a monosaccharide, and what splits them into two classes?
![Panel outlining the types of sugars found in cells, including trioses, pentoses and hexoses, ring formation and isomers](assets/img/panel-2-04.png)
A: $(\text{CH}_2\text{O})_n$, where $n$ is usually 3 to 8, with two or more hydroxyl groups. They divide by which carbonyl group they carry: those with an aldehyde group are **aldoses**, and those with a ketone group are **ketoses**.

---

Q+: Why do sugars in solution not look like the straight chains they are usually drawn as?
A: Because in water the aldehyde or ketone group tends to react with a hydroxyl group on the same molecule, closing it into a ring. The hydroxyl on the carbon that carried the aldehyde or ketone can then sit in either of two positions, called α and β, and it swaps between them rapidly.

---

Q: Glucose, galactose and mannose all have the formula C₆H₁₂O₆ and differ only in the arrangement of groups around one or two carbons. Why does something so slight matter biologically?
A: Because the chemistry barely changes but the recognition does. Those small differences in spatial arrangement make only minor changes to the chemical properties of the sugars, yet enzymes and other proteins distinguish them precisely, so the biological consequences can be large. It is a clean illustration of the fact that specificity in cells comes from shape rather than from reactivity.

---

Q: What do all fatty acids have in common, and what makes one **unsaturated**?
![Panel on fatty acids and other lipids, showing saturated and unsaturated chains, triacylglycerols, phospholipids and micelle and bilayer formation](assets/img/panel-2-05.png)
A: Every fatty acid has a carboxyl group at one end and a long hydrocarbon tail at the other. Hundreds of kinds exist, and those with one or more double bonds in the tail are unsaturated, while those with none are saturated. A double bond is rigid and puts a kink in the chain, whereas the rest of the chain rotates freely about its C-C bonds.

---

Q+: How are fatty acids stored, and how does a phospholipid differ from a stored fat?
A: Fats and oils are **triacylglycerols**: three fatty acids linked to glycerol through ester bonds. In a **phospholipid**, only two of the glycerol hydroxyls carry fatty acids, and the third is linked to phosphoric acid. That phosphate carries a negative charge and is further linked to a small polar group such as choline, which is what gives the molecule its hydrophilic head.

---

Q: A fatty acid has a hydrophilic head and a hydrophobic tail. What structures do fatty acids and their derivatives form in water?
A: Fatty acids themselves form either a surface film or small spherical **micelles**. Their derivatives build larger aggregates held together by hydrophobic forces: triacylglycerols form the large spherical fat droplets seen in cytoplasm, while phospholipids and glycolipids form self-sealing lipid bilayers, which are the basis of every cell membrane.

---

Q+: How are **lipids** defined, and what are steroids and glycolipids?
A: Lipids are defined by solubility rather than structure: water-insoluble molecules that dissolve in organic solvents. Steroids share a common multiple-ring structure and, like the polyisoprenoids, are built from isoprene units. Glycolipids resemble phospholipids in having a hydrophobic region with two long hydrocarbon tails and a polar region, but their polar part contains one or more sugars and no phosphate.

---

Q: What are the three parts of a nucleotide, and what does the prime mark in "5-prime" distinguish?
![Panel surveying the nucleotides, showing bases, sugars, phosphates and the phosphodiester bonds that join nucleotides into nucleic acids](assets/img/panel-2-06.png)
A: A nitrogen-containing base, a five-carbon sugar, and a phosphate group, which is what makes the nucleotide negatively charged. Carbons on the sugar are numbered with a prime mark to distinguish them from the atoms of the base, so one speaks of the 5-prime carbon. Phosphates normally join the C5 hydroxyl of the ribose or deoxyribose, and mono-, di- and triphosphates are all common. The base is linked at C1, the same carbon used in sugar-sugar bonds.

---

Q+: How are nucleotides joined into a nucleic acid, and which end does the sequence start from?
A: By **phosphodiester bonds** between the 5′ and 3′ carbon atoms of adjacent sugar rings. The linear sequence is written in one-letter code such as AGCTT, starting from the 5′ end of the chain.

---

Q+: Besides building nucleic acids, what else do nucleotides do?
A: As nucleoside di- and triphosphates they carry chemical energy in easily hydrolysed phosphoanhydride bonds. They combine with other groups to form coenzymes, and they serve as small intracellular signalling molecules.

Q: True or false: a $10^{-8}$ M solution of HCl has a pH of 8.
A: False, and the trap is worth understanding. Taking $-\log$ of the acid concentration gives 8, but that would mean adding an acid to water made it basic, which is impossible. At this dilution the protons water supplies itself are no longer negligible. Charge balance requires $[\text{H}^+] = [\text{Cl}^-] + [\text{OH}^-]$, and solving with $K_w = 10^{-14}$ gives $[\text{H}^+] = 1.05 \times 10^{-7}$ M, so the pH is about 6.98: very slightly acidic, as it must be.

Q: True or false: most of the interactions between macromolecules could be mediated just as well by covalent bonds as by noncovalent bonds.
A: False. Covalent bonds are roughly 20 times stronger than a single noncovalent bond and are not broken by thermal motion, so an association made covalently would be effectively permanent and would need enzymatic action to undo. Most biological interactions have to be reversible on the timescale of the process they serve. Noncovalent binding also gives graded affinity, since strength depends on how many contacts form, and it delivers specificity through complementary surfaces. A covalent alternative would lose the reversibility and the tunability at once.

Q: True or false: animals and plants both use oxidation to extract energy from food molecules.

Q: True or false: if an oxidation occurs in a reaction, it must be accompanied by a reduction.
A: True. Electrons are conserved in a chemical reaction, so an electron lost by one molecule has to be gained by another. The two halves are inseparable, which is why the pair is called a redox reaction.

Q: True or false: linking the energetically unfavorable reaction A → B to a second, favorable reaction B → C will shift the equilibrium constant for the first reaction.
A: False, and the distinction matters. The equilibrium constant of A → B is fixed by the intrinsic properties of A and B, and coupling does not touch it. What coupling changes is the *position* the system settles at: by consuming B, the second reaction keeps $[\text{B}]$ low, which keeps $\Delta G$ for A → B negative and pulls the conversion along. The constant is unchanged; the concentrations are not.

Q: Is the organic chemistry of living cells really so different from the organic chemistry done in the best laboratories in the world?
A: In the reactions themselves, no: cells obey the same chemistry, and synthetic chemists can make many of the same molecules. The differences are in the conditions and the control. Q: Polymerizing tubulin subunits into a microtubule clearly makes the subunits more ordered, yet the reaction proceeds with an increase in entropy. How can both be true?

This is the hydrophobic force at work, and it is the standard resolution whenever binding appears to create order out of nothing.
