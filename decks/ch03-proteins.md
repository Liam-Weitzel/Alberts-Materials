Q: Alberts opens the chapter by claiming that when we look at a cell down a microscope, or measure its electrical or biochemical activity, we are essentially observing proteins. What is behind that claim?
A: Proteins make up most of a cell's dry mass, and they carry out the majority of its functions rather than merely providing its structure. Enzymes supply the molecular surfaces that catalyse the cell's reactions, membrane proteins form the channels and pumps controlling what crosses the plasma membrane, and other proteins carry messages between cells or relay signals inward to the nucleus. Others again are machines with moving parts: kinesin propels organelles through the cytoplasm and topoisomerase untangles knotted DNA. The list of specialized roles runs to antibodies, toxins, hormones, antifreeze molecules, elastic fibers, ropes and sources of luminescence.

Q: What is the relationship between a **protein**, a **polypeptide**, and the **polypeptide backbone**?
![Formation of a peptide bond between glycine and alanine with removal of water, and a two-dimensional representation of a polypeptide backbone with its attached side chains](assets/img/fig-3-01.png)
A: Every protein molecule is a long unbranched chain of amino acids, each joined to its neighbour by a covalent peptide bond, which is why proteins are also called polypeptides. The repeating sequence of atoms running along the core of that chain is the polypeptide backbone. Attached to this repetitive backbone are the parts of each amino acid not involved in the peptide bond, the 20 different side chains, and it is the sequence of those chemically distinct side chains that makes each protein distinct rather than anything about the backbone, which is the same in all of them.
Tags: structure

Q+: The two ends of a polypeptide chain are chemically different. What are they called, and which way round is a sequence always written?
A: The end carrying the free amino group is the amino terminus or N-terminus, and the end carrying the free carboxyl group is the carboxyl terminus or C-terminus. At neutral pH neither is actually in the neutral form drawn in most figures: the amino group takes up a proton to become NH3+ and the carboxyl group loses one to become COO-. An amino acid sequence is always presented in the N-to-C direction, reading from left to right, so the convention is fixed and a sequence written the other way would be read wrongly.
Tags: structure

Q: How is the balance between polar and nonpolar side chains among the 20 amino acids, and what is the catch in that tidy split?
![The 20 amino acids commonly found in proteins, grouped as acidic, basic, uncharged polar and nonpolar, with three-letter and one-letter abbreviations](assets/img/fig-3-02.png)
A: There are equal numbers of polar and nonpolar side chains, ten of each. The catch is that the classification is a simplification: several side chains listed as polar are large enough to have substantial nonpolar character too, threonine, tyrosine, arginine and lysine among them. So the grouping is a guide to chemistry rather than a partition, and a residue can behave as both depending on which part of it is exposed.

Q: What is the general formula of an amino acid, and what state are its two ends in at pH 7?
![Panel 3-1, first page: families of amino acids, the general amino acid formula, optical isomers, the basic side chains, and peptide bond formation](assets/img/panel-3-01.png)
A: A central α-carbon atom carrying four things: an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen, and a side chain written R, which is commonly one of 20 different groups. At pH 7 neither end is in the neutral form the formula suggests. Both are ionized, so the amino group is $\text{NH}_3^+$ and the carboxyl group is $\text{COO}^-$, giving a molecule with a positive and a negative charge and no net charge. The panel groups the common amino acids by whether their side chains are acidic, basic, uncharged polar or nonpolar, and gives each a three-letter and a one-letter abbreviation, so alanine is Ala is A.
Tags: amino-acids

Q+: The α-carbon is asymmetric, so amino acids come in two mirror-image forms. Which does life use?
A: Only L. The α-carbon carries four different groups, which makes it asymmetric and allows two mirror-image or stereoisomers, designated L and D. Proteins contain exclusively L-amino acids. Since the two forms are chemically identical in every respect that does not involve another chiral molecule, the exclusive use of one of them is a historical choice frozen into the machinery rather than a chemical necessity, and it is the same kind of fact as the universality of the genetic code.
Tags: amino-acids

Q: Three amino acids have basic side chains, but they are not equally basic. What distinguishes lysine, arginine and histidine?
A: Lysine ends its side chain in a simple $\text{NH}_3^+$ group. Arginine is very basic, more so than lysine, because its positive charge is stabilized by resonance across the guanidinium group, so the charge is spread rather than localized. Histidine is the odd one out: the nitrogens of its ring have a relatively weak affinity for a proton, so at neutral pH they are only partly positive rather than fully charged.
Tags: amino-acids

---

Q+: Why does that weak affinity make histidine so useful in enzyme active sites?
A: Because a group that is only partly protonated at physiological pH can act as either an acid or a base without the cell having to change its conditions. It can accept a proton from one group and donate it to another, which is exactly what is needed to shuttle protons during catalysis. That is the histidine in the Asp-His-Ser catalytic triad of the serine proteases, where it removes a proton from the serine to activate it. An amino acid that was fully charged or fully neutral at pH 7 could not do that job.
Tags: amino-acids, enzymes

Q: Proline is listed with the nonpolar amino acids, but the panel adds a parenthetical correction to its name. What is it, and what follows from its structure?
![Panel 3-1, second page: the atomic structures of the acidic, uncharged polar and nonpolar side chains, and the disulfide bond between two cysteines](assets/img/panel-3-01b.png)
A: It is actually an imino acid rather than an amino acid, because its side chain loops back and bonds to the nitrogen of its own backbone amino group, so that nitrogen carries no free hydrogen. The consequence is structural: the ring locks the backbone geometry at that residue and there is no amide hydrogen available to donate a hydrogen bond, which is why proline disrupts α helices and β sheets and turns up at the corners and turns of a fold instead.
Tags: amino-acids

Q: Where is the line between a **protein** and a **peptide**?
A: Length, and the line is a convention rather than a chemical boundary. Both are chains of amino acids linked by peptide bonds and both are written with the N-terminus toward the left, but peptides are shorter, usually fewer than 50 amino acids. The panel's own example is the tripeptide histidine-cysteine-valine, which shows that even three residues is enough to be worth naming as a sequence.
Tags: amino-acids

Q: The peptide bond is a single bond, so why can the chain not rotate freely around it?
![Steric limitations on bond angles in a polypeptide chain, with the planar peptide unit and the phi and psi rotations, and a Ramachandran plot of observed angle pairs](assets/img/fig-3-03.png)
A: Because delocalization of electrons gives the peptide bond partial double-bond character, which makes the four atoms of the linkage a rigid planar unit and forbids rotation about it. Rotation is possible about the other two backbone bonds contributed by each amino acid: the angle about the Cα-C bond is psi and the angle about the N-Cα bond is phi. The conformation of a protein's main chain is therefore fixed by one pair of phi and psi angles per residue.
Tags: structure

---

Q: What does a **Ramachandran plot** show, and what is striking about it?
A: Each dot is an observed pair of phi and psi angles taken from a real protein structure. What is striking is how much of the plot is empty: because atoms behave as hard spheres with a van der Waals radius and no two may overlap, most of the possible phi and psi combinations simply do not occur. The dots fall into three shaded clusters, which correspond to three secondary structures that recur throughout proteins, the most prominent being the α helix and the β sheet. The plot is therefore evidence that the regular folding patterns are a consequence of steric restriction rather than a coincidence of particular sequences.
Tags: structure

---

Q: How much weaker is an individual noncovalent bond than a covalent bond, and how do proteins get away with folding on such weak interactions?
![Three types of noncovalent bond acting together between amino acid side chains: electrostatic attractions, hydrogen bonds and van der Waals attractions](assets/img/fig-3-04.png)
A: Individual noncovalent bonds are 30 to 300 times weaker than the typical covalent bonds that build biological molecules, weak enough that thermal motion breaks any one of them readily. What makes folding stable is that they act in parallel and in large numbers, so their combined strength holds two regions of a chain tightly together. Three types are involved, hydrogen bonds, electrostatic attractions and van der Waals attractions, and they form between atoms of the backbone as well as atoms of the side chains.
Tags: structure

---

Q+: A fourth weak force is usually named alongside those three, but it is not really a bond at all. Which is it, and what does it do to a folding chain?
![How a protein folds into a compact conformation, with polar side chains on the outside and nonpolar side chains buried in a hydrophobic core](assets/img/fig-3-05.png)
A: The hydrophobic clustering force. Nonpolar side chains disrupt the hydrogen-bonded network of surrounding water, so they are pushed together to minimize that disruption rather than being pulled together by any attraction of their own. The consequence for folding is that the distribution of polar and nonpolar amino acids along the sequence largely governs the shape: nonpolar side chains such as phenylalanine, leucine, valine and tryptophan cluster in the interior away from water, while polar ones such as arginine, glutamine and histidine sit near the surface where they can hydrogen-bond to water. Polar residues that do end up buried are usually hydrogen-bonded to other polar residues or to the backbone.
Tags: structure

---

Q: What did the denaturation and renaturation of purified proteins in a test tube establish, and why does it matter for cell biology?
A: Treating a protein with solvents that disrupt its noncovalent interactions unfolds, or denatures, it into a flexible chain that has lost its natural shape. When the denaturing solvent is removed, the protein often refolds spontaneously, or renatures, into its original conformation. Since nothing but the chain itself was present, the amino acid sequence must contain all the information needed to specify the three-dimensional shape. That is what licenses the whole logic of reading structure and function out of sequence, and it is why a gene specifying a sequence is sufficient to specify a protein's shape.
Tags: folding

---

Q+: If sequence is sufficient, why do cells need **molecular chaperones** at all?
A: Because sufficiency in a test tube is not reliability in a cell. The cytoplasm is crowded, and a newly synthesized chain exposes hydrophobic regions before it has finished folding, which lets partly folded chains associate with each other and form aggregates instead. Chaperones bind partly folded chains and help them along the most energetically favourable folding pathway, preventing that. The important qualification is that they do not specify the answer: the final shape is still determined by the amino acid sequence, and chaperones only make reaching the folded state more reliable.
Tags: folding

---

Q: Most proteins fold into a single stable conformation, and it is tempting to picture that as a fixed shape. Why is that picture wrong?
A: Because the folded conformation is a lowest-free-energy state, not a frozen one. It is under constant thermal bombardment from the Brownian motion of molecules colliding with it, so its atoms are always moving and neighbouring regions oscillate in concerted ways. On top of that, the conformation can change when the protein binds another molecule, and that change of shape is often essential to what the protein does. Function generally depends on this dynamic character rather than being hindered by it.
Tags: dynamics

---

Q+: NMR studies of ubiquitin are used to make this concrete. What do they show?
![A ribbon model of ubiquitin, and an overlay of backbone conformations coloured by the rates of atomic motion measured by NMR](assets/img/fig-3-12.png)
A: That a folded protein is best described as an ensemble of closely related substructures, or conformers, rather than one structure. Overlaying a set of backbone conformations determined for ubiquitin reveals the regions that transition rapidly between substructures, and NMR residual dipolar coupling experiments give the rates of motion of the individual atoms, which differ greatly from region to region. Function exploits these fluctuations, as when a surface loop flips out to expose a binding site for a second molecule.
Tags: dynamics

Q: The α helix and the β sheet were both discovered around 70 years ago, from unlikely materials. Which materials, and why are these two patterns so common?
![The regular conformation of the polypeptide backbone in the alpha helix and the beta sheet, showing the hydrogen bonds between backbone peptide bonds](assets/img/fig-3-06.png)
A: The α helix was found first, in α-keratin, the protein forming the filaments in hair. Within a year the β sheet was found in fibroin, the major constituent of silk. They are common because they arise from hydrogen bonding between the N-H and C=O groups of the polypeptide backbone, without involving the side chains at all. Since the backbone is identical in every protein, many different sequences can adopt them, though some individual side chains are incompatible with one or the other.
Tags: structure

---

Q: In an α helix, which groups hydrogen-bond to which, and how many amino acids does one turn take?
A: A hydrogen bond forms between every fourth peptide bond, linking the C=O of one peptide bond to the N-H of another four peptide bonds along the same chain. That produces a regular helix with a complete turn every 3.6 amino acids. Because all the N-H groups point one way and all the C=O groups point the other, the helix has a polarity, with a partial negative charge at the C-terminal end and a partial positive charge at the N-terminal end.
Tags: structure

---

Q: Where in a cell are α helices especially abundant, and what makes them suited to that setting?
A: In proteins of cell membranes, such as transport proteins and receptors, where the segments crossing the lipid bilayer usually cross as α helices built largely from amino acids with nonpolar side chains. The arrangement solves a specific problem: the polypeptide backbone is hydrophilic and would be very unhappy in a lipid environment, but in an α helix the backbone is hydrogen-bonded to itself and so satisfied internally, while the protruding nonpolar side chains shield it from the surrounding lipid.
Tags: structure

---

Q: What distinguishes a **parallel** from an **antiparallel** β sheet, and what do both give the protein?
![An antiparallel beta sheet and a parallel beta sheet, both formed from hydrogen-bonded neighbouring strands](assets/img/fig-3-07.png)
A: In a parallel sheet the neighbouring segments of backbone run in the same orientation; in an antiparallel sheet the chain folds back and forth on itself so each section runs opposite to its immediate neighbours. Both are held together by hydrogen bonds connecting the peptide bonds of neighbouring strands, and both produce a very rigid structure. The cores of many proteins contain extensive regions of β sheet. By convention an arrow representing a strand points toward the C-terminus, and the side chains project alternately above and below the plane of the sheet.
Tags: structure

Q: What is a **coiled-coil**, and what does the "abcdefg" labelling in its figure actually encode?
![A coiled-coil, showing the sevenfold abcdefg labelling of side chains, the stripe of nonpolar a and d residues, and two helices wrapped around each other](assets/img/fig-3-08.png)
A: It is a structure in which two α helices, and in some cases three or four, wrap around each other. The labelling is a heptad repeat: successive side chains along one helix are labelled a to g and the pattern restarts, which means positions "a" and "d" lie close together on the cylinder surface and form a stripe winding slowly around the helix. Proteins that form coiled-coils put nonpolar amino acids at those two positions, so the helices can twist around each other with the nonpolar stripes facing inward and the hydrophilic side chains left exposed to water. Long rodlike coiled-coils are the structural framework of many elongated proteins, α-keratin in skin and its appendages and the myosin of muscle among them.
Tags: structure

Q: Name the four levels of protein structure, and say what each one refers to.
A: Primary structure is the amino acid sequence itself, unique to each protein and determined by the gene encoding it. Secondary structure is those stretches of the chain that form α helices and β sheets. Tertiary structure is the full three-dimensional organization of one polypeptide chain, including its helices, sheets and all the twists and turns between its N- and C-termini. Quaternary structure applies only when a protein is a complex of more than one polypeptide chain, and describes the complete conformation of that assembly. The levels are a convenience biologists found useful rather than four physically separate things.
Tags: structure

---

Q: Four representations are conventionally used to draw a protein structure. What does each emphasize?
![The SH2 domain drawn as a polypeptide backbone model, a ribbon model, a wire model including side chains, and a space-filling model](assets/img/fig-3-09.png)
A: A backbone model traces the path of the chain and nothing else, so the fold is visible without clutter. A ribbon model adds the secondary structure, drawing helices as coiled ribbons and β strands as arrows, which is why it is the usual choice for showing how a domain is built. A wire model includes the amino acid side chains, so it shows the chemistry available at a given position. A space-filling model draws atoms at their van der Waals radii, which hides the interior entirely but shows the surface contours that actually meet other molecules. The example is the SH2 domain, 100 amino acids, coloured from its N-terminus in purple to its C-terminus in red so the chain can be followed in every version.
Tags: structure

Q: What is a **protein domain**, and how big is one relative to a whole protein?
![Ribbon models of three protein domains: an all-alpha cytochrome, a mixed alpha and parallel beta NAD-binding domain, and an all-beta immunoglobulin light chain domain](assets/img/fig-3-10.png)
A: A domain is a structural unit that folds more or less independently, formed from roughly 40 to 350 contiguous amino acids, and it is the modular unit from which larger proteins are built. Most proteins run between 50 and 2000 amino acids, so the smallest are a single domain while large ones are a set of smaller domains joined together. The central core of a domain can be built from α helices, from β sheets, or from combinations of the two, and it is typically the protruding loop regions rather than the core that form the binding sites for other molecules.
Tags: structure, domains

---

Q+: The Src protein kinase is used as the example of a multidomain protein. Which domains does it have, and how common are they in us?
![The Src protein, with its SH3 and SH2 regulatory domains and a two-lobed C-terminal kinase domain with ATP bound at the interface between the lobes](assets/img/fig-3-11.png)
A: Three. Its C-terminal domain, which has two lobes, is the core protein kinase and carries the catalytic activity, while its SH2 and SH3 domains are regulatory and respond to the signals that switch the kinase on and off. Both regulatory domains are named after this protein, being short for "Src homology 2" and "Src homology 3". They are not rare: the human genome encodes about 300 different SH3 domains and 120 SH2 domains. The ATP-binding site sits at the interface between the two lobes of the kinase domain.
Tags: domains, signaling

Q: What is an **intrinsically disordered region**, and how much of the eukaryotic proteome carries one?
A: A stretch of polypeptide chain that does not fold into a fixed structure and instead bends and flexes continually under thermal buffeting. Short unstructured lengths often connect the domains of a large protein and act as flexible hinges. Current estimates suggest that about a third of all eukaryotic proteins also carry longer intrinsically disordered regions, defined as more than 30 amino acids in length, and these can be very long. Their ubiquity was only appreciated once bioinformatics methods were developed that could recognize them from sequence, which is why they were largely missed for so long.
Tags: idr

---

Q: With 20 amino acids available at each position, how many different chains of length $n$ are possible, and what does that number come to for an ordinary protein?
A: There are $20^n$ possible chains of length $n$, so a chain of four amino acids has $20^4 = 160{,}000$ possibilities. For a typical protein length of about 300 amino acids, a cell could in principle make more than $10^{390}$ different polypeptide chains, since $20^{300} \approx 10^{390}$. The number is large enough that producing even a single molecule of each kind is not physically possible, so the proteins that exist are a vanishingly small and heavily selected sample of sequence space rather than a representative one.
Tags: sequence-space

---

Q: Of all the polypeptide chains that could conceivably be written, roughly what fraction would fold into a stable three-dimensional conformation, and how do cells manage to be full of ones that do?
A: By some estimates fewer than one in a billion. Cells are full of well-behaved proteins because of natural selection rather than any chemical tendency: a protein with an unpredictably variable structure and activity does not help the survival of the cell carrying it, so such proteins were eliminated over the long trial-and-error process of evolution. What is left are proteins whose chemical properties suit a particular catalytic or structural job. The precision this produces cuts both ways, since changing even a few atoms in a single amino acid can sometimes disrupt the whole molecule badly enough to abolish all function.
Tags: folding, evolution

Q: What is a **protein family**, and how did families arise?
![Backbone conformations of elastase and chymotrypsin compared, with the amino acids identical in both shaded green and the active site of each circled](assets/img/fig-3-13.png)
A: A group of proteins whose members resemble one another in both amino acid sequence and three-dimensional conformation. They arose because once a protein had evolved that folded stably and did something useful, its structure was repeatedly modified during evolution to do new things. Accidental gene duplication accelerated that greatly, since a spare copy can evolve independently without the original job going unfilled. The serine proteases are the standard example, a large family of protein-cleaving enzymes including the digestive enzymes chymotrypsin, trypsin and elastase along with several proteases of the blood-clotting cascade, each cleaving different proteins or different types of peptide bond.
Tags: evolution, families

---

Q+: Comparing elastase with chymotrypsin, which is better conserved, the sequence or the structure?
A: The structure, and by a wide margin. Only the amino acids shaded green in the figure are actually the same in the two enzymes, yet most of the detailed twists and turns of their chains, several hundred amino acids long, are virtually identical. This holds generally across protein families: structure has been more highly conserved than sequence. The family takes its name from the serine whose side chain sits in the active site of each enzyme and participates directly in the cleavage reaction.
Tags: evolution, families

Q+: Yeast α2 and *Drosophila* engrailed are both homeodomain transcription regulators, separated by more than a billion years. How similar are they, and how was the relationship established?
![A ribbon model and alpha-carbon trace of the homeodomain shared by the yeast alpha2 protein and the Drosophila engrailed protein, with their sequences aligned below](assets/img/fig-3-14.png)
A: They are identical at only 17 of the 60 amino acids of the homeodomain, which is too little for the sequences alone to settle the question. The relationship became certain only when their three-dimensional structures were determined by x-ray crystallography and found to superimpose. The useful rule of thumb running the other way is that two proteins with more than 25% identity in their amino acid sequences usually do share the same overall structure, so sequence is sufficient evidence above that threshold and not below it.
Tags: evolution, families

Q: Not all the amino acid differences between members of a protein family mean something. What are the three fates of a mutation here?
A: Mutation is random, so the changes fall into three groups by their consequences. Some were selected during evolution because they produced useful changes in biological activity, and these are what give present-day family members their different functional properties. Others were effectively neutral, having neither a beneficial nor a damaging effect on the protein's basic structure and function, and they simply accumulated. The rest were deleterious, altering the three-dimensional structure enough to make the protein useless, and those faulty proteins were readily lost during evolution, which is why we do not see them.
Tags: evolution

Q: Sequencing the human genome gave a number of protein-coding genes that surprised people. What was it, and what did comparisons of those genes to known structures reveal?
A: About 20,000 protein-coding genes. Through sequence comparison, the products of more than half of them can be assigned to known protein structures belonging to more than 500 different protein families. The database of known protein sequences now holds more than 100 million entries and grows quickly as more genomes are sequenced, and the encoded polypeptides range in size from 6 amino acids up to titin, a structural protein of muscle running to 34,000 amino acids.
Tags: human-genome

Q: Distinguish **paralogs** from **orthologs**.
A: Paralogs are evolutionarily related proteins within one organism that have evolved to do somewhat different jobs, as elastase and chymotrypsin have. Orthologs are evolutionarily related proteins that perform the same function in different organisms, such as mouse elastase and human elastase. The distinction matters because it separates duplication followed by divergence of function from simple descent of one function down two lineages, and only the second licenses transferring what is known about one protein to the other.
Tags: evolution, families

---

Q: We know the three-dimensional structure of more than 100,000 proteins. How many distinct ways of folding does that vast set actually use?
A: Only about 2000 so-called protein folds, counting across all organisms, and representative structures have been determined for most of them. The limited repertoire is what makes protein comparison so productive, since related structures often imply related functions and years of experiment can be saved by noticing that a new protein resembles one already understood. That is how sequence relationships first revealed that certain genes which make mammalian cells cancerous encode protein kinases.
Tags: structure, evolution

Q: What is **domain shuffling**, and why are the joins between domains interesting?
![Domain shuffling, showing the two protease domains of chymotrypsin combined with EGF-like, calcium-binding and kringle domains in urokinase, factor IX and plasminogen](assets/img/fig-3-15.png)
A: The evolutionary process in which large proteins arise by joining preexisting domains in new combinations, thought to originate from the accidental joining of the DNA sequences that encode each domain into a new gene. The joins matter because novel binding surfaces are often created at the juxtaposition of two domains, and many of the functional sites where proteins bind small molecules turn out to sit there. The figure traces it through the serine proteases: chymotrypsin is two protease domains, while the more regulated and specialized urokinase, factor IX and plasminogen connect those same two domains to domains resembling epidermal growth factor, a calcium-binding protein, or kringle domains.
Tags: evolution, domains

Q+: A subset of domains have been especially mobile in evolution and are called **protein modules**. What structural features explain their success?
![Ribbon diagrams of an immunoglobulin module, a fibronectin type 3 module and a kringle module, with N- and C-termini marked by red spheres](assets/img/fig-3-16.png)
A: Two features. First, each has a stable core built from strands of β sheet with less-ordered loops of chain protruding from it, and those loops are ideally placed to form binding sites, so a new binding specificity needs only small changes to the loops rather than a redesigned core. The immunoglobulin fold, the basis of antibody molecules, is the clearest demonstration. Second, they are easy to integrate into other proteins, which is what let them spread.
Tags: domains, evolution

---

Q+: Two geometries of module get integrated in quite different ways. What are they?
![Four fibronectin type 3 domains from the extracellular matrix protein fibronectin, shown as ribbon and space-filling models forming an extended rod](assets/img/fig-3-17.png)
A: In-line domains have their N- and C-terminal ends at opposite poles, so when the encoding DNA undergoes tandem duplication the copies link readily in series to form extended structures, either with themselves or with other in-line domains. Stiff extended structures of this kind are especially common in extracellular matrix molecules and in the extracellular parts of cell-surface receptors, and fibronectin type 3 is the example. Plug-in domains such as SH2 and kringle have their N- and C-termini close together, so after genomic rearrangement they are usually accommodated as an insertion into a loop region of another protein.
Tags: domains, evolution

---

Q+: Domain usage is remarkably similar across eukaryotes, but there are exceptions. Which one does the book give?
A: The frequency with which many common domains are used, protein kinases among them, is similar in organisms as different as yeast, plants, worms, flies and humans. The MHC antigen-recognition domain is the striking exception: it is present in 57 copies in humans and entirely absent from the other four. Domains like this have specialized functions not shared with other eukaryotes, and the multiple copies are assumed to reflect strong selection during recent evolution.
Tags: domains, human-genome

Q: On gene count alone, humans look unimpressive next to a weed and a worm. How does the book resolve that, and what does it admit it cannot explain?
![Domains in a group of evolutionarily related DNA-binding proteins from yeast, worm and human, with more domains added in the more complex organisms](assets/img/fig-3-18.png)
A: Our roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes make us look no more complex than the mustard weed *Arabidopsis* and only about 1.3-fold more complex than a nematode. The resolution is that our individual proteins are on average more complicated: vertebrates inherited nearly all their protein domains from invertebrates, with only 7% of identified human domains being vertebrate-specific, but domain shuffling during vertebrate evolution produced nearly twice as many combinations of domains in human proteins as in a worm or a fly. What the book explicitly does not claim is that this explains us. It greatly increases the range of possible protein interactions, but how that contributes to making us human is not known, and we lack even the tiniest hint of the function of more than 10,000 identified human proteins.
Tags: human-genome

Q: What is a **binding site**, and how does one turn two polypeptides into a single protein?
![A symmetrical CAP protein dimer formed through one identical binding site on each monomer, and a symmetrical neuraminidase tetramer formed through two nonidentical binding sites](assets/img/fig-3-19.png)
A: Any region of a protein's surface that can interact with another molecule through sets of noncovalent bonds. The same weak bonds that fold a chain also let folded chains bind each other, so when a binding site recognizes the surface of a second protein, the two chains are held together with a precisely defined geometry and the result is one larger protein whose individual chains are called subunits. The arrangement of those subunits is the protein's quaternary structure. Using the same binding site on each of two identical monomers gives a symmetrical dimer, while a pair of nonidentical binding sites on each monomer gives a symmetrical tetramer.
Tags: structure, assembly

---

Q: Hemoglobin is the standard example of a protein built from more than one kind of chain. What is it made of?
![Hemoglobin as a symmetrical assembly of two alpha-globin and two beta-globin subunits, each carrying a heme group](assets/img/fig-3-20.png)
A: Two identical α-globin subunits and two identical β-globin subunits, arranged symmetrically. Each of the four chains carries one heme molecule, which is the site that binds oxygen, so one hemoglobin molecule carries four molecules of oxygen. The point being illustrated is that quaternary structure is not restricted to copies of one chain: many proteins contain two or more types of polypeptide chain, and such multisubunit proteins can be very large.
Tags: structure, assembly

Q: A protein subunit with binding sites can assemble into a dimer, a filament or a ring. What decides which?
![A protein with one binding site forming a dimer, identical proteins with two different binding sites forming a long helical filament, and the same two sites arranged to close into a ring](assets/img/fig-3-21.png)
A: The number and the relative disposition of the binding sites. One binding site lets a protein pair with an identical copy and stop there, giving a dimer. Two different binding sites, each complementary to a region on another copy of the same molecule, let the chain continue indefinitely and usually produce a long helical filament, as actin does. If those two sites are disposed appropriately with respect to each other, the chain closes on itself after a fixed number of subunits and gives a ring instead of a helix.
Tags: assembly

Q+: **Actin** is the standard example of that filament case. What is it, and what does the filament look like?
![Electron micrographs of negatively stained actin filaments, and the helical arrangement of actin molecules with a 37 nm repeat between the minus and plus ends](assets/img/fig-3-22.png)
A: Actin is a globular protein, very abundant in eukaryotic cells, where it forms one of the major filament systems of the cytoskeleton. Many actin molecules assemble into a long helical filament with a repeat of about 37 nm, and the filament is not symmetrical end to end: one end is designated minus and the other plus. That polarity follows directly from the head-to-tail arrangement, since every subunit sits in the same orientation, and it is what later allows motor proteins to travel along the filament in a defined direction.

Q: Why is a helix such a common structure in biology?
![Helices built from rectangular subunits with two, three and six subunits per turn, and the distinction between left-handed and right-handed](assets/img/fig-3-23.png)
A: Because it is what you get for free from repeating identical subunits. If all subunits are identical, neighbouring subunits can usually fit together in only one way, the one minimizing the free energy of the contact, so subunit 3 sits on subunit 2 exactly as subunit 2 sits on subunit 1. Since it is very rare for subunits to join in a straight line, a fixed rotation followed by a fixed translation along an axis is what results, and that is a helix. This holds whether the subunits are small molecules linked covalently, as amino acids are in an α helix, or whole proteins linked noncovalently, as actin molecules are in a filament.

Q: Enzymes tend to be globular, but some jobs need a molecule that spans a distance. What are **fibrous proteins**, and what is α-keratin made of?
A: Proteins with a relatively simple elongated three-dimensional structure, as opposed to the compact irregular ball of a globular protein. An α-keratin molecule is a dimer of two identical subunits whose long α helices form a coiled-coil, and the coiled-coil regions are capped at each end by globular domains carrying binding sites. Those end caps are what let the molecules assemble further into ropelike intermediate filaments, part of the cytoskeleton that gives a cell its internal structural framework. Keratin filaments are extremely stable and are the main component of long-lived structures such as hair, horn and nails.

Q: **Collagen** is the most abundant fibrous protein outside the cell. What in its sequence makes the triple helix possible?
![A short section of collagen fibril resolving into individual 300 nm collagen molecules, each a triple helix of three wound chains](assets/img/fig-3-24.png)
A: Glycine at every third position in each of its three long polypeptide chains. Glycine is the smallest side chain, and putting it at every third residue is what allows the three chains to wind around one another into a long regular triple helix, since anything bulkier would not fit where the chains pack against each other. Many collagen molecules then bind side by side and end to end into long overlapping arrays, covalently linked through their lysine side chains, producing fibrils with the tensile strength of steel that give connective tissue its strength. The regular repeating arrangement of molecules within the fibril is what causes its striping.

Q: What is a **disulfide bond**, where does it form, and what does it actually do to a protein's shape?
![Covalent disulfide bonds forming between adjacent cysteine side chains, joining either two parts of one polypeptide chain or two different chains](assets/img/fig-3-25.png)
A: A covalent sulfur-sulfur cross-link formed between the -SH groups of two cysteine side chains that lie adjacent in the folded protein, catalysed in the endoplasmic reticulum by an enzyme as cells prepare newly synthesized proteins for export. The important qualification is that it does not change the conformation of a protein. It acts as an atomic staple reinforcing the conformation the protein already favours, and because breaking one covalent bond costs far more energy than breaking a whole set of noncovalent bonds, that reinforcement is substantial. Lysozyme, the enzyme in tears that dissolves bacterial cell walls, keeps its antibacterial activity for a long time because it is stabilized this way.

Q: Why do disulfide bonds generally fail to form in the cytosol?
A: Because the cytosol holds a high concentration of reducing agents, which converts S-S bonds back into cysteine -SH groups. The cross-links are therefore restricted to proteins that are secreted or attached to the outside of the plasma membrane, which are the ones directly exposed to extracellular conditions and in need of the extra stabilization. The reading Alberts offers is that proteins apparently do not require this reinforcement in the relatively mild environment inside the cell.

Q: Large cellular structures are not built as one giant covalently linked molecule. What three advantages does building them from repeating subunits give?
![Hexagonally packed globular protein subunits forming either a flat sheet or a tube](assets/img/fig-3-26.png)
A: First, a large structure built from one or a few repeating small subunits requires only a small amount of genetic information, since the same short instruction is reused. Second, both assembly and disassembly can be readily controlled reversible processes, because the subunits associate through multiple bonds of individually low energy that can be made and broken without breaking anything covalent. Third, errors in synthesis are more easily avoided, because correction mechanisms can operate during assembly to exclude malformed subunits rather than having to detect a fault in a finished giant molecule.

Q+: A hexagonally packed sheet of subunits can become a tube or a sphere. What does closing the structure buy, and what does it cost?
A: Closing it into a ring, tube or sphere increases the number of noncovalent bonds between subunits and so adds stability. The cost, or rather the consequence, is that the structure is now held by mutually dependent cooperative interactions, so a relatively small change affecting each subunit individually can tip the whole thing into assembling or disassembling. That sensitivity is a feature rather than a defect, since it is how such structures are controlled. Only a slight change in the geometry of the individual subunit is needed to convert a flat hexagonal sheet into a tube, or with more change into a hollow sphere.

Q: Why can no more than 60 identical subunits pack into a virus capsid in a precisely symmetrical way, and how do real viruses get around it?
![The assembly pathway of the tomato bushy stunt virus capsid from dimers, with the three slightly different subunit conformations coloured differently](assets/img/fig-3-28.png)

Real viruses get around it by allowing slight irregularities, which lets more subunits build a larger capsid that still retains icosahedral symmetry. Tomato bushy stunt virus is about 33 nm across and is built from 180 identical copies of a 386-amino-acid capsid protein, arranged as 90 dimers, around an RNA genome of 4500 nucleotides. The price is paid by the protein: to fit into three somewhat different environments it must adopt three slightly different conformations, so the capsid protein has to be unusually adaptable.

Q: Tobacco mosaic virus was the first large macromolecular aggregate shown to self-assemble. What is it made of, and what does the demonstration establish?

Q: Insulin cannot refold on its own after denaturation, even though it is a small protein. Why not?
![Proinsulin folding and stabilization by disulfide bonds, removal of the connecting peptide to leave two-chain insulin, and the irreversible separation of the chains by reduction](assets/img/fig-3-30.png)

Proteolytic cleavage of this kind is an essential and irreversible step in normal assembly, and collagen is treated the same way.

Q+: Bacterial virus heads make the same point with a different trick. What do they use?

Because that second protein is absent from the finished particle, the head cannot spontaneously reassemble once it has been taken apart, and nothing about the finished structure reveals what was needed to build it. Q: What is an **amyloid fibril**, structurally?
![The alpha-synuclein monomer shown as an atomic model and schematically, with the beta strand forming the cross-beta spine, and how monomers stack into a sheet](assets/img/fig-3-31.png)
A: A self-propagating and very stable β-sheet aggregate built from a series of identical polypeptide chains layered one over the other, producing a continuous stack of β strands with every strand oriented perpendicular to the axis of the fibril. Two such stacks then pair with each other to make a long cross-beta filament. Many hundreds of monomers go into one, giving an unbranched fibrous structure that can be several micrometers long and 5 to 15 nm wide. The example illustrated is α-synuclein, the protein whose abnormal aggregates contribute to Parkinson's disease.

Q+: A surprisingly large fraction of proteins could in principle form amyloid, yet very few do. What accounts for both halves of that?
![How two alpha-synuclein monomers pair through a hydrophobic zipper to create the fibril spine, a cryo-EM rendering of the fibril, and an electron micrograph of the fibrils](assets/img/fig-3-32.png)

Q: Amyloid fibrils form in several tissues, so why are the most severe amyloid pathologies neurodegenerative?
A: Because the damage accumulates and the tissue cannot repair itself. The brain is a highly organized collection of nerve cells that cannot regenerate, so cells killed by accumulating fibrils are not replaced and the loss is cumulative. Abnormal amyloid formation is thought to play a central causative role in both Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases. Amyloid does cause pathology elsewhere in the body, but those tissues can tolerate and replace losses in a way the brain cannot.

Q: What makes **prion** diseases different from Alzheimer's or Parkinson's, and which diseases are they?
![The rare conformational change of PrP into the misfolded PrP\* form, and how PrP\* converts normal PrP through heterodimer and homodimer to a stable amyloid fibril](assets/img/fig-3-33.png)
A: That they can readily spread from one organism to another, provided the second eats a tissue containing the protein aggregate. The set of closely related diseases is scrapie in sheep, Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease and kuru in humans, and bovine spongiform encephalopathy in cattle. All are caused by a misfolded aggregated form of PrP, the prion protein, which normally sits on the outer surface of the plasma membrane and is most prominent in neurons. Its unfortunate property is that its amyloid fibrils are infectious in the sense that they convert normally folded PrP into the same pathological form, called PrP*, which creates a positive feedback loop propagating the abnormal conformation from cell to cell in the brain.

Q+: Given that positive feedback, why is anyone without PrP\* safe?
A: Because the first step is the hard one. In the absence of PrP*, PrP is extraordinarily difficult to convert into its abnormal form, so the rare spontaneous conformational change is the bottleneck and the chain reaction almost never starts. Once seeded, though, the barrier no longer applies, which is why eating tissue containing PrP* is dangerous and why BSE spread from cattle to humans.

Q: Studying prions in yeast revealed a feature that sounds impossible for a protein-only agent. What is it?
A: That several different "strains" of infectious particle can arise from a single polypeptide chain. A prion protein can form several distinctly different types of amyloid fibril from the same sequence, and each type of aggregate is separately infectious, forcing normal molecules to adopt that same abnormal structure rather than one generic one. Strain identity is therefore carried in conformation rather than sequence, which is the whole point of protein-only inheritance.

Q+: How far does the prion mechanism extend into the common neurodegenerative diseases?
A: Recent data suggest at least some of the abnormal amyloids in common human neurological disease promote it by spreading cell to cell in the brain in a prion-like way, with the misfolded protein taken up by neighbouring cells where it seeds more of the same structure. α-Synuclein in Parkinson's disease and tau protein in Alzheimer's disease are the named examples. The careful distinction is that this is spread within a brain rather than between organisms, so these diseases are not infectious in the way prion diseases are. Drugs and antibody treatments are being designed to block the spreading events.

Q: Amyloid was discovered as a disease structure, but cells use it deliberately. What are the two normal functions given?
![Amyloid fibrils storing peptide hormone densely in a secretory granule and dissolving after exocytosis, and bacterial amyloid fibrils projecting from the cell surface into a biofilm](assets/img/fig-3-34.png)

Eukaryotic cells store peptide and protein hormones such as glucagon and calcitonin at high concentration in the dense structured cores of secretory vesicles, and those cores are amyloid fibrils whose particular structure causes them to dissolve and release soluble cargo once secreted to the cell exterior. Since biofilms help bacteria survive adverse conditions including antibiotic treatment, drugs that disrupt bacterial amyloid networks are a promising line for treating infections.

Q: What is a **ligand**, and what does it take for a protein to bind one tightly?
![A ligand fitting precisely into a protein's binding site like a hand into a glove, shown schematically and as a space-filling model](assets/img/fig-3-35.png)
A: A ligand is whatever a protein binds, from an ion to a small molecule to another macromolecule, from the Latin *ligare*, to bind. Tight binding requires a set of weak noncovalent bonds, hydrogen bonds, electrostatic attractions and van der Waals attractions, together with favourable hydrophobic interactions. Since each individual bond is weak, effective binding only happens when many form at once, and that in turn is only possible if the surface contours of the ligand fit the protein closely, matching like a hand in a glove. All proteins bind something, sometimes tightly and sometimes weakly, but always with great specificity: a protein usually binds just one or a few molecules out of the many thousands of types it meets.
Tags: binding

Q: Where on a protein is the binding site, and why can a mutation buried in the interior destroy it?
![A crevice on a protein surface formed by folding, and a close-up of cyclic AMP bound by hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions](assets/img/fig-3-36.png)
A: Usually a cavity or crevice in the surface, formed by a particular arrangement of amino acid side chains which often belong to quite different portions of the chain brought together by folding. Buried interior atoms never touch the ligand, but they form the framework that gives the surface its contours and its chemical and mechanical properties, so even small changes to interior amino acids can shift the three-dimensional shape enough to destroy a surface binding site. Separate regions of the surface generally provide sites for different ligands, which is what allows a protein's activity to be regulated.
Tags: binding

Q: How can a protein keep water out of a binding site without also excluding the ligand, given that water is far smaller?
A: By exploiting the fact that water molecules are not free agents. Water has a strong tendency to hydrogen-bond to other water, so it exists in a large hydrogen-bonded network, and an individual molecule must break away from that network to reach into a crevice on a protein surface. That is energetically unfavourable, so the crevice stays dry without any need for a size filter. Keeping it dry matters because water readily forms hydrogen bonds that would otherwise compete with the ligand for sites on the surface, so excluding it lets the ligand form tighter hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions.

Q+: The second way a protein surface enhances its own chemistry is by clustering polar side chains. What does that achieve?
![The Asp-His-Ser catalytic triad of chymotrypsin and other serine proteases, with the aspartate inducing the histidine to remove a proton from the serine](assets/img/fig-3-37.png)
A: It changes the reactivity of groups that would be unremarkable in isolation. Folding a number of negatively charged side chains together against their mutual repulsion greatly increases the site's affinity for a positively charged ion. More dramatically, hydrogen bonding between side chains can make a normally unreactive group reactive: in the Asp-His-Ser catalytic triad of the serine proteases, the aspartate induces the histidine to remove a proton from a particular serine, which activates the serine so that its ordinarily inert -CH2OH can form a covalent bond with the substrate and hydrolyse a peptide bond. The reactivity of a protein surface therefore depends not only on which side chains are exposed but on their exact orientation relative to one another, which is why two slightly different conformations of the same protein can differ greatly in chemistry.

Q: What is **evolutionary tracing**, and what does it find?
![Front and back space-filling views of the SH2 domain with evolutionarily conserved surface amino acids coloured, and the same domain with its bound phosphorylated polypeptide](assets/img/fig-3-38.png)
A: A method for identifying the sites in a protein domain most crucial to its function. The amino acids that are the same, or nearly so, across all known members of the family are mapped onto a three-dimensional model of one member, and the most invariant positions usually turn out to form one or more clusters on the surface which correspond to ligand-binding sites. Applied to the SH2 domain, the slowest-changing residues are exactly those at the binding site for the phosphorylated polypeptide. The logic behind it is worth stating plainly: mutation is a random process but survival is not, so natural selection produces the conservation by preferentially eliminating organisms whose SH2 domains have been altered in a way that inactivates the site. Since genome sequencing has revealed huge numbers of proteins of unknown function, one solved structure per family plus evolutionary tracing gives a useful start on the rest.

Q: Three types of interface let one protein bind another. Which are they, and what is each good for?
![Three ways two proteins bind: a rigid surface against an extended string of polypeptide, two alpha helices forming a coiled-coil, and two complementary rigid surfaces](assets/img/fig-3-39.png)
A: A surface-string interaction, where a rigid surface on one protein binds an extended loop of chain on the other, which is how an SH2 domain recognizes a phosphorylated loop and how a protein kinase recognizes its substrates. A helix-helix interaction, where one α helix from each protein pairs into a coiled-coil, found in several families of transcription regulators. And a surface-surface interaction, the precise matching of two complementary rigid surfaces, which can be very tight and extremely specific because many weak bonds form between well-matched surfaces, specific enough to let a protein select one partner from the thousands in a cell. Binding can also involve the pairing of β strands.

Q: An antibody must be able to recognize any of billions of antigens. How does the immune system get that diversity without inventing billions of protein folds?
![A Y-shaped antibody of two heavy and two light chains held by disulfide bonds, with antigen-binding sites formed where variable domains meet, and a light chain showing the hypervariable loops](assets/img/fig-3-40.png)
A: By varying only the loops. An antibody is Y-shaped with two identical binding sites, each formed from several loops of polypeptide that protrude from the ends of a pair of closely juxtaposed domains. The genes encoding different antibodies generate enormous diversity by changing only the length and amino acid sequence of those loops, leaving the basic protein structure untouched. The molecule itself is four chains, two identical heavy and two identical smaller light chains held together by disulfide bonds, and each binding site is formed where a heavy-chain variable domain and a light-chain variable domain come close together. Both constant and variable domains are sandwiches of two antiparallel β sheets connected by a disulfide bond.

Q+: Why are protruding loops so well suited to forming binding sites generally, not just in antibodies?
A: Because a loop can wrap around a ligand rather than merely presenting a face to it. That lets a large number of chemical groups surround the bound molecule so the protein can link to it through many weak bonds at once, which is what tight binding requires. It is the same reason the β-sheet-based modules succeeded evolutionarily: a stable core with protruding loops gives new binding specificities cheaply, since only the loops need change.

Q: Why does a population of antibodies and ligands reach an equilibrium rather than ending with everything bound?
![Molecule A encountering B, C and D, with poorly matched surfaces breaking apart under thermal motion and the well-matched pair staying bound](assets/img/fig-3-41.png)
A: Because two processes run at once and only their rates are fixed, not the outcome. Ligands keep bumping into binding sites and forming complexes, so the number of complexes rises, but as complexes accumulate a second process becomes increasingly important: individual complexes break apart from thermally induced motion. Eventually the number of association events per second exactly equals the number of dissociation events, and that steady state is equilibrium. Whether a given collision leads anywhere depends on surface match, since poorly matched surfaces form only a few weak bonds and are pulled apart by thermal motion immediately, while a good match forms enough bonds to withstand the jolting.

Q: The equilibrium constant $K$ measures binding strength. What are its units, and what ligand concentration half-occupies the binding sites?
![The dissociation and association rate equations, their equality at equilibrium giving K as kon over koff, and a table relating equilibrium constants to standard free-energy differences](assets/img/fig-3-42.png)
A: For two molecules A and B binding to form a complex AB, $K$ has units of liters/mole, and half the binding sites are occupied when the ligand concentration in moles/liter reaches $1/K$. $K$ is larger the stronger the binding, and it is a direct measure of the free-energy difference between the bound and free states, since at equilibrium the association and dissociation rates are equal and $K = k_{on}/k_{off}$. Because it is an exponential function of that free-energy difference, even a change of a few noncovalent bonds has a striking effect on binding.

Q: How much does the standard free-energy difference have to change to move the equilibrium constant by a factor of 10, and why is that number worth remembering?
A: At 37°C, every 5.91 kJ/mole decrease in standard free energy multiplies the equilibrium constant by ten. It is worth remembering because it converts a thermodynamic quantity into a countable one: a single hydrogen bond is worth a few kJ/mole, so gaining or losing two or three noncovalent bonds shifts binding by orders of magnitude. That is the arithmetic behind the claim that small changes in weak bonding have drastic effects on binding.

Q+: Take 1000 molecules of A and 1000 of B in a eukaryotic cell, giving each a concentration of about $10^{-9}$ M. How does the number of AB complexes change when $K$ drops from $10^{10}$ to $10^{8}$?

Q+: The equilibrium constant here is written as an association constant. What is the other convention, and how do they relate?
A: As defined in this treatment, $K$ is the association or affinity constant $K_a$, with units of liters/mole, and it gets larger as binding gets tighter. The reciprocal is the dissociation constant $K_d$, in moles/liter, which gets smaller as binding gets tighter. Both are widely used, so the direction of the inequality depends on which one is being quoted, and $K_d$ has the convenience of being directly readable as the ligand concentration that half-occupies the binding sites.

Q: What makes an enzyme different from a protein that merely binds something, and how specific are enzymes in practice?
A: For many proteins binding is the whole job, as it is for an actin molecule joining a filament. For an enzyme, binding its ligands, which are called substrates, is only the necessary first step, after which it catalyses the breaking or forming of covalent bonds and converts them into products, repeatedly and without being changed itself. Speed-ups of a million-fold or more are ordinary. The specificity is severe: hexokinase adds a phosphate to D-glucose but ignores its optical isomer L-glucose, and the blood-clotting enzyme thrombin cuts one type of blood protein between a particular arginine and its adjacent glycine and nowhere else.

Q: Enzyme names nearly all end in "-ase". Which ones do not, and why?
A: Pepsin, trypsin, thrombin and lysozyme, among others. They were discovered and named before the convention became generally accepted at the end of the nineteenth century, so the exceptions are historical accidents rather than a separate class of enzyme. Where the convention does apply, the common name usually indicates the substrate or product plus the nature of the reaction, as citrate synthase catalyses the synthesis of citrate from acetyl CoA and oxaloacetate.

Q: Match these enzyme classes to what they do: **nuclease**, **ligase**, **isomerase**, **phosphatase**, **oxido-reductase**.
A: A nuclease breaks down nucleic acids by hydrolysing the bonds between nucleotides, with endonucleases cutting within the chain and exonucleases from its ends. A ligase joins two molecules together in an energy-dependent process, as DNA ligase joins two DNA molecules end to end through phosphodiester bonds. An isomerase catalyses the rearrangement of bonds within a single molecule. A phosphatase catalyses the hydrolytic removal of a phosphate group, the opposite of a kinase. An oxido-reductase catalyses reactions in which one molecule is oxidized while another is reduced, and is often named more specifically as an oxidase, reductase or dehydrogenase.

Q+: **ATPase** and **GTPase** appear in that same table, but they name something slightly different. What?
A: They name an activity rather than a dedicated class of metabolic enzyme. An ATPase hydrolyses ATP and a GTPase hydrolyses GTP, but many proteins with a wide range of roles carry such an energy-harnessing activity as one part of what they do, rather than existing to catalyse that hydrolysis for its own sake. Motor proteins such as myosin and membrane transport proteins such as the sodium-potassium pump are ATPases in this sense, and the large family of GTP-binding proteins are GTPases with central roles in regulating cell processes.

Q: Write out the basic path of an enzyme reaction, and explain why the rate stops rising when substrate is added.
![Enzyme kinetics: the rate of reaction rising with substrate concentration to a maximum Vmax, with Km marked at half-maximal rate](assets/img/fig-3-44.png)
A: The path is $E + S \rightarrow ES \rightarrow EP \rightarrow E + P$. Raising the substrate concentration raises the rate at which product forms, but only until every substrate-binding site on every enzyme molecule is occupied. At that point the enzyme is saturated, adding more substrate changes nothing because there is nowhere for it to bind, and the rate has reached $V_{max}$, which depends only on how quickly the enzyme can process a substrate molecule once it has one.
Tags: enzymes, kinetics

Q: What is the **turnover number**, and what values does it take?
A: The maximum rate divided by the enzyme concentration, so it is the number of substrate molecules processed per second per enzyme molecule. Turnover numbers are often around 1000 per second, though the known range runs from 1 to 10,000. It is the honest measure of an enzyme's speed because it has divided out how much enzyme happened to be present, unlike $V_{max}$ itself.

Q: What does a low $K_m$ tell you, and what is the caveat in reading it as a binding constant?
A: $K_m$ is the substrate concentration at which the reaction runs at half its maximum rate. A low value means the enzyme reaches its maximum catalytic rate at low substrate concentration, which generally indicates that it binds substrate very tightly, while a high value corresponds to weak binding. The caveat is in that word "generally": $K_m$ only approximates the dissociation constant $K_d$ for substrate binding. Since $K_m = (k_{-1} + k_{cat})/k_1$, it equals $K_d$ only when $k_{cat}$ is much smaller than $k_{-1}$, meaning the complex falls apart far more often than it turns over. For a fast enzyme the two part company.

Q: State the Michaelis-Menten equation and say what each term is doing.
![Panel 3-2, first page: why enzyme kinetics is analysed, and the steady-state derivation of the Michaelis-Menten equation](assets/img/panel-3-02.png)
A: $$V = \frac{V_{max}[S]}{K_m + [S]}$$ where $V$ is the velocity of the reaction, $[S]$ the substrate concentration, $V_{max} = k_{cat}[E_o]$ the maximum rate when essentially all the enzyme is bound to substrate, and $K_m$ the substrate concentration giving half that rate. It is derived by assuming a steady state, in which $[ES]$ rises rapidly from zero after mixing and then stays nearly constant because ES is broken down as fast as it forms, and by ignoring the reverse reaction in which free enzyme and product recombine, which happens rarely enough to neglect.

Q: Why is a **double-reciprocal plot** used, and what do its slope and intercepts give?
![Panel 3-2, second page: the double-reciprocal plot, the significance of Km, kcat and kcat/Km, and a table of diffusion-limited enzymes](assets/img/panel-3-02b.png)
A: Because neither $V_{max}$ nor $K_m$ is immediately clear from a plot of $V$ against $[S]$: the curve only approaches $V_{max}$ asymptotically, so reading it off by eye is guesswork. Rearranging the Michaelis-Menten equation to $$\frac{1}{V} = \left(\frac{K_m}{V_{max}}\right)\frac{1}{[S]} + \frac{1}{V_{max}}$$ makes the relationship a straight line when $1/V$ is plotted against $1/[S]$. The slope is $K_m/V_{max}$, the intercept on the vertical axis is $1/V_{max}$, and the intercept on the horizontal axis is $-1/K_m$, so both constants can be extracted from a fitted line rather than estimated from a curve.

Q+: The ratio $k_{cat}/K_m$ is treated as the most meaningful single measure of an enzyme. Why, and what is its ceiling?
A: Because it is the rate constant for the reaction between free enzyme and free substrate. At very low substrate concentration, where $[S] \ll K_m$, most of the enzyme is free, so $[E] \approx [E_o]$ and the Michaelis-Menten equation simplifies to $V = (k_{cat}/K_m)[E][S]$. Comparing $k_{cat}/K_m$ for one enzyme with different substrates, or two enzymes with theirs, is therefore widely used as a measure of enzyme effectiveness. Its ceiling is set by physics rather than chemistry: an enzyme and its substrate must collide before they can react, and diffusion theory gives a maximum between $10^8$ and $10^9$ $\text{sec}^{-1}\text{M}^{-1}$ if every collision forms a complex and all subsequent steps are immediate.

Q+: Which enzymes actually sit at that diffusion limit, and what does the phrase "perfect enzyme" mean?

Q: How does an enzyme actually accelerate a reaction, beyond just holding two substrates near each other?
![Free-energy diagram comparing the activation energy for an uncatalysed reaction with the lower activation energy of the enzyme-catalysed route through ES and EP](assets/img/fig-3-45.png)
A: Increasing the local concentration of substrates at the catalytic site and holding them in the right orientation is real but secondary. The more important contribution is that some of the binding energy goes directly into catalysis. Substrates must pass through intermediate states of altered geometry and electron distribution, and the free energy needed to reach the most unstable of these, the transition state, is the activation energy that mainly determines the reaction rate. An enzyme has a much higher affinity for the transition state than for the stable form of its substrate, so binding it tightly lowers its energy and therefore lowers the activation energy. Where a reaction passes through several transition states, it is the one with the highest energy that sets the rate.

Q+: An enzyme is said to bind the transition state more tightly than the substrate. What would go wrong if it bound the substrate most tightly instead?
- [ ] The reaction would run backwards
- [x] The enzyme would stabilize the starting material and raise the barrier it needs to lower
- [ ] The enzyme would be consumed by the reaction
- [ ] Nothing, since binding energy is binding energy
A: It would stabilize the wrong state. Lowering the energy of the substrate deepens the well the reaction has to climb out of, so the gap up to the transition state grows and the reaction gets slower rather than faster. Catalysis is a matter of preferential stabilization, so what matters is not how tightly an enzyme binds anything in absolute terms but how much more tightly it binds the transition state than the ground state.

Q: Peptide bonds can be hydrolysed by strong acid or strong base without any enzyme. What can an enzyme do that neither can?
![The uncatalysed hydrolysis of a peptide bond compared with acid catalysis, base catalysis, both at once as performed by an enzyme, and the tetrahedral intermediate](assets/img/fig-3-47.png)

In solution the two catalysts would simply neutralize each other, but the rigid framework of a protein holds its acidic and basic residues in position and prevents them from combining, so both effects act on the same reaction at once. Q+: That $10^{23}$ figure is doing something slightly different from the others, and conflating the two is a standard trap. What is the distinction?

Q: Lysozyme is the worked example of enzyme mechanism. What does it do, and why was it chosen?

Q+: The reaction lysozyme catalyses is energetically favourable, so why does the polysaccharide not simply fall apart on its own?

Q: Trace the lysozyme reaction through its four stages, naming the two catalytic residues and what each does.
![Events at the active site of lysozyme, from the strained sugar D in the enzyme-substrate complex through the covalent transition state to the hydrolysis products](assets/img/fig-3-49.png)

Q: A cell contains thousands of enzymes working at once in the same small volume. At how many levels does it regulate them?

Q: What is **feedback inhibition**, and what makes it well suited to its job?
![Feedback inhibition of a biosynthetic pathway, where the end product Z inhibits the first enzyme unique to its own synthesis](assets/img/fig-3-52.png)
A: A product made late in a reaction pathway inhibits an enzyme acting earlier in it, so that when large quantities of the final product accumulate, the product binds that enzyme, slows it, and limits further entry of substrate into the pathway. It suits the job because it is fast in both directions: it works almost instantaneously and is rapidly reversed when the product level falls, so a pathway can track demand without waiting for changes in gene expression. It is a form of negative regulation, since it prevents an enzyme from acting. The enzyme inhibited is characteristically the first one unique to that product's synthesis, so the pathway is shut off at the earliest point that does not also block anything else.

Q: Give an example of **positive** regulation, where a regulatory molecule stimulates rather than inhibits.

Q: Where does the word **allostery** come from, and what does an allosteric protein need in order to work?
A: From the Greek *allos*, meaning "other", and *stereos*, meaning "solid" or "three-dimensional", which names the striking observation behind the concept: the regulatory molecule often has a shape totally different from the substrate. Such an enzyme needs at least two different binding sites on its surface, an active site recognizing the substrate and a separate regulatory site recognizing the regulatory molecule, and the two must communicate. That communication is a conformational change: binding at one site shifts the protein from one folded shape to a slightly different one, and in feedback inhibition the inhibitor's binding shifts it to a conformation that incapacitates the active site located elsewhere.
Tags: regulation, allostery

---

Q: How widespread is allostery thought to be?
A: Most protein molecules are thought to be allosteric, not just the enzymes where the effect was discovered. Proteins can adopt many slightly different conformations, and a shift from one to another on binding a ligand can alter their activity, which applies equally to receptors, structural proteins and motor proteins. The unifying statement is that each conformation has somewhat different surface contours, so the protein's binding sites for ligands are altered whenever it changes shape, and each ligand will stabilize the conformation it binds most strongly, tending at high enough concentration to switch the protein into that conformation.
Tags: regulation, allostery

Q: What is **linkage**, and why must its effects be reciprocal?
![Positive regulation by conformational coupling, where both glucose and molecule X bind best to the closed conformation and so help each other bind](assets/img/fig-3-54.png)
A: If a protein binds glucose and also binds another molecule X at a distant site, and the site for X changes shape as part of the conformational change induced by glucose binding, the two sites are said to be coupled. Whenever two ligands prefer the same conformation, basic thermodynamics requires that each increases the protein's affinity for the other, so the protein binds glucose more tightly when X is present. The reciprocity is not a separate empirical fact but a consequence of the two ligands sharing one conformational equilibrium, and it is quantitative: if glucose has a very large effect on the binding of X, then X has a very large effect on the binding of glucose.

Q+: Run linkage the other way. What happens when two ligands prefer different conformations?
![Negative regulation by conformational coupling, where molecule X prefers the open conformation and glucose the closed, so each interferes with the other](assets/img/fig-3-55.png)
A: Binding of the first ligand discourages binding of the second. If the shape change caused by glucose binding decreases the protein's affinity for X, then binding X must also decrease the protein's affinity for glucose, by the same reciprocity. Since molecule X binds at a site distinct from where catalysis happens, it need have no chemical relationship to the substrate at all, and it can either turn an enzyme on or turn it off. That is what lets allosteric proteins serve as general switches by which, in principle, one molecule in a cell can affect the fate of any other.

Q: A single-subunit enzyme responds to an inhibitor too gently for good regulation. Put a number on "too gently".

Q+: How does building the enzyme from symmetrical identical subunits sharpen that response?

Q+: There are two models of the cooperative transition. What are they, and which is correct?

Q: Phosphorylation is the commonest regulatory modification in higher eukaryotes. What three things can adding a phosphate do to a protein?

Q: Which side chains get phosphorylated, where does the phosphate come from, and why is the reaction essentially one-way?

The state of phosphorylation of a protein at any moment, and so its activity, depends on the relative activities of the kinases and phosphatases acting on it.

Q+: What holds a Src-family kinase in its inactive conformation, and what does switching it on require?

Q+: Why is Src described as a microprocessor rather than just a switch?
![Src-type kinase activity as a set of inputs asking whether a phosphate has been removed, a binding disrupted and a phosphate added, with full activity only if all answers are yes](assets/img/fig-3-62.png)
A: Because it computes rather than merely responds. Its activity turns on fully only if the answers to several independent questions are all yes: has this phosphate been removed, has this binding been disrupted, has this phosphate been added. It therefore behaves as an input-output device performing something like a logical AND, and its activation signals the completion of a particular set of separate upstream events rather than the arrival of any one signal. That is what lets a cell integrate and amplify many extracellular signals and compute useful responses to a complex set of conditions.

Q: GTP-binding proteins are switched by phosphate too, but not by a kinase. How does the switch work?
![The GTPase cycle: an active GTP-bound protein, GTP hydrolysis releasing phosphate to give the inactive GDP-bound form, slow GDP dissociation and fast GTP rebinding](assets/img/fig-3-63.png)
A: The phosphate is not transferred onto the protein at all. It is part of a guanosine triphosphate molecule bound tightly to the protein, which is in its "on" conformation while GTP is bound. The protein hydrolyses that GTP to GDP, releasing inorganic phosphate and flipping itself to the "off" conformation. Resetting requires the tightly bound GDP to dissociate, which is the slow step and the one cell signals control, followed by rapid binding of a fresh GTP. Hundreds of different GTP-binding proteins work as such molecular switches, and they all contain variations of the same globular domain that changes conformation when its bound GTP is hydrolysed.

Q+: Ras is the prototype of that family. What changes when its GTP is hydrolysed?
![The structure of the Ras protein with GTP bound, with the regions that change conformation on hydrolysis highlighted and the switch helix marked](assets/img/fig-3-64.png)
A: Only a small part of the structure. Ras is a monomeric GTPase with important roles in cell signalling, and when its GTP is hydrolysed to GDP and inorganic phosphate, the GDP remains bound while the phosphate is released, and a limited set of regions change conformation. Among them is a piece of α helix called the switch helix, which is what carries the news of the hydrolysis outward from the nucleotide-binding site to the rest of the protein.

Q: What is **ubiquitin**, and how is it attached to a target protein?
![The three-dimensional structure of ubiquitin, and the patterns of monoubiquitylation, multiubiquitylation and Lys48 and Lys63 polyubiquitylation with their meanings](assets/img/fig-3-65.png)

Q: Ubiquitin can be attached in several patterns, and they mean different things. Which linkage sends a protein to the proteasome?
A: A polyubiquitin chain in which each successive ubiquitin is linked to Lys48 of the previous one, all hanging off a single lysine of the target protein. A related family member called SUMO is attached to lysines in the same way for different purposes.

Q: Walk through the E1, E2, E3 system. What does each do?
![Activation of ubiquitin by E1 through a thioester bond via an AMP-ubiquitin intermediate, transfer to E2, and addition of a polyubiquitin chain to a target protein by E2 with E3](assets/img/fig-3-66.png)
A: E1, the ubiquitin-activating enzyme, uses the energy of ATP hydrolysis to attach ubiquitin to itself through a high-energy thioester bond to one of its own cysteine side chains, proceeding via a covalent AMP-ubiquitin intermediate. E1 then passes the activated ubiquitin to the cysteine of one of a set of E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes. Each E2 acts together with accessory E3 proteins, the ubiquitin ligases, which are what actually select the target proteins by binding specific degradation signals called degrons. There are roughly 30 distinct E2 enzymes in mammals and hundreds of different E3 proteins forming complexes with them, giving several hundred distinct E2-E3 combinations, so the specificity lives almost entirely in the E3 layer.

Q: The SCF ubiquitin ligase is used to illustrate a general design principle. What is the structure, and what is the principle?
![The five-subunit C-shaped SCF ubiquitin ligase with its E2 enzyme and interchangeable F-box substrate-binding arms, and the anaphase-promoting complex shown at the same scale](assets/img/fig-3-67.png)
A: A C-shaped structure of five protein subunits, the largest of which, a cullin, is a scaffold on which the rest is built. An E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme sits at one end of the C and a substrate-binding arm called an F-box protein at the other, separated by a gap of about 5 nm. When activated, the F-box protein binds a specific site on a target and positions it in that gap so some of its lysine side chains reach the E2, which then catalyses repeated additions of ubiquitin. The principle is interchangeable parts: many of the F-box arms are swappable, more than 70 human genes encode them, and there is a whole family of cullin scaffolds, so a small number of designs generate a large number of specificities. That makes economical use of genetic information and allows rapid evolution, since a new function for the whole complex needs only an alternative version of one subunit.

Q+: What sets the timing of a target protein's destruction by SCF?
A: Usually a specific pattern of phosphorylation on the target, which is required before the F-box subunit will recognize it, together with activation of an SCF-like ligase carrying the appropriate substrate-binding arm. So proteolysis is put under the control of the phosphorylation system rather than being timed by the ligase itself, which is how specific proteins get destroyed at specific moments to drive the cell cycle forward. Ubiquitin ligases form a diverse family, some far larger than SCF, such as the 15-protein anaphase-promoting complex whose ubiquitylations control the late stages of mitosis.

Q: EF-Tu shows how a tiny chemical event produces a large movement. Trace the amplification.

Q: What is EF-Tu actually for, and why is the timing of that GTP hydrolysis the interesting part?
![An aminoacyl tRNA bound to EF-Tu, with the bound protein blocking use of the tRNA-linked amino acid until GTP hydrolysis dissociates the complex](assets/img/fig-3-69.png)

The timing is what makes it a proofreader: GTP hydrolysis is triggered by a proper fit of the tRNA to the mRNA on the ribosome, so EF-Tu only lets go when the pairing is correct, and it therefore discriminates between correct and incorrect mRNA-tRNA pairings rather than merely delivering cargo. Q: A protein undergoing a cycle of shape changes along a filament gets nowhere. Why, and what fixes it?
![A protein cycling between three conformations as it moves along a cytoskeletal filament, wandering randomly without an input of energy](assets/img/fig-3-70.png)
A: Because with nothing driving the changes in an orderly sequence they are perfectly reversible, so the protein wanders randomly back and forth and ends up nowhere. Put thermodynamically, directional movement does work, so it must consume free energy from some source or the protein would be a perpetual motion machine. The fix is to make any one of the shape changes irreversible, which is enough to force the whole cycle to run one way. Most proteins that walk long distances achieve this by coupling one conformational change to the hydrolysis of a tightly bound ATP.
Tags: machines, motors

Q+: Trace the three-conformation cycle of a motor protein, and say which step is the ratchet.
![An allosteric motor protein driven by ATP hydrolysis walking along a filament, and a walking myosin motor visualized by high-speed atomic force microscopy](assets/img/fig-3-71.png)
A: ATP binding shifts the protein from conformation 1 to conformation 2. The bound ATP is then hydrolysed to ADP and inorganic phosphate, driving the change from conformation 2 to conformation 3. Finally, release of the bound ADP and phosphate returns the protein to conformation 1, one step further along. The ratchet is the middle step: because ATP hydrolysis releases a great deal of free energy, running that transition backward would require the protein to resynthesize ATP from ADP and phosphate, which is vanishingly unlikely. That single irreversible step makes the whole cycle effectively one-way.
Tags: machines, motors

Q+: Which motors use this ratchet, and how fast can such movement be?
A: Myosin, the muscle motor protein, walks along actin filaments, and the kinesins walk along microtubules, both by the same kind of unidirectional ratchet. The movements can be quick: some of the motor proteins involved in DNA replication, the DNA helicases, propel themselves along a DNA strand at rates as high as 1000 nucleotides per second. A walking myosin has been filmed directly by high-speed atomic force microscopy, with less than 0.5 seconds elapsing between steps.

Q: What is a **protein machine**, and why did cells evolve them?
![A schematic protein machine in which ATP-driven conformational changes move successive enzymes into position to catalyse a series of reactions](assets/img/fig-3-72.png)
A: A large assembly of protein molecules linked by noncovalent bonds that carries out one of the central processes of the cell. Now that most biological processes can be reconstructed in cell-free systems, it is clear that each central process, DNA replication, protein synthesis, vesicle budding or transmembrane signalling, is catalysed by a highly coordinated linked set of 10 or more proteins. In most of them, energetically favourable reactions such as hydrolysis of bound ATP or GTP drive an ordered series of conformational changes in the subunits, so the ensemble moves in a coordinated way and each enzyme is brought directly into position as successive reactions are catalysed. The reason offered is the same one that explains human machines: for almost any task, manipulations coordinated in space and time through linked processes are much more efficient than many separate tools.
Tags: machines

Q: Intrinsically disordered regions form binding sites of high specificity but usually low affinity. Why the combination, and why is it useful?
![Unstructured regions of polypeptide forming binding sites for other proteins, and the same regions carrying multiple phosphorylation sites that change their binding preferences](assets/img/fig-3-73.png)
A: The low affinity comes from a free-energy cost that a folded domain does not pay: the normally unfolded partner has to fold in order to bind, and that loss of conformational freedom is subtracted from the binding energy. Specificity survives because the folded-on-binding sequence still has to match. The combination is useful precisely because it makes the interaction readily reversible, so it can be formed and broken as a regulatory event rather than being permanent. These regions are also easy to modify covalently, and most protein phosphorylation sites lie in intrinsically disordered regions rather than in globular domains.

Q: Some disordered regions are called **low-complexity domains**. What does that mean, and why do we think disorder is functional at all?
A: A low-complexity domain is one built from only a limited subset of the 20 amino acids, so its sequence is repetitive rather than varied. The reason for believing disorder is functional rather than just unresolved structure is evolutionary: many unstructured regions have been conserved in a particular protein over long periods of evolutionary time, and conservation of that kind implies the regions benefit the organisms carrying them. Disordered regions otherwise tend to evolve rapidly, which is itself useful because it facilitates the fine-tuning and evolution of cell signalling networks.

Q+: Give the worked example of a disordered tail used as a timing device.
A: The eukaryotic RNA polymerase that makes mRNAs carries an unstructured C-terminal tail of 200 amino acids. As the polymerase proceeds along the gene, that tail is covalently modified, and the changing pattern of modification attracts different specific proteins to the transcription complex at different times. The disorder is what makes this possible: a rigid domain could present one interface, whereas a flexible tail carrying a shifting combinatorial mark can recruit a succession of partners as transcription progresses.

Q: **Elastin** uses disorder for something entirely unlike binding. What?
![Elastin molecules cross-linked into an elastic fiber, uncoiling when stretched and recoiling spontaneously when the stretching force is relaxed](assets/img/fig-3-74.png)
A: Elasticity. Elastin is an abundant extracellular matrix protein formed as a highly disordered polypeptide, and its relatively loose unstructured chains are covalently cross-linked in the extracellular space to produce a meshwork that can be stretched like a rubber band. Each molecule uncoils into a more extended conformation when the fibre is stretched and recoils spontaneously as soon as the force is relaxed. The resulting elastic fibers let skin and other tissues such as arteries and lungs stretch and recoil without tearing, which is a mechanical function rather than an informational one.

Q: How does mammalian fatty acid synthase use an unstructured region, and why is that better than releasing intermediates?
![The seven domains of the 270-kilodalton fatty acid synthase, the dimeric structure with five active sites per monomer, and the flexible tether carrying the intermediate between them](assets/img/fig-3-75.png)

Q: What is a **scaffold protein**, and what is the range of forms they take?
![A large scaffold protein whose unstructured regions tether structured domains binding a set of reacting proteins, speeding reactions by rapid random collision](assets/img/fig-3-76.png)
A: A protein with binding sites for multiple other proteins or RNA molecules, which both links specific sets of interacting macromolecules together and positions them at specific locations in the cell. At one extreme are rigid scaffolds such as the cullin in the SCF ubiquitin ligase, which holds its parts at a fixed separation. At the other are large flexible scaffolds whose long unstructured regions act as tethers, speeding reactions by causing rapid random collision among everything bound to the scaffold, and creating special regions inside the cell with a unique biochemistry. Networks of such large scaffolds often underlie regions of specialized plasma membrane.

Q: How does a **biomolecular condensate** differ from a macromolecular machine?
![Types of weak multivalent interaction that drive condensate formation between unstructured proteins, structured proteins and RNA, and a FRAP experiment on labelled nucleoli](assets/img/fig-3-77.png)
A: A machine is built from the same parts every time and assembled into the same three-dimensional structure with a defined stoichiometry, as the bacterial ribosome is built from 55 proteins and three RNAs in an invariant complex. A condensate is held together by a large number of weak and constantly changing interactions instead. Each one is created by at least one multivalent scaffold macromolecule, a protein or RNA able to make multiple independent interactions with itself or others. Its composition is selective but its stoichiometry is usually not fixed, and it is generally larger than most macromolecular machines.
Tags: condensates

---

Q+: If every individual interaction in a condensate is weak and constantly breaking, why does the condensate not simply fall apart?
A: Because the interactions are multivalent and their breaking is staggered. When any one interaction breaks, other interactions at different sites on that same macromolecule are still holding, which prevents it diffusing away and keeps it locally concentrated, and by the time those break new ones have already formed elsewhere. So every molecule in the condensate is continually interacting with the others even though the specific set of contacts changes moment to moment. That is also why a condensate behaves like a liquid, with its molecules jostling and rapidly exchanging positions, and often exchanging rapidly with their equivalents outside.
Tags: condensates

Q: What experiment demonstrates that molecules inside a condensate really are mobile?

The nucleoli of a mammalian cell were labelled by fusing GFP to a nucleolar scaffold protein, one nucleolus was bleached with a flash from a focused laser, and fluorescence in it recovered rapidly, which can only happen if labelled molecules from the surroundings are continually exchanging into the bleached condensate. One point of care: the 7th edition names the protein "fibrillin", but fibrillin is an extracellular matrix glycoprotein, and the nucleolar scaffold protein used in the Phair and Misteli experiment is fibrillarin.

Q: What do condensates actually do for a cell? Give the two contrasting uses.
![A UV laser line of DNA damage in an interphase nucleus recruiting GFP-labelled FUS scaffold protein into liquid-droplet-like DNA repair factories](assets/img/fig-3-79.png)

DNA repair, DNA replication and transcription factories in the nucleus appear only where and when those processes occur.

Q+: Condensate formation is called liquid-liquid phase separation. What visible behaviour justifies calling it that?

Q: Phase separation costs entropy, so why does it happen at all, and why is it easier for polymers than for small molecules?
![Phase diagrams showing a polymer separating into dilute and concentrated phases above a threshold concentration, and the two phases merging above a critical temperature](assets/img/fig-3-80.png)
A: Demixing is entropically expensive because there are far more ways of distributing molecules throughout a solution than of gathering them together, so the mixed state is much more probable and $\Delta S$ for demixing is large and unfavourable, contributing a positive term to $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$. Separation happens only when some even larger favourable contribution outweighs it, as excluding oil from the hydrogen-bonded network of water does for a salad dressing. Polymers have an advantage: their monomeric subunits are already greatly constrained in their possible arrangements by covalent attachment to their neighbours, so the entropic cost of demixing is much smaller than for an equivalent mass of small molecules, and a set of relatively weak attractions between polymers can be enough to drive separation.
Tags: condensates, thermodynamics

---

Q: Add more polymer to a phase-separated solution at fixed temperature. What changes?
A: Not the concentrations. Above the threshold, a concentrated phase at concentration C2 sits in equilibrium with a dilute phase at concentration C1, and adding more polymer leaves both of those values unchanged. What changes is volume: the concentrated phase grows and the dilute phase shrinks, until above C2 there is only a single phase again. In the illustrated case C2 is more than tenfold greater than C1. Raising the temperature at fixed total concentration does change the concentrations, with C1 rising and C2 falling until they meet at a critical temperature and the two phases become one, because the unfavourable $-T\Delta S$ term grows with temperature and eventually prevents separation.
Tags: condensates, thermodynamics

Q: Compare macromolecular machines, biomolecular condensates and membrane-enclosed compartments on permeability and how they form.
A: A machine has fixed macromolecular composition with defined stoichiometry and spatial organization, assembles spontaneously and can form de novo. A condensate is dynamic and liquid-like or gel-like, with RNAs and low-complexity domains forming specific but transient interactions, is selective in composition but not fixed in stoichiometry, is readily permeable to small molecules, and can assemble de novo and disassemble in response to changing conditions. A membrane-enclosed compartment is different in kind: it creates a distinct chemical and protein environment maintained by active transport across its membrane, is not permeable to most small molecules, its interior contents are set by those transport processes, and its formation usually requires a preexisting compartment of the same special kind rather than forming de novo.

Q: How many types of post-translational modification are known, and what does the p53 example show about combining them?

Q: Why is predicting a protein's structure from its sequence computationally hard, in one number?
A: Because of the size of the search space. A protein folds to its lowest free-energy state, so in principle one computes the energy of each conformation and picks the minimum, but for even a relatively short protein of 100 amino acids the number of possible conformations is of the order $3^{100}$, since each amino acid has on average 3 or more rotatable bonds. That is about $5 \times 10^{47}$ structures, so success depends entirely on methods for searching efficiently rather than exhaustively.
Tags: folding, design

Q+: What progress has been made in prediction and in design, and what remains hard?
![Three computationally designed proteins: a 122-amino-acid protein, an octahedral shell of 24 identical subunits, and an antiparallel three-helix bundle](assets/img/fig-3-83.png)
A: For small proteins, or ones from very large families where the family constrains the problem, large-scale computer searches for the lowest-energy state can often predict structure accurately from sequence, and recently developed deep learning approaches using artificial intelligence do better still. In the other direction, many new structures and functions have been created from scratch by designing sequences whose lowest-energy state is the desired structure, then producing them in bacteria and confirming the actual structure matches the intended one. What remains hard is function rather than structure: predicting function from structure by analogy is unreliable because even a few amino acid changes can considerably change it, such as which substrate an enzyme acts on, and designed proteins still fall well short of the activities of natural enzymes and of the information integration and force generation of natural molecular machines.

Q: True or false? Each strand in a β sheet is a helix with two amino acids per turn.
A: True, though it takes a moment to see. A helix is defined by a fixed rotation plus a fixed translation per subunit, and a β strand qualifies with a rotation of 180 degrees per residue, which is two amino acids per complete turn. That is exactly what produces the familiar feature of β sheets, that successive side chains project alternately above and below the plane of the sheet. It is a very extended helix rather than a coiled one, which is why it does not look like the α helix, but it is a helix by the definition given for the assembly of repeating subunits.

Q: True or false? Loops of polypeptide that protrude from the surface of a protein often form the binding sites for other molecules.
A: True, and for a reason worth restating. A loop can wrap around a ligand and surround it with chemical groups from several directions, so many weak bonds form at once, which is what tight binding requires. The immunoglobulin fold is the clearest case, since antibodies generate their entire diversity by varying only the length and sequence of the loops protruding from a constant framework. The same design explains why β-sheet-based modules spread so successfully in evolution: a new specificity costs only a change to the loops.

Q: True or false? An enzyme reaches a maximum rate at high substrate concentration because it has a fixed number of active sites where substrate binds.
A: True. Once every active site on every enzyme molecule is occupied, the enzyme is saturated and adding more substrate cannot increase the rate, because there is nowhere for the extra substrate to bind. At that point the rate no longer depends on substrate concentration at all and is set purely by how fast the enzyme can process a bound molecule, which is what $V_{max}$ measures.

Q: True or false? Higher concentrations of enzyme give rise to a higher turnover number for that enzyme.
- [ ] True, since more enzyme processes more substrate per second
- [x] False, because the turnover number has already divided out the amount of enzyme
- [ ] True, but only below saturating substrate
- [ ] False, because turnover number depends only on substrate concentration
A: False, and this is the trap the question is built around. Adding enzyme does raise $V_{max}$, since more enzyme molecules process more substrate per second in total. But the turnover number is $V_{max}$ divided by the enzyme concentration, which is the number of substrate molecules processed per second by each enzyme molecule. Dividing out the enzyme concentration is precisely what makes it a property of the enzyme rather than of the sample, so it does not change when more enzyme is added.

Q: True or false? Enzymes that undergo cooperative allosteric transitions invariably consist of symmetrical assemblies of multiple subunits.
A: False, and the word doing the damage is "invariably". Cooperativity does require multiple interacting binding sites, so a strictly single-site protein cannot show it, and most enzymes regulated this way are indeed symmetrical assemblies of identical subunits. But the subunits need not be identical, as hemoglobin shows with its two α-globin and two β-globin chains, and the sites need not sit on separate polypeptides at all, since a single chain folded into multiple domains can carry several communicating sites. Symmetry makes the cooperative mechanism especially clean, because binding disrupts and then restores favourable monomer-monomer contacts, but it is a common solution rather than a requirement.

Q: True or false? Continual addition and removal of phosphates by protein kinases and protein phosphatases is wasteful of energy, because their combined action consumes ATP, but it is a necessary consequence of effective regulation by phosphorylation.
A: False, though the first clause is accurate. The cycle really does consume one ATP per phosphorylation event and the phosphate is then simply removed again, so energy is spent with no net chemical product. Calling that waste is the error: what the ATP buys is the speed with which a population of protein molecules can change its state of phosphorylation when the phosphorylation rate changes, and the faster the cycle turns the faster the switch responds. Nor is it a necessary consequence of regulation by phosphorylation, since slow cycling would still regulate, just sluggishly. The expenditure is a purchase of responsiveness, not an unavoidable side effect.

Q: Titin acts as a spring in muscle. In an atomic force microscope experiment on a fragment containing seven Ig domains, the force-versus-extension curve is a sawtooth. Do the controls support sequential domain unfolding?
![The structure of a single titin Ig domain, and a sawtooth force-versus-extension curve rising to about 300 piconewtons over 200 nm of extension](assets/img/fig-q3-01.png)
A: Yes, and it is the two controls rather than the sawtooth itself that make the case. Titin has a mass of about $3 \times 10^6$ daltons, is the largest polypeptide described, and extends from muscle thick filaments to the Z disc, where it keeps the thick filaments centred in the sarcomere. In 8 M urea, a denaturant, the peaks disappear and the extension for a given force becomes much longer, which is what must happen if the domains are already unfolded and there is nothing left to unfold. After cross-linking with glutaraldehyde the peaks also disappear, but now the extension for a given force is much smaller, because the domains have been stapled shut and cannot unfold at all. Losing the peaks in opposite directions under the two treatments is exactly the signature of unfolding, and the number of peaks matches the number of Ig domains in the fragment.

Q+: Is the extension gained at each unfolding event the size you would expect? Amino acids in an extended chain are spaced at 0.34 nm.
A: Yes. Each Ig domain is 89 amino acids, so fully extended it would span $89 \times 0.34 = 30.3$ nm. It already contributes about 4 nm while folded, so unfolding one domain should add roughly $30.3 - 4 = 26$ nm to the length of the molecule. The sawtooth peaks in the data are spaced about 25 nm apart, which agrees within the accuracy of the estimate. Getting the right magnitude, and not merely the right shape, is what rules out the peaks being some other periodic event.

Q+: Why does the force collapse so abruptly after each peak?
A: Because unfolding a domain suddenly hands the molecule about 25 nm of extra chain. The tension in a stretched molecule depends on how far it is extended relative to its contour length, so when that contour length jumps in a single step, the molecule is abruptly no longer taut and the force it pulls back with drops almost to nothing. The microscope must then extend a further 25 nm or so before tension builds enough to unfold the next domain, which is what generates the repeating sawtooth rather than a smooth curve.

Q: "To produce one molecule of each possible kind of polypeptide chain 300 amino acids in length would require more atoms than exist in the universe." Check that by mass, given the observable universe holds about $1.7 \times 10^{56}$ g and an average amino acid is 110 daltons.
A: The statement is correct, by an enormous margin. There are $20^{300} \approx 10^{390}$ possible chains. Each weighs $300 \times 110 = 33{,}000$ daltons, which is $33{,}000 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-24} = 5.5 \times 10^{-20}$ g. One molecule of each would therefore weigh about $10^{390} \times 5.5 \times 10^{-20} \approx 10^{371}$ g. Against a universe of $1.7 \times 10^{56}$ g, the collection would outweigh everything that exists by about $10^{315}$ times. The point is not that the library is large but that it is physically impossible even in principle, so the proteins that exist can only ever be an unimaginably sparse sample of sequence space.

Q: The kelch motif is a four-stranded β sheet shaped like a propeller blade, repeated four to seven times to form a β propeller. Is a kelch repeat domain an in-line or a plug-in domain?
![The seven-bladed kelch repeat domain of galactose oxidase, with the N- and C-termini marked adjacent to one another](assets/img/fig-q3-02.png)
A: Plug-in. The test is where the two termini sit: an in-line domain has its N- and C-terminal ends at opposite poles, so tandem copies can be joined into an extended rod, whereas a plug-in domain has them close together and is usually accommodated as an insertion into a loop of another protein. Because the propeller blades are arranged around a central axis, the chain comes back round to where it started, and the figure shows N and C adjacent to each other. So a kelch domain inserts into another protein rather than stacking in series.

Q: Dimers of identical proteins could in principle be head-to-head, which is the same as tail-to-tail, or head-to-tail. Is one much more common, and why?
![Schematic of a head-to-head arrangement of two arrows pointing at each other and a head-to-tail arrangement of two arrows pointing the same way](assets/img/fig-q3-03.png)
A: Head-to-head is much more common, and the reason is that it needs only one kind of binding site. If a single surface patch on the monomer binds the identical patch on another copy, the two molecules pair through that one interaction and the result is a twofold symmetric dimer that is complete and stops there. A head-to-tail arrangement requires two different binding sites, a "head" site complementary to a "tail" site, and having made one such contact each partner still has a free head and a free tail, so nothing stops a third and fourth subunit joining. That arrangement therefore tends to produce a helical filament or a closed ring rather than a dimer, which is exactly what was described for actin.

Q: An antibody binds one protein with $K = 5 \times 10^9\ \text{M}^{-1}$. Against a related protein it forms three fewer hydrogen bonds, losing 11.9 kJ/mole of binding affinity. What is $K$ for the second protein? Use $\Delta G° = -2.3RT \log K$ with $R = 8.3 \times 10^{-3}$ kJ/(mole K) and $T = 310$ K.
A: About $5 \times 10^{7}\ \text{M}^{-1}$. The conversion factor is $2.3RT = 2.3 \times 8.3 \times 10^{-3} \times 310 = 5.92$ kJ/mole, which is the free-energy change corresponding to one factor of ten in $K$. Losing 11.9 kJ/mole is therefore $11.9 / 5.92 = 2.01$ log units, so $K$ falls by very nearly a hundredfold, from $5 \times 10^9$ to $4.9 \times 10^7\ \text{M}^{-1}$. Three hydrogen bonds costing two orders of magnitude in affinity is the same arithmetic as the worked example earlier in the chapter, where dropping from $K = 10^{10}$ to $10^8$ turned a mostly bound population into a mostly free one.

Q: For SmpB binding tmRNA, fraction bound $= [\text{SmpB}]/([\text{SmpB}] + K_d)$. Calculate the fraction bound at concentrations from $10^4 K_d$ down to $10^{-4} K_d$, and say what general rule the numbers illustrate.

Q: Hexokinase phosphorylates D-glucose and ignores L-glucose. Suppose you synthesized hexokinase entirely from D-amino acids. What relationship would the "D" enzyme have to the normal one, and which sugar would it act on?
A: The D enzyme would be the exact mirror image of the normal one, assuming it folds to a stable conformation, so every feature of its structure would be reflected: its α helices would be left-handed rather than right-handed, and every turn of the chain would be reversed in hand. Because its active site is the mirror image of the normal active site, and because the normal site is complementary to D-glucose, the mirrored site is complementary to L-glucose. So yes, the D enzyme would phosphorylate L-glucose and ignore D-glucose. Reflecting a lock reflects which key fits it, and nothing else about the chemistry changes, since mirror images have identical energies.

Q: Using rate $= V_{max}[S]/([S] + K_m)$, what are the rates at $[S] = 0$, $[S] = K_m$, and $[S] = \infty$?
A: At $[S] = 0$ the numerator is zero, so the rate is zero, as it must be with no substrate. At $[S] = K_m$ the expression becomes $V_{max}K_m/(K_m + K_m) = V_{max}/2$, which is the definition of $K_m$ recovered from the equation rather than assumed. At infinite substrate, $[S]$ swamps $K_m$ in the denominator and the ratio $[S]/([S]+K_m)$ tends to 1, giving the rate $V_{max}$. Substituting the three values is worth doing once, because it shows the equation encodes saturation and the meaning of $K_m$ rather than merely fitting a curve through data.

Q: AMP and GMP are made by a branched pathway from ribose 5-phosphate through a shared trunk to intermediate E, which then branches to each product. Propose a feedback strategy that keeps both supplied while minimizing buildup of intermediates.
![The branched pathway from R5P through intermediates A to E, splitting to F and G to give AMP and to H and I to give GMP](assets/img/fig-q3-05.png)
A: Put feedback at both branch points and at the head of the shared trunk. AMP should inhibit the first enzyme unique to its own branch, the one converting E to F, and GMP should inhibit the first enzyme unique to its branch, E to H. That is enough to stop either product being overproduced while leaving the other branch free, and it prevents the buildup of the intermediates specific to whichever branch is shut. The trunk from R5P to E still needs controlling, or intermediates A through E would accumulate when both products are plentiful, but it cannot simply be inhibited by either product alone, because a shortage of the other would then be impossible to make good. The solution is the one the chapter shows for the bacterial amino acid pathways: make full inhibition of the first common step require both AMP and GMP together, either by having two isozymes catalyse it with each inhibited by a different product, or by requiring both products to bind before the single enzyme is fully inhibited.

Q: How does hemoglobin manage to bind oxygen efficiently in the lungs and yet release it efficiently in the tissues?
A: By being cooperative rather than by having any particular affinity. Hemoglobin is a symmetrical assembly of four subunits, and binding oxygen to one subunit shifts the whole assembly toward a conformation with higher affinity, so the others bind more readily. That makes the binding curve sigmoidal instead of hyperbolic, and a steep curve is exactly what the job needs: across the fairly modest drop in oxygen concentration between the lungs and the tissues, saturation swings from nearly complete to substantially unloaded. A single-site carrier with simple one-to-one binding could not do it, since its shallow hyperbolic curve needs an 81-fold change in concentration to move from 90% to 10% occupancy, and no such difference exists between lungs and tissues. Any single fixed affinity would be either too tight to unload or too weak to load.

A: The normal Src is roughly 830 times more concentrated there. The whole cell has volume $\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 = \frac{4}{3}\pi (10)^3 = 4189\ \mu\text{m}^3$. The 4-nm shell beneath the membrane has volume $\frac{4}{3}\pi(r^3 - (r-t)^3)$ with $t = 0.004\ \mu\text{m}$, which comes to $5.02\ \mu\text{m}^3$, essentially the same as the thin-shell estimate $4\pi r^2 t$. Q+: Given that both forms have identical kinase activity, why does only the membrane-anchored Src drive proliferation?
A: Because the substrate is in the membrane. The target X for phosphorylation by Src resides there, so what matters is not how much kinase activity the cell contains but how much of it is in the thin layer where X is. The mutant is diluted through the whole cell volume and is therefore present near the membrane at roughly 1/830 of the normal concentration, which slows phosphorylation of X by about the same factor, far too little to drive proliferation. The lesson is that localization is a form of regulation in its own right: an enzyme with full catalytic activity in the wrong place is functionally inactive, which is why so many proteins carry lipid anchors, scaffold-binding sites and other addressing information.
