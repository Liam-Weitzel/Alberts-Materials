Q: An animal cell contains roughly 10 billion protein molecules of perhaps 10,000 kinds, and almost all of them begin their synthesis in the same place. Where, and what problem does that create?
A: In the cytosol, the part of the cytoplasm outside the membrane-enclosed organelles. Since synthesis starts in one shared compartment but the proteins are needed in a dozen different ones, every protein has to be delivered specifically to the organelle that requires it after it is made. The address is carried in the protein's own amino acid sequence. What keeps the system stable is that each organelle's distinctive surface protein and lipid composition acts as the cue directing new deliveries to it, so an organelle's identity is what lets it be resupplied, and being resupplied is what maintains its identity.
Tags: core, organelles

---

Q: Why does a cell build internal membranes at all, when it already has a plasma membrane?
A: Two reasons that work together. Membranes provide surface area for the many biochemical processes that happen in or on them, such as lipid metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation and photosynthesis, the last two of which need a membrane to couple H⁺ transport to ATP synthesis. More importantly, an internal membrane encloses a separate aqueous space in which a chosen subset of proteins, reactants and ions can be concentrated to optimize the reactions they take part in. With several kinds of compartment in one cell, reactions that demand very different conditions, or that would compete with each other, can run at the same time without interfering.
Tags: core, organelles

---

Q+: What does an organelle membrane therefore have to contain, beyond lipid?
A: Membrane transport proteins, because the lipid bilayer is impermeable to most hydrophilic molecules, so every metabolite the compartment needs to take in or send out requires a specific transporter. It also needs a mechanism for importing the proteins that make the organelle what it is, and incorporating them into it.

Q: Go through the major organelles and say what each one is for.
A:
| Compartment | What happens there |
|---|---|
| Nucleus | Holds the genome apart from mitochondrial and chloroplast DNA; principal site of DNA and RNA synthesis |
| Cytosol | A little over half the cell's volume; main site of protein synthesis and degradation, and most intermediary metabolism |
| Endoplasmic reticulum | About half the cell's total membrane area; assembles most soluble and integral membrane proteins bound for the exterior or other organelles, makes most of the cell's lipid and sterol, stores Ca²⁺ |
| Golgi apparatus | Stacks of disc-like cisternae that receive lipids and proteins from the ER and dispatch them onward, usually modifying them covalently en route |
| Lysosome | Digestive enzymes that degrade defunct organelles and material taken in by endocytosis |
| Endosome | The series of compartments endocytosed material passes through on the way to lysosomes |
| Mitochondrion and plastid | Generate most of the cell's ATP; plastids also store food or pigment |
| Peroxisome | Small vesicular compartments holding enzymes for various oxidative reactions |

Q: What fraction of a cell's volume do the membrane-enclosed compartments occupy, and how much membrane does that take?
A: On average they take up nearly half the cell's volume, and a very large amount of membrane goes into them. In a liver cell the ER alone has a total membrane surface area 25 times that of the plasma membrane; in a pancreatic exocrine cell it is 12 times. In area and mass the plasma membrane is a minor membrane in most eukaryotic cells, which is the opposite of the impression a textbook diagram gives.
Tags: core, organelles

Q+: Roughly how is the volume divided up in a liver cell (hepatocyte)?
![Electron micrograph of part of a liver cell in cross section with the major intracellular organelles labelled](assets/img/fig-12-02.png)
A: The cytosol takes a little more than half, at about 54%. Mitochondria are the next largest at about 22%, then the ER at about 12% (rough and smooth together, plus a few percent of Golgi), the nucleus at about 6%, and the small compartments (peroxisomes, lysosomes, endosomes) at roughly 1% each. The point of the table is that a compartment can be functionally central and still be a very small share of the cell's volume.

Q: The same organelles are present in every cell type, so why do cells still look so different from each other under the microscope?
A: Because each organelle performs the same set of basic functions everywhere, but its abundance and its additional properties vary with what the cell does. Plasma cells, which secrete their own weight in antibody every day, contain vastly amplified rough ER in large flat sheets. Cardiac muscle cells instead expand their smooth ER for Ca²⁺ storage and proliferate their mitochondria. Organelles also sit in characteristic positions: the Golgi close to the nucleus, the ER tubule network extending from the nucleus through the whole cytosol, distributions that depend on interactions with the cytoskeleton.

Q: The ancestor of eukaryotes is thought to have been a cell with a plasma membrane and no internal membranes. Trace how the internal membrane systems could have arisen from that.
![Evolutionary origin of the internal membrane systems, from an archaeon with plasma membrane protrusions through enclosure of a bacterial symbiont to a cell with nucleus, internal compartments and an endosymbiont](assets/img/fig-12-03.png)
A: Such a cell had a volume perhaps 1000 to 10,000 times smaller than a present-day eukaryotic cell, and to get bigger it had to keep up its surface-to-volume ratio, since the plasma membrane was doing every membrane-dependent job at once: ion pumping, ATP synthesis, protein secretion, lipid synthesis. Judging by modern archaea, the extra area probably came first as plasma membrane protrusions and blebs. Those protrusions made metabolite exchange with the surroundings easier, which favoured symbiotic relationships, and the extra resources from symbiosis plus the extra membrane allowed the cell to grow larger still. Eventually neighbouring protrusions fused with each other, sealing off the network of spaces between them from the outside world. The highly curved membrane at the necks of the protrusions may have been stabilized by proteins that later became part of the nuclear pore.
Tags: organelles, genome-evolution

---

Q+: That single fusion event has three consequences, and between them they account for most of what makes a eukaryotic cell a eukaryotic cell. What are they?
A:
1. **An internal membrane system derived from the old plasma membrane.** The spaces it encloses are topologically equivalent to each other and to the outside of the cell, so a molecule can travel between them without ever crossing a membrane. This holds for the ER, Golgi, endosomes, lysosomes and peroxisomes.
2. **A nucleus.** The old plasma membrane around the genome becomes the inner nuclear membrane, which is why it is continuous with the outer nuclear membrane and the rest of the internal system, with nuclear pore complexes at the points where inner and outer connect. Segregating the genome gave it protection, and separating transcription from translation opened up modes of gene regulation distinctive to eukaryotes.
3. **Endosymbionts.** Symbionts that had been outside are now trapped inside, and later escaped their enclosing membrane into the cytosol to become mitochondria and plastids with genomes of their own.
Tags: organelles, genome-evolution

Q: What does it mean for two compartments to be **topologically equivalent**, and which of the cell's spaces are equivalent to the outside of the cell?
![Topologically equivalent spaces shown in red across the secretory and endocytic pathways, and the cycles of vesicle budding and fusion that connect them](assets/img/fig-12-04.png)
A: That molecules can get from one to the other without having to cross a membrane. The lumens of the ER, Golgi apparatus, endosomes, lysosomes, peroxisomes and transport vesicles are all topologically equivalent to each other and to the extracellular space, which is why a protein that enters the ER lumen can end up secreted from the cell without ever being translocated again. In principle cycles of budding and fusion let any of these lumens communicate with any other and with the cell exterior. Mitochondria and plastids are the exception: they take no part in this vesicular traffic and are isolated from it.
Tags: core, organelles

Q+: A newly made protein has been translocated into the ER lumen and has never crossed another membrane since. Which of these could it now be, without any further translocation?
- [x] A digestive enzyme released into the gut
- [x] A soluble protein of the lysosomal interior
- [ ] A protein of the mitochondrial matrix
- [ ] A cytosolic enzyme of glycolysis
A: The first two. Everything topologically equivalent to the ER lumen is reachable by budding and fusion alone: the Golgi lumen, endosomes, lysosomes, secretory vesicles and, through exocytosis, the outside of the cell. Getting to the mitochondrial matrix means crossing two more membranes through translocators, and mitochondria receive no vesicles at all. Returning to the cytosol also means crossing a membrane, which happens only in the special case of retrotranslocation for degradation.

---

Q+: The nucleus and the cytosol are also topologically equivalent to each other. Why, given that they are separated by two membranes?
A: Because of where the nucleus came from. In the inside-out model, what is now the nucleus was the cytosol of the ancient archaeon, and what is now the cytosol was built from the spaces between its protrusions. The nuclear pore complexes provide a continuous aqueous conduit between the two, so no membrane is crossed in passing between them, which is also why the two can intermix freely during mitosis when the envelope breaks down. Note that this makes nucleus-and-cytosol equivalent to each other but *not* to the ER lumen, even though the ER lumen is continuous with the space between the two nuclear membranes.
Tags: core, organelles

---

Q: What is the strongest line of evidence for these evolutionary schemes, and what does it let you do?
A: The striking similarity of the protein transport machinery between modern prokaryotes and eukaryotic organelles. Moving proteins across and into membranes is fundamental and essential in all living things, so the machinery for it must have arisen in the earliest life-forms and been retained ever since. That makes it a marker you can read: the presence and orientation of transport components lets you trace the origin and the topology of the membrane they now sit in. The components that import proteins into the ER are homologous to those that export proteins across the archaeal plasma membrane, and the membrane protein insertion machinery in the outer and inner membranes of mitochondria and plastids has homologues in the outer and inner membranes of modern bacteria.
Tags: organelles, genome-evolution

Q: The chapter sorts every major compartment into three families. Which are they, and what is the criterion?
A: The criterion is how a protein gets in, which follows from where the compartment came from.
1. **The nucleus and the cytosol**, topologically equivalent although functionally distinct, connected by nuclear pore complexes.
2. **The organelles of the secretory and endocytic pathways**, meaning the ER, Golgi apparatus, endosomes, lysosomes and the transport vesicles between them, plus peroxisomes.
3. **The endosymbiont-derived organelles**, mitochondria and, in plants, the plastids.
Tags: core, organelles

Q: A membrane is not the only way to keep a set of macromolecules together. What is the alternative, and what holds it together?
![Scaffold macromolecules forming a biomolecular condensate through weak multivalent interactions, with client macromolecules recruited into it](assets/img/fig-12-05.png)
A: A **biomolecular condensate**. One or more interacting proteins or nucleic acids act as scaffolds, creating the condensate through multiple weak, fluctuating binding interactions among themselves. They then recruit other specific proteins and nucleic acids as **clients**. A client stays inside not because anything blocks its exit but because the local concentration of binding sites on the scaffold is so high: when it lets go of one site, it is more likely to re-bind another site nearby than to diffuse out of the condensate altogether. Condensate formation does not depend on the clients; they are there because of their specific interactions with the scaffold.
Tags: condensates, core

---

Q: What is the largest and most conspicuous condensate in a eukaryotic cell, and what is scaffolding it?
A: The **nucleolus**, the structure inside the nucleus where ribosomes are assembled. Its central scaffolding component is nascent pre-rRNA being actively transcribed from arrays of rRNA genes. That nascent pre-rRNA recruits the many proteins and small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs) needed to process it, and those in turn recruit further scaffold proteins plus clients including ribosomal proteins, assembly chaperones and modification enzymes. In total more than 400 proteins and RNAs contribute to it.
Tags: condensates

Q+: How big is the range of condensate sizes, and how many kinds does a eukaryotic cell have?
![Four biomolecular condensates: P-granules in a C. elegans one-cell embryo, pericentriolar material in a centrosome, GFP-labelled photobodies in a plant nucleus, and carboxysomes in a cyanobacterium](assets/img/fig-12-06.png)
A: From about 50 nm in diameter, slightly bigger than a ribosome, up to a micrometer or more for a nucleolus. A eukaryotic cell contains a dozen or more different types. They include many kinds of ribonucleoprotein condensate in both nucleus and cytoplasm, signaling protein clusters that form tethered to the plasma membrane, and the "biochemical factories" that assemble as needed for DNA repair, DNA replication and transcription in the nucleus. They are not a eukaryotic invention: carboxysomes in cyanobacteria are condensates too.

Q: Condensates are sometimes called "membraneless organelles". Why is the chapter uneasy about that name?
A: Because "organelle" is a historical term, not a functional one. Organelles were originally intracellular structures that could be seen directly in the light or electron microscope, which is why the nucleolus and the centrosome got the name. Most condensates are too small or too indistinct to qualify on that definition, so calling them organelles quietly redefines the word. They are cellular structures that segregate and concentrate specific macromolecules, which is the thing worth saying about them.
Tags: condensates

---

Q: What is the minimum a macromolecule needs in order to drive condensate formation?
A: A set of weak, **multivalent** interactions with itself or with other constituents, with the interaction sites typically separated by flexible, unstructured regions. Nascent pre-rRNA in the nucleolus is a flexible molecule binding clients and scaffolds at many points along its length; the scaffolding proteins of signaling condensates under the plasma membrane carry several protein-protein interaction domains separated by flexible intrinsically disordered regions with no secondary structure. Experiments with artificial multivalent proteins showed this is the minimal element needed, both in a test tube and in a cell.
Tags: condensates

---

Q+: Why does a condensate behave as a **liquid**, and why does it not simply dissolve into the cytosol around it?
A: Each individual interaction is very weak, so the macromolecules can swap their relative positions rapidly. Those constant rearrangements, plus the structural flexibility of many of the constituents, mean the molecules inside are highly mobile and hold no fixed positions relative to each other, which is what a liquid is. It does not dissolve away because the interaction energy inside the condensate offsets the entropy that would be gained by dispersing. The result is a liquid stably resident inside another liquid, which is what "liquid-liquid phase separation" names.
Tags: condensates, thermodynamics

---

Q+: Which chemical interactions actually do the work, and what is the requirement they have to satisfy?
A: Cation-pi interactions, pi-pi interactions, charge-charge interactions, short regions of crossed β sheets, and short stretches of nucleic acid base-pairing can all contribute. None of them is special; what matters is the balance. The interactions need enough binding energy to offset the entropy lost on association, while staying dynamic enough to keep the condensate liquid. When both hold, the condensate is spherical, deforms and flows under shear, and can undergo fusion and fission.
Tags: condensates

---

Q+: What happens if the interactions become less dynamic over time?
A: The condensate's physical properties shift along a continuum, from liquid to gel and eventually to a solid in which the binding interactions stay fixed. Different condensates sit at different points on that spectrum rather than falling into two clean categories.
Tags: condensates

Q: How can a cell get a condensate inside a condensate, and what is the standard example?
![Purified fibrillarin and nucleophosmin each forming their own RNA-protein condensate separately, and forming one condensate encased inside the other when mixed](assets/img/fig-12-07.png)
A: If a subset of the macromolecules in a condensate has slightly higher affinity for each other than for the rest of the condensate's contents, that subset forms a new condensate of its own with distinct physical properties, nested inside the first. This is thought to be how the nucleolus is segregated into its morphologically different concentric shells, each enriched for the nucleolar proteins dedicated to a different stage of ribosome assembly. The experiment that demonstrated it took two nucleolar scaffolds, fibrillarin from the fibrillar component and nucleophosmin from the granular component, both of which bind RNA. Each on its own with RNA formed an ordinary condensate; mixed together they formed a multilayered structure with one type of condensate encased inside the other.
Tags: condensates

---

Q: A condensate excludes other macromolecules. Why does that not also stop it working as a biochemical factory?
A: Because the exclusion is size- and interaction-selective rather than a physical seal. Nucleotides, metabolites, cofactors and other small molecules diffuse into the condensate rapidly and meet the enzymes that live there. Since the enzymes for successive steps are co-resident, the product of one can be picked up by the next before it diffuses away, so a multistep reaction runs faster than it could with the enzymes dispersed.
Tags: condensates

Q: What is the **pyrenoid**, and why does an alga need one when a land plant does not?
![The pyrenoid of Chlamydomonas by light and electron microscopy, the Rubisco and EPYC1 condensate, thylakoid tubules supplying CO2 from bicarbonate, and a photobleaching experiment showing recovery within 90 seconds](assets/img/fig-12-08.png)
A: A structure found in many algae containing a condensate enriched in Rubisco (ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase) together with a pyrenoid-specific multivalent scaffolding protein, EPYC1. The condensate is interwoven with thylakoid membrane tubules whose carbonic anhydrase converts bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) into CO₂ right where Rubisco is. Rubisco's problem is that O₂ competes with CO₂ for its active site, and at the low free CO₂ concentration available to a cell in water the competing oxygenation reaction (photorespiration) would win. Locally generated CO₂ tips the balance back toward carboxylation. Land plants do not need the arrangement because CO₂ in air is far more plentiful than the dissolved CO₂ available to an alga.
Tags: condensates

Q+: How was it shown that the contents of the pyrenoid matrix really are dynamic rather than a fixed lattice?
A: By photobleaching. A pyrenoid containing a Rubisco subunit tagged with a fluorescent protein was imaged, half of it was bleached, and recovery was followed. Fluorescent molecules from the unbleached half intermixed with the bleached ones within 90 seconds, which a solid assembly could not do.
Tags: condensates

Q: Engineers have built artificial condensates. What did the stop-codon experiment show?
A: That the multivalent interaction module is genuinely the whole trick. All the factors needed to misread a UAG stop codon as a sense codon were engineered with artificial multivalent interaction domains so that they would form a condensate inside the cell. Within that condensate, UAG on the resident mRNA was efficiently read as a sense codon, while other mRNAs out in the surrounding cytosol terminated at UAG normally. So a condensate-based biochemical factory with a chosen chemistry can be designed rationally, and the concentration effect is strong enough to redirect an outcome as basic as translation termination.
Tags: condensates

Q: A condensate depends on weak interactions just barely outcompeting the entropy of mixing. What does the cell get out of that marginal arrangement?
![A receptor phosphorylated on ligand binding recruiting multivalent proteins into a signaling condensate, which disassembles on dephosphorylation](assets/img/fig-12-09.png)
A: Control. Because the balance is fine, a small change in interaction strength is enough to make or unmake the condensate, so the cell can switch one on and off quickly. The usual switch is a post-translational modification, above all phosphorylation: a ligand-engaged receptor has its cytosolic tail and associated proteins phosphorylated, the phosphorylated sites plus their surrounding residues form binding sites for multivalent cytosolic and membrane proteins, and those proteins interact to build a signaling condensate. Dephosphorylation disassembles it and signaling stops. Changes in temperature, pH or osmolarity can also drive formation and dissolution.
Tags: condensates

---

Q+: A signaling condensate has assembled under the plasma membrane after a receptor was engaged by its ligand. Which single intervention would be most likely to disperse it?
- [x] Activating a phosphatase that acts on the receptor's cytosolic tail
- [ ] Adding more of the multivalent cytosolic proteins
- [ ] Deleting one of several client proteins inside it
- [ ] Raising the concentration of ATP in the cytosol
A: The phosphatase. The phosphorylated sites on the tail, together with their surrounding residues, are what form the binding sites that recruit the multivalent proteins, so removing the phosphates removes the scaffold and the condensate disassembles. Adding more multivalent protein would favour assembly, not dispersal. Deleting a client changes nothing structural, since formation does not depend on clients. And nothing here is driven by ATP hydrolysis: a condensate is held together by weak noncovalent interactions, not by an energy-consuming machine.

Q: What are **stress granules**, and what is the sequence of events that nucleates one?
A: Condensates that form only during certain kinds of cellular stress and dissolve when the stress is relieved. They are enriched in translationally inactive mRNAs, various translation factors, ribosomal subunits and RNA-binding proteins. They form when a block in translation initiation leaves mRNA regions exposed that would normally be covered by translating ribosomes; the exposed mRNAs interact with each other and with RNA-binding proteins, and that nucleates the condensate. The point of sequestering rather than degrading these components is that the cell does not then have to make them all again from scratch once the stress passes.
Tags: condensates

---

Q: A protein has finished being made on a cytosolic ribosome. What decides where it goes, and what happens to a protein that has no instructions?
A: Its amino acid sequence, which can contain one or more **sorting signals** directing it to a particular part of the cell. Each signal is recognized by a complementary **sorting receptor** that mediates the move. A protein with no sorting signal at all is not lost or degraded for want of an address: it stays in the cytosol as a permanent resident, which is the default outcome. Only a few proteins, those made inside mitochondria and plastids, start anywhere other than a cytosolic ribosome.
Tags: sorting, core

Q: There are four fundamentally different ways a protein moves from one compartment to another. Name them and say what is distinctive about each.
![A road map of protein traffic within a eukaryotic cell, with protein translocation in blue, gated transport in red, vesicular transport in green and engulfment in grey](assets/img/fig-12-10.png)
A:
1. **Protein translocation.** A transmembrane protein translocator carries specific proteins from the cytosol into a topologically distinct space: across a membrane, or into the bilayer itself for an integral membrane protein. The protein usually has to unfold to snake through the translocator. This is how proteins first enter the ER lumen, the ER membrane, or mitochondria.
2. **Gated transport.** Proteins and RNAs move between cytosol and nucleus through nuclear pore complexes, which act as selective gates supporting active transport of specific macromolecules and whole assemblies between two spaces that are already topologically equivalent.
3. **Vesicular transport.** Membrane-enclosed intermediates, small spherical vesicles, elongated tubules or larger irregular organelle fragments, ferry proteins between topologically equivalent compartments. The cargo never crosses a membrane, so topological relationships are preserved.
4. **Engulfment.** Double-membrane sheets wrap around a portion of cytoplasm, sometimes including whole organelles, and seal by membrane fusion into a new compartment.
Tags: sorting, core

Q+: Give the example of engulfment from each end of the chapter.
![A double-membrane sheet wrapping around cytoplasmic contents and sealing to form a new compartment](assets/img/fig-12-12.png)
A: **Autophagy**, where the wrapping seals into an autophagosome that is delivered to the lysosome, and **re-formation of the nuclear envelope after mitosis**, where ER tubes and sheets wrap around the decondensing chromosomes and then fuse laterally with one another to form a sealed double-membrane envelope traversed only by the nuclear pores. The second is conceptually the same process even though nothing is being degraded.
Tags: sorting

Q: In vesicular transport, what is transferred besides the soluble cargo, and why does the orientation of everything survive the trip?
![A transport vesicle budding from a donor compartment and fusing with a target compartment, carrying soluble contents and membrane](assets/img/fig-12-11.png)
A: Membrane is transferred too, and with it the membrane proteins and lipids. Because the vesicle buds outward from the donor and fuses into the target without ever turning inside out, the original orientation is preserved: a membrane protein domain facing the cytosol in the donor still faces the cytosol in the target. This is the structural reason vesicular transport cannot change a protein's topology, only its location.
Tags: sorting

---

Q: Entering a condensate needs no translocator and no vesicle. What is the mechanism, and what is it analogous to?
A: Direct physical binding. The macromolecule binds specifically to a protein or RNA that is already part of the condensate, and stays there because those interactions are persistent and repeated. The chapter makes the analogy explicit: the interaction between a macromolecule and its binding partner in the condensate plays the same role as the interaction between a sorting signal and its cognate sorting receptor. In both cases it is a specific molecular recognition event that specifies the destination; the difference is only that no membrane has to be crossed.
Tags: condensates, sorting

---

Q: Sorting signals come in two general varieties. What are they, and which processes use which?
A: A **signal sequence**, a linear stretch of amino acids, or a **signal patch**, a specific three-dimensional arrangement of amino acids brought together by folding. Sorting signals for protein translocation into organelles are always linear signal sequences. Both linear signals and signal patches are known for nuclear and vesicular transport.
Tags: sorting, core

---

Q+: Where do linear signal sequences for translocation usually sit, and what happens to them afterwards?
A: Often at the N-terminus of the chain, which is the end that emerges from the ribosome first, so the address is read before most of the protein even exists. N-terminal signal sequences are usually cut off the finished protein by specialized **signal peptidases** once sorting is complete. Other kinds of signal sequence are not removed and stay part of the mature protein.
Tags: sorting

Q: Learn the signal sequences: what chemical character does each destination's signal have?
![Examples of signal sequences directing proteins to the nucleus, out of the nucleus, to mitochondria, to plastids, to peroxisomes, to the ER and back to the ER](assets/img/fig-12-13.png)
A:
| Destination | Character of the signal |
|---|---|
| Import into the ER | About 5 to 10 predominantly hydrophobic amino acids, usually N-terminal |
| Return to the ER | Four amino acids at the C-terminus, KDEL (-Lys-Asp-Glu-Leu) |
| Import into mitochondria | Positively charged amino acids alternating with hydrophobic ones, N-terminal, and able to form an amphipathic helix |
| Import into plastids | N-terminal, rich in serine and threonine, few charged residues |
| Import into the nucleus | Primarily positively charged amino acids, for example -Pro-Pro-Lys-Lys-Lys-Arg-Lys-Val- |
| Export from the nucleus | Hydrophobic, leucine-rich |
| Import into peroxisomes | Three characteristic amino acids at the C-terminus, -Ser-Lys-Leu |
The requirement is only that a signal be distinctive enough from all the others for its own receptor to pick it out.

Q+: You find an uncharacterized protein whose N-terminal 25 residues are rich in serine and threonine with almost no charged amino acids, and whose C-terminus ends -Ser-Lys-Leu. In a plant cell, where will it go?
- [x] To plastids, because the N-terminal signal is read first and translocation into the ER never begins
- [ ] To peroxisomes, because a C-terminal signal is recognized after synthesis is finished
- [ ] To both, splitting between the two compartments
- [ ] To the cytosol, because the two signals conflict and cancel
A: To plastids. The serine- and threonine-rich, uncharged N-terminal stretch is a plastid signal sequence, and it is at the N-terminus, so it emerges from the ribosome first and is engaged first. Once the protein is inside the plastid, its C-terminal -Ser-Lys-Leu is sealed away from the cytosolic Pex5 that would have recognized it, so the peroxisomal signal is never read. Order of presentation is what settles a conflict between two otherwise valid addresses, which is the same logic that makes a bipartite chloroplast signal work in sequence rather than in competition.

Q: What experiments showed that signal sequences are both necessary and sufficient for targeting?
A: Transferring the peptide from one protein to another by genetic engineering. Putting an N-terminal ER signal sequence at the beginning of a cytosolic protein redirects that protein to the ER, which shows sufficiency. Removing or mutating the signal sequence of an ER protein leaves it stranded in the cytosol, which shows necessity. Nothing else about the protein has to change for either result.
Tags: sorting, core

---

Q+: Signal sequences for the same destination can differ greatly in sequence and still work interchangeably. What does that tell you about how they are recognized?
A: That the receptor is reading a physical property rather than a specific string. Hydrophobicity, in the ER case, matters more in recognition than the exact identity of the residues. This is a different mode of recognition from, say, an enzyme reading a substrate, and it is why signal sequences are hard to find by sequence alignment alone.
Tags: sorting

Q: Sorting receptors are described as a public transportation system. What two features of their behaviour does the analogy capture?
A: That they act **catalytically**, delivering a cargo and then returning to their point of origin to be reused rather than being consumed, and that most of them recognize **classes** of protein rather than one individual species, so a single receptor carries many different cargoes to the same destination. Neither the vehicle nor the route is dedicated to one passenger.
Tags: sorting

---

Q: Why can a cell not make an ER from scratch, and which organelles can be built de novo?
A: Because growing the ER, mitochondria or plastids requires delivering new proteins through an organelle-specific protein translocator, and installing a new translocator itself requires a preexisting translocator to put it there. So a cell must already have some functional ER to make more ER, and the same holds for mitochondria and plastids. Two kinds of information are therefore needed to build these organelles and both are passed from parent to daughter: the DNA specifying the organelle's proteins, and the preexisting translocator in the organellar membrane. Lysosomes, which acquire all their proteins and membrane by vesicular transport from other organelles, could in principle be constructed de novo and do not have to be inherited. Condensates certainly can be: a nucleolus is disassembled at cell division, its constituents distributed stochastically between the daughters, and reassembled afterwards.
Tags: organelles, core

Q: How much of a cell's membrane is ER, and what shape is it?
![Fluorescence micrographs of the ER: a network of tubules and sheets through the cytosol of an animal cell, and an ER network in a living plant cell](assets/img/fig-12-14.png)
A: Typically more than half the total membrane of an average animal cell. It is a netlike labyrinth of branching tubules and flattened sacs extending throughout the cytosol, often occupying more than 10% of the cell's volume. The tubules and sacs interconnect, and the ER membrane is continuous with the outer nuclear membrane. The whole thing encloses **one** internal space, the ER lumen, which is continuous with the space between the inner and outer nuclear membranes. Because the network is so extensive, every region of the cytosol is close to some part of the ER membrane.
Tags: er, organelles

---

Q: What does the ER do, listed properly?
A: It is where most transmembrane proteins and lipids of the cell's organelles are made, covering the ER itself, the Golgi, lysosomes, endosomes, secretory vesicles, peroxisomes and the plasma membrane. It makes most of the lipids for mitochondrial and plastid membranes too, even though those organelles receive no vesicles. Almost all proteins that will be secreted, plus all those destined for the lumen of the ER, Golgi or lysosomes, are delivered into the ER lumen first. And the ER lumen stores the intracellular Ca²⁺ that is mobilized in many signaling responses.

Q: What is the difference between **rough** and **smooth** ER, and how much does the ratio vary between cells?
![Electron micrographs of rough ER packed with ribosomes in a pancreatic exocrine cell, abundant smooth ER in a testosterone-secreting Leydig cell, and a three-dimensional reconstruction of both in a liver cell](assets/img/fig-12-15.png)
A: The rough appearance comes from the ribosomes studding the surface, engaged in synthesizing proteins that are being translocated as they are made. Smooth ER lacks ribosomes and handles other ER jobs, above all lipid biosynthesis and metabolism. All cells have both, but the proportions vary enormously with what the cell does: rough ER makes up 60% of the membranes of a pancreatic exocrine cell, which secretes its own weight in digestive enzymes every day, while a testosterone-secreting Leydig cell is dominated by smooth ER instead. In a liver cell the rough ER forms oriented stacks of flattened cisternae with a lumen 20 to 30 nm wide, connected to a fine network of smooth tubules 30 to 60 nm across.

---

Q+: That correlation was historically important. What did it first suggest?
A: That the ER is responsible for the synthesis and assembly of secreted proteins. Biologists noticed that the cells specialized for secretion were exactly the cells stuffed with rough ER, and that was the first clue pointing at the compartment before any of the mechanism was known.

Q: Smooth ER is more functionally varied than rough ER. Name its specializations.
A:
- **Transitional ER**, present in all cells, the region from which transport vesicles carrying newly made proteins and lipids bud off for the Golgi.
- **Steroid hormone synthesis**, in cells that hold the enzymes making cholesterol and modifying it into steroid hormones.
- **Lipoprotein particle production** in hepatocytes, whose smooth ER membranes are enriched in the enzymes that make the lipid components carried through the bloodstream.
- **Detoxification**, also in hepatocytes, above all by the cytochrome P450 family.
- **Ca²⁺ storage**, taken to an extreme in the **sarcoplasmic reticulum** of muscle cells.
- **Organelle contact sites** with mitochondria, plastids, endosomes and the plasma membrane.

---

Q+: What do the cytochrome P450 enzymes actually do to a drug?
A: They catalyze a series of reactions that make a water-insoluble drug or metabolite sufficiently water-soluble to leave the cell and be excreted in the urine or bile. The problem being solved is that such compounds would otherwise accumulate to toxic levels in cell membranes, which is where anything that insoluble ends up.
Tags: er

---

Q+: How does the ER hold on to Ca²⁺, and what is it used for?
A: A Ca²⁺ pump transports Ca²⁺ from the cytosol into the ER lumen, and a high concentration of Ca²⁺-binding proteins inside facilitates storage. Release into the cytosol and subsequent reuptake underlie many rapid responses to extracellular signals. In muscle, the release and reuptake by the sarcoplasmic reticulum trigger myofibril contraction and relaxation on each cycle of contraction.
Tags: er

Q: What is an **organelle contact site**, and what is it for?
![Electron micrographs of ER closely apposed to a mitochondrion in a mouse fibroblast, and ER juxtaposed with the plasma membrane in a yeast cell](assets/img/fig-12-16.png)
A: A region where two organelle membranes are held very close to each other, around 10 to 30 nm apart, by specific junction complexes, and where the apposed membranes are enriched for proteins involved in communication or transport of key metabolites. The ER makes them with mitochondria, plastids, endosomes and the plasma membrane. Transport of lipids from their site of synthesis in the ER to the mitochondrion is thought to happen at ER-mitochondria contact sites, which matters because mitochondria receive no vesicles. Contact with the plasma membrane modulates the level of plasma membrane phosphoinositides, the lipids feeding numerous signaling pathways.

Q: The ER is interleaved with everything else in the cytoplasm, which ought to make isolating it hopeless. How is it done?
![Isolation of rough and smooth microsomes: homogenization, then equilibrium sedimentation through a sucrose gradient, with electron micrographs of the two fractions](assets/img/fig-12-17.png)
A: By exploiting what happens when you break the cell. Homogenization shatters the ER into fragments that reseal into small closed vesicles about 100 to 200 nm across, called **microsomes**. Rough microsomes, from rough ER, keep their ribosomes on the outside and enclose a piece of ER lumen; the ribosomes make them denser. Smooth microsomes lack ribosomes and come from smooth ER but also from plasma membrane, Golgi, endosomes and mitochondria. The density difference lets equilibrium density centrifugation through a sucrose gradient separate them: rough microsomes sediment to a high sucrose concentration, smooth ones stop at low. Smooth microsomes from different organelles can be separated further on differences in protein content.

---

Q+: Why is a microsome such a useful thing to have in a test tube?
A: Because it is a small authentic version of the ER rather than a broken piece of one. It is still capable of protein translocation, protein glycosylation, Ca²⁺ uptake and release, and lipid synthesis. That made microsomes invaluable in working out the molecular details of ER function, since the process could be reconstituted and manipulated outside the cell.

Q: Which proteins does the ER capture from the cytosol, and what do they have in common?
A: Two types: transmembrane proteins, which become embedded in the ER membrane, and water-soluble proteins, which are fully translocated across the membrane into the lumen. Some stay and work in the ER, but many are bound for another organelle, for the plasma membrane, or for secretion. Whatever their eventual fate, all of them are directed to the ER membrane in the first place by an **ER signal sequence**. Capture happens while they are still being synthesized.

Q: Describe the experiment that produced the **signal hypothesis**, and say what each control established.
![Cell-free translation of an mRNA for a secreted protein, with and without rough microsomes, showing signal cleavage and delivery into the microsome lumen](assets/img/fig-12-18.png)
A: mRNA encoding a secreted protein was added to cytosol extracted from cells. In that cell-free reaction the cytosolic ribosomes translated it into a protein slightly **larger** than the normal secreted protein. Repeating the reaction with microsomes derived from rough ER produced a protein of the correct size, and it was located **inside** the microsomes. The control was mRNA encoding a cytosolic protein, which gave the correctly sized product whether microsomes were present or not, showing that the extra piece is a feature of secreted proteins and not an artifact of the cell-free system. The extra polypeptide was at the N-terminus and was removed only when microsomes were there.
Tags: er, sorting, core

---

Q+: What did the signal hypothesis propose to explain this?
A: That the mRNA for a secretory protein codes for a protein bigger than the one eventually secreted, and that the extra polypeptide is a signal sequence directing the protein to the ER membrane. When the signal sequence emerges from the ribosome it directs the ribosome to a translocator that forms a pore in the ER membrane, through which the polypeptide is translocated. A signal peptidase closely associated with the translocator clips the signal sequence off during translation, so the mature protein is released into the ER lumen as soon as synthesis finishes.
Tags: er, sorting, core

---

Q+: The translocator is a pore through a membrane whose whole job is to be a barrier. How is the barrier not compromised?
A: The translocator stays closed until a ribosome has bound, so the permeability barrier of the ER membrane is maintained at all times. The pore is only ever open with a ribosome sealed over it and a polypeptide threading through.
Tags: er

---

Q: What made the cell-free system so valuable beyond this one result?
A: It showed that a complex cellular process could be reconstituted by mixing together the requisite components, here mRNA, cytosol and microsomes, and then taken apart again by recombining them differently. That is how the existence of signal sequences on secreted proteins was deduced long before it was possible to sequence their mRNAs directly. The same approach was later used to identify and purify the machinery of ER import, and then extended to protein transport into and out of the nucleus, import into mitochondria and chloroplasts, and vesicular transport.
Tags: methods

Q: What are the two components that guide an ER signal sequence to the ER membrane?
![The signal-recognition particle: its rodlike ribonucleoprotein structure, the hydrophobic signal-sequence-binding pocket, SRP bound to a ribosome by cryo-EM, and the conformational change exposing the SRP receptor site](assets/img/fig-12-19.png)
A: A **signal-recognition particle (SRP)**, which binds the signal sequence, and an **SRP receptor** in the ER membrane. In animal cells SRP is a large complex of six different polypeptide chains bound to a single RNA molecule. Bacteria have fewer subunits in both, but homologues of both components are present in all living organisms, so the targeting mechanism arose early and has been conserved.
Tags: er, sorting, core

---

Q: ER signal sequences vary greatly in amino acid sequence, sharing only eight or more nonpolar residues at their centre. How can one binding site recognize all of them?
A: The signal-sequence-binding site is a large hydrophobic pocket enriched in **methionines**. Methionine side chains are unbranched and flexible, so the pocket is plastic enough to accommodate hydrophobic signal sequences of different sizes and shapes. Recognition is of a general physical character, hydrophobicity presented in roughly the right amount, rather than of a defined sequence, which is exactly what is needed for a receptor that must serve thousands of different cargo proteins.
Tags: er, sorting

---

Q: SRP is a hinged rod that wraps along the large ribosomal subunit. What does each end of it do?
A: One end carries the signal-sequence-binding pocket and is positioned right next to the ribosomal tunnel from which newly made polypeptides emerge, so it can engage a signal sequence the moment it appears. Once a signal sequence is engaged, the other end binds at the interface between the large and small ribosomal subunits, which is the same site where translation elongation factors bind. A ribosome with SRP on it therefore translates more slowly than normal.
Tags: er, sorting

---

Q+: Why would the cell want to slow translation down at that moment?
A: To buy time. Slower elongation presumably gives the ribosome enough time to reach and bind the ER membrane before the polypeptide is finished, so the protein is not released into the cytosol by accident. This safety device may matter most for secreted and lysosomal hydrolases, which would do real damage loose in the cytosol. Cells that secrete large amounts of hydrolases take a further precaution and keep high concentrations of hydrolase inhibitors in their cytosol.
Tags: er, sorting

---

Q: Trace the SRP cycle from signal sequence to resumed translation.
![The SRP cycle: recognition of the signal sequence and slowing of translation, targeting to the SRP receptor, release of SRP, and translocation beginning](assets/img/fig-12-20.png)
A: The signal sequence emerges and SRP binds it plus the ribosome, slowing translation. Binding of the signal sequence causes a conformational change that exposes SRP's binding site for the **SRP receptor**, a transmembrane protein complex in the rough ER membrane, which in animal cells has two different polypeptide chains. SRP binding to its receptor brings the SRP-ribosome complex to an unoccupied protein translocator. The part of SRP that sat near the ribosomal tunnel moves aside so the translocator can take that position; SRP releases the signal sequence, which inserts into the translocator and initiates transfer of the chain across the bilayer. SRP and SRP receptor dissociate, are recycled, and protein synthesis resumes at full speed with the translocator now tightly bound to the translating ribosome.
Tags: er, sorting, core

---

Q+: Where does the directionality of that cycle come from?
A: From GTP. One of the SRP proteins and both chains of the SRP receptor contain GTP-binding domains, and the conformational changes accompanying cycles of GTP binding and hydrolysis ensure that SRP preferentially binds a signal sequence out in the cytosol and releases it only after successfully engaging the SRP receptor at the ER membrane. The energy of GTP hydrolysis is spent imparting direction to a cycle that would otherwise be reversible, not on moving the cargo.
Tags: er, sorting

Q: Co-translational transfer creates two spatially separate populations of ribosomes. What actually distinguishes them?
![A common pool of ribosomal subunits feeding both a free ribosome cycle and a membrane-bound ribosome cycle, with the mRNA retained on the ER membrane, and an electron micrograph of polyribosomes on the ER](assets/img/fig-12-21.png)
A: Nothing about the ribosomes. **Membrane-bound ribosomes** are attached to the cytosolic side of the ER membrane and are synthesizing proteins that are being translocated at the same time; **free ribosomes** are unattached and make everything else encoded by the nuclear genome. They are structurally and functionally identical and differ only in which protein they happen to be making at that moment. A ribosome is not a member of one class or the other; it moves between them.

---

Q+: If individual ribosomes come and go, what keeps a polyribosome making a secreted protein at the ER?
A: Multiplicity, and then the mRNA itself. Many ribosomes engage a single mRNA to form a polyribosome, and if that mRNA encodes a protein with an ER signal sequence the whole polyribosome becomes attached to the ER membrane, held there by the signal sequences on several growing chains at once. Individual ribosomes return to the cytosol and rejoin the free pool when they finish, but the mRNA stays attached to the ER membrane, so there is always another ribosome on it holding the complex in place.

Q+: Which components are shared between the free and the membrane-bound ribosome cycles? Select all that apply.
- [x] The pool of ribosomal subunits in the cytosol
- [x] The ribosomes themselves, which are identical in both
- [ ] The mRNA molecules, which are permanently assigned to one route
- [ ] The Sec61 translocator, which is used by both
A: The subunit pool and the ribosomes. Everything is drawn from one common pool: subunits are released at the end of each round of synthesis and rejoin it, whatever they were making. The mRNA is the component that *is* committed, because an mRNA encoding an ER signal sequence stays bound to the ER membrane as part of a polyribosome, held by a changing population of ribosomes each transiently attached through the translocator. And a free ribosome never engages a translocator at all, since the protein it is making has no signal sequence to open one.

Q: Does a polypeptide cross the ER membrane through the lipid bilayer itself or through a protein channel, and how was the argument settled?
![Structure of the Sec61 translocator: side and top views showing the pseudosymmetric α subunit, the β and γ subunits, the plug helix and the lateral gate, with models of the closed and open states](assets/img/fig-12-22.png)
A: Through a protein channel. The debate ran for a long time and ended with the identification of the translocator itself, which was shown to form a water-filled channel across the membrane. Its core, the **Sec61 complex**, is built from three subunits highly conserved from bacteria to eukaryotes, and its structure shows 10 α helices surrounding a central channel. The α subunit has an inverted repeat structure, the two smaller β and γ subunits sit alongside, and regions of the α subunit protruding into the cytosol bind the ribosome during translocation.

---

Q+: What is the **plug**, and why does the channel need one?
A: A short α helix that sits in the pore and seals the translocator when it is idle. Keeping it closed matters because the ER is a Ca²⁺ store, and an open channel would let Ca²⁺ and other ions leak out. During translocation the plug moves out of the way so the polypeptide can pass.
Tags: er, translocation

---

Q+: What is the **lateral gate**?
A: A seam in the Sec61 α subunit through which the translocator can open sideways toward the membrane. It is the access route from the central channel into the hydrophobic core of the bilayer, which is what makes the same machine capable of both threading a soluble protein right through and releasing a transmembrane segment into the lipid.

---

Q: How does a signal sequence open the Sec61 translocator, and why is that step described as proofreading?
![Cross sections of the Sec61 translocator before and after a signal sequence inserts into the lateral gate, and a translating ribosome bound to an opened translocator with a polypeptide passing through](assets/img/fig-12-23.png)
A: The signal sequence wedges into the lateral gate with its N-terminus facing the cytosol. That insertion widens the central channel and releases the plug, so a continuous path across the membrane appears, and the open translocator then accommodates the stretch of polypeptide following the signal sequence. It is proofreading because Sec61 opens **only** for proteins carrying a signal sequence: the translocator is not a passive hole that anything can enter, it is a lock the cargo has to fit, which is what ensures only proteins genuinely bound for the ER get in.

---

Q+: What becomes of the signal sequence afterwards?
A: Being hydrophobic, it exits laterally through the gate into the membrane. There signal peptidase cleaves it off, and other proteases in the ER membrane and cytosol rapidly degrade it to amino acids. It leaves by the side door rather than following the rest of the chain into the lumen.
Tags: er, translocation

---

Q: During co-translational translocation the ribosome and the translocator are aligned. What does that arrangement accomplish, and where does the energy come from?
A: The polypeptide tunnel inside the large ribosomal subunit lines up with the channel in the Sec61 translocator, creating a continuous path for the chain from the peptidyl-transferase centre, where each new amino acid is added, all the way to the ER lumen 15 nm away. Because the only route open to the growing chain is across the membrane, no separate motor is needed: the energy already being spent on polypeptide elongation indirectly drives translocation as well. When translation terminates, the C-terminus is released from the ribosome, slips through the translocator, and the plug returns to close the channel.
Tags: er, translocation, core

---

Q+: Import happens before the protein has a chance to fold. What problem does that solve?
A: How to move a large protein across a membrane barrier without leaking much smaller ions and metabolites while you do it. A folded protein needs a wide hole; an unfolded chain threading through a narrow one, with a ribosome sealed over the cytosolic end, keeps the barrier essentially intact throughout.

Q: Some proteins are made completely in the cytosol and imported afterwards. What is that called, and where is it common?
![Co-translational translocation with a ribosome bound to the ER membrane, and post-translational translocation of a completed chain held unfolded by chaperones](assets/img/fig-12-24.png)
A: **Post-translational translocation**. It is more common across the yeast ER membrane and the evolutionarily related bacterial plasma membrane than in animal cells. The same Sec61 translocator is used, called SecY in bacteria, and its channel is narrow, so precursors can only be translocated unfolded. That means a precursor protein must not fold after synthesis: it is held by cytosolic chaperones, typically of the hsp70 family, which prevent folding or aggregation and then dissociate as the chain is threaded through. The signal sequence still engages Sec61 and opens the channel in the same way.

Q: Three ways of driving a polypeptide through a structurally similar translocator. Work through each.
![Co-translational translocation, post-translational translocation pulled by BiP via the Sec62-Sec63 complex, and bacterial post-translational translocation pushed by the SecA ATPase](assets/img/fig-12-25.png)
A:
- **(A) Co-translational.** SRP and SRP receptor bring the ribosome to the membrane and it engages Sec61; the chain is threaded across as it is made. No additional energy is needed, because the only path available to the growing chain is across the membrane.
- **(B) Post-translational in eukaryotes.** A complex of **Sec62** and **Sec63** attached to Sec61 positions **BiP** molecules where they can grab the chain as it emerges into the ER lumen. ATP-driven cycles of BiP binding and release pull the protein in.
- **(C) Post-translational in bacteria.** The completed chain is fed into SecY from the cytosolic side by the **SecA** ATPase, whose ATP-driven conformational changes give a pistonlike motion that both pushes several residues through and prevents backsliding.
Sec61, SRP and SRP receptor are found in all organisms; SecA only in bacteria; the Sec62-Sec63 complex only in eukaryotes.

---

Q+: Explain why BiP works as a **ratchet** rather than a pump.
A: BiP has high affinity for unfolded polypeptide chains, like its cytosolic hsp70 cousin, and binds tightly to the imported chain as soon as it emerges into the ER lumen. It does not pull: the chain is diffusing back and forth through the channel anyway, and a bound BiP simply blocks the backward direction, so the next stretch that happens to emerge can be caught by another BiP molecule. ATP hydrolysis by BiP releases the chain so the same molecule can bind again further along. Energy is spent on resetting the pawl, not on the stroke.
Tags: er, translocation, core

---

Q+: Why does a bacterium need SecA where a eukaryotic cell uses BiP?
A: Because of where the protein is going. Bacteria transport proteins directly to the extracellular space, where no energy is available, so they cannot put an ATP-driven chaperone on the far side of the membrane. The driving machinery has to sit in the cytosol instead, which is why SecA pushes from behind rather than pulling from in front.
Tags: er, translocation

Q: Every transmembrane protein of the ER, Golgi, lysosomes, endosomes, secretory vesicles and plasma membrane is inserted at the ER. Why can the same machinery that handles soluble proteins also do this job?
A: Because the transmembrane segments that define a transmembrane protein resemble the hydrophobic ER signal sequences that direct soluble translocation. SRP recognizes a hydrophobic α-helical segment emerging from the ribosome as a signal sequence and brings the ribosome to Sec61 exactly as it would for a secreted protein, and the segment inserts into the lateral gate, which is the same site where signal sequences bind. The extra complexity of membrane protein biosynthesis, with some parts crossing the bilayer, some staying in the cytosol and some integrating into the membrane, is handled with no extra factors.

Q: A single-pass transmembrane protein can insert in either of two orientations. What determines which?
![A transmembrane segment inserting into the Sec61 lateral gate in two orientations, one retaining the N-terminal domain in the cytosol and one translocating it](assets/img/fig-12-26.png)
A: The orientation in which the segment enters the lateral gate, which fixes whether the part preceding or the part following it gets moved into the ER lumen. Two things decide it.
- **Charge.** A net positive charge on the residues flanking the N-terminal side favours keeping the N-terminal domain in the cytosol; a net positive charge on the C-terminal side favours keeping the C-terminal flanking region in the cytosol and translocating the N-terminal one. The length of the transmembrane segment matters too.
- **Folding.** If the preceding N-terminal segment is long and stably folded it cannot go through the narrow Sec61 channel at all, so the still-unfolded C-terminal segment is translocated instead. The positive-inside rule is a preference; a folded domain is a hard constraint.
Tags: er, membrane-proteins, core


---

Q+: A single-pass membrane protein has a transmembrane segment flanked by residues that carry a net positive charge on its **C-terminal** side. Where does the N-terminal region end up?
- [x] In the ER lumen, and therefore outside the cell if the protein reaches the plasma membrane
- [ ] In the cytosol, because positive charges are always retained cytosolically at the N-terminus
- [ ] It depends only on the length of the transmembrane segment
- [ ] Either, with the population splitting evenly between the two orientations
A: In the ER lumen. Positively charged flanking residues tend to stay on the cytosolic side, so putting them after the transmembrane segment holds the C-terminal region in the cytosol and forces the N-terminal region across into the lumen. Since the lumenal face of the ER becomes the exterior face of the plasma membrane, the N-terminal region finishes up outside the cell. Length of the segment does contribute, but it does not override the charge distribution, and the rule fails only when the N-terminal region is long and already folded, since a folded domain cannot pass through the narrow Sec61 channel whatever the charges say.

Q: Many transmembrane proteins have a large N-terminal domain that ends up in the ER lumen. How are they inserted?
![A cleaved N-terminal ER signal sequence initiating translocation, followed by a transmembrane segment that stops transfer and exits laterally into the bilayer](assets/img/fig-12-27.png)
A: With **both** a cleaved ER signal sequence and a transmembrane segment, used in sequence. An N-terminal signal sequence initiates translocation exactly as for a soluble secretory protein, which commits the N-terminus of the mature polypeptide to the ER lumen, and it is cleaved by signal peptidase. The rest of the chain then begins translocating through Sec61. When a hydrophobic segment emerges from the ribosome it inserts into the lateral gate, and because it is more stable in the membrane than in the aqueous channel it exits sideways into the bilayer. Translocation stops there, and the remainder of the protein is synthesized on the cytosolic side.

---

Q: In a multipass transmembrane protein, what fixes the topology of the whole chain?
![Successive hydrophobic segments of a multipass protein inserting into the Sec61 lateral gate in alternating orientations until the protein is woven into the membrane](assets/img/fig-12-28.png)
A: The orientation in which the **first** transmembrane segment inserts. Synthesis up to that point follows the single-pass story, with the orientation dictated by the preceding N-terminal domain and nearby charged amino acids, and once that first segment is in, the topology for everything that follows is locked in. From then on, each successive hydrophobic segment is interpreted by the translocator on the basis of the topology and properties of the parts already inserted: in the simplest case it engages the lateral gate in the orientation **opposite** to the segment most recently inserted. The hydrophilic sequences in between are either synthesized into the cytosol or threaded through Sec61, depending on which way the preceding segment went.

---

Q+: Why does each hydrophobic segment reliably find the lateral gate at all?
A: Because the ribosome and the Sec61 translocator are tightly coupled, so every hydrophobic segment emerges from the ribosomal exit tunnel very close to the gate that gives access to the lipid bilayer. The decision is made locally and immediately, before the segment has any chance to do something else.

---

Q+: Some transmembrane segments of a multipass protein are only partially hydrophobic and would not be stable in the bilayer alone. How do they get in, and why does it matter?
A: They can insert if they interact with one of the preceding transmembrane segments still near the lateral gate, so the two are stabilized together. This cooperation is what makes it possible to have hydrophilic parts buried inside the lipid bilayer, which is essential for transporters and channels, whose whole function depends on a polar pathway through the membrane. A purely hydrophobicity-driven insertion rule could never build one.

---

Q: Membrane proteins are always inserted from the cytosolic side of the ER in this programmed way. What follows from that?
A: Every copy of a given polypeptide ends up with the same orientation in the bilayer, so the ER membrane is asymmetrical: the protein domains exposed on one face differ from those on the other. That asymmetry is then preserved through all the budding and fusion events that carry the protein onward, because vesicular transport never flips a membrane. So the way a protein is first inserted into the ER membrane determines its orientation in every other membrane it will ever reach.

Q: A **tail-anchored protein** is held in the membrane by a single α helix very close to its C-terminus. Why can SRP not target it, and what does instead?
![The post-translational insertion pathway for tail-anchored proteins: a pre-targeting complex loading the C-terminal segment onto Get3, delivery to the Get1-Get2 receptor, and the crystal structure of Get3 bound to a transmembrane helix](assets/img/fig-12-29.png)
A: Because of timing. By the time the transmembrane helix would be available for recognition, the ribosome has already reached the termination codon and that stretch of sequence is still inside the ribosomal exit tunnel, so SRP never sees it and the protein is released into the cytosol. A specialized chaperone complex recognizes the hydrophobic segment instead and transfers it to a targeting factor called **Get3**. Two proteins at the ER membrane, **Get1** and **Get2**, act both as the receptor for Get3 and as the translocator that inserts the segment into the bilayer. SNARE protein subunits that guide vesicular traffic are a large class of tail-anchored proteins.

Q+: Get3 is unrelated to SRP in sequence, yet the two solve the same problem in the same way. What do they share?
A: A hydrophobic pocket lined with many flexible methionine side chains, letting it recognize diverse hydrophobic segments independently of their exact sequence. The cycle is conceptually the same too: capture in the cytosol, delivery to a membrane receptor, release, and recycling, with directionality bought by binding and hydrolyzing a nucleoside triphosphate. The difference is which one: Get3 uses ATP, SRP uses GTP.

Q+: Are all tail-anchored proteins sent to the ER?
A: No. Some are targeted to mitochondria or peroxisomes instead, and the mechanism of that targeting is not known.

Q: How is a **GPI anchor** attached, and what is unusual about the reaction?
![A precursor protein anchored by a hydrophobic C-terminal sequence being cleaved by transamidase and simultaneously joined to a preassembled GPI anchor](assets/img/fig-12-30.png)
A: A GPI-anchored protein is made with an N-terminal signal sequence that takes it to the ER and a hydrophobic segment of 15 to 20 amino acids very close to its C-terminus, which leaves it anchored in the ER membrane with the rest of the protein in the lumen. Within less than a minute a **transamidase** in the ER membrane recognizes that hydrophobic segment and does two things at once: it cleaves the protein away from its membrane-bound C-terminus and attaches the new C-terminus to an amino group on a preassembled GPI intermediate. So the protein never lets go of the membrane, it just swaps one kind of anchor for another. The signal specifying the modification lies in the hydrophobic C-terminal sequence plus a few residues next to it on the lumenal side, and adding that signal to other proteins makes them GPI-anchored too.
Tags: er, membrane-proteins

---

Q+: What is the GPI anchor actually made of?
A: A lipid bearing an **inositol**, which is where the name glycosylphosphatidylinositol comes from, followed by a glucosamine and three mannoses. The terminal mannose links to a phosphoethanolamine, and that phosphoethanolamine supplies the amino group through which the protein is joined by an amide bond.
Tags: er, membrane-proteins

---

Q+: Where do GPI-anchored proteins end up, and what can be done with them that cannot be done with a transmembrane protein?
A: Because the protein's amino acids were all exposed on the lumenal side of the ER, they end up on the exterior face of the plasma membrane. Since the only thing holding them there is the lipid anchor, a signal that activates a specific phospholipase in the plasma membrane releases them from the cell in soluble form. Trypanosome parasites use exactly this to shed their coat of GPI-anchored surface proteins when the immune system attacks. Q: Proteins arrive in the ER lumen unfolded. What does the ER provide to deal with that, and how do those helpers stay put?
A: A high concentration of resident chaperones and other protein-folding catalysts. They stay because they carry an **ER retention signal** of four amino acids at their C-terminus, KDEL, which is recognized and used to return them to the ER whenever they escape.

Q: What does **BiP** do in the ER lumen, and how does it recognize a protein that needs help?
A: BiP, an hsp70-family chaperone, is a major component of the ER folding machinery, and the same protein that pulls chains through Sec61 post-translationally. It recognizes incorrectly folded proteins and subunits that have not yet assembled into their final oligomeric complexes by binding exposed hydrophobic amino acid sequences: stretches that would be buried in the interior of a correctly folded or assembled chain. An exposed hydrophobic patch is therefore a reliable signature of something unfinished. Bound BiP both stops the protein aggregating and keeps it in the ER, out of the Golgi and the rest of the secretory pathway.

Q+: BiP holds on to a misfolded protein. How does the protein ever get a chance to fold?
A: BiP hydrolyzes ATP to shuttle between high-affinity and low-affinity polypeptide-binding states. So it periodically lets go, giving the substrate an opportunity to fold, and re-binds it if folding has not been achieved. The cycle is what turns a binding protein into a chaperone: holding alone would only freeze the problem in place.

Q: Trace the chemistry by which **protein disulfide isomerase** oxidizes a substrate.
![Protein disulfide isomerase transferring its intramolecular disulfide to a substrate protein through a mixed disulfide intermediate, leaving an oxidized protein and reduced PDI](assets/img/fig-12-31.png)
A: PDI carries an intramolecular disulfide bond. A free sulfhydryl group on the substrate attacks it and donates electrons, which produces an intermolecular **mixed disulfide** between PDI and its substrate. A second free sulfhydryl in the substrate then donates its electrons to that mixed disulfide, which resolves it: the substrate ends up with an internal S-S bond and PDI is left reduced. Other ER enzymes then reoxidize PDI so it can go again.

---

Q+: Why are nearly all cysteines in secreted and lumenal domains disulfide bonded while cytosolic ones are almost never?
A: Because of the redox environment. Forming disulfide bonds needs an oxidizing environment, which the ER lumen maintains, whereas the cytosol is reducing, so disulfides form there only very rarely. The functional reason for having them outside is that a disulfide bond stabilizes the folded state, letting the protein withstand a harsh, variable and chaperone-free extracellular environment where nothing is available to refold it.

Q+: Proteins often have many cysteines and can pair them wrongly. How is that fixed, and what does the name **isomerase** point at?
A: PDI enzymes can run in reverse, reducing incorrectly paired disulfides of immature proteins, and so rearrange the bonds until the protein is correctly folded. That rearranging is the isomerase activity, as distinct from simply making bonds. All PDI enzymes are oxidoreductases capable of catalyzing either formation or breakage. The ER lumen contains several family members, some specialized for reducing disulfides to fully unfold misfolded proteins that need to be sent back to the cytosol for degradation.

Q: What fraction of ER-processed proteins are glycosylated, and how does the ER modification differ from the one seen in the cytosol and nucleus?

Some cytosolic and nuclear proteins are glycosylated too, but not with large oligosaccharides: they carry a much simpler modification, a single N-acetylglucosamine added to a serine or threonine.

Q: Describe **N-linked glycosylation** in the ER: what is transferred, to what, and in how many steps?

Q+: When during a protein's life does this happen, and what makes the timing possible?

Q+: The amide bond of an asparagine side chain is poorly reactive. How does the enzyme get around that?

Q: Which asparagines get glycosylated, and what does the rarity of those sequences tell you?

Q: How is the precursor oligosaccharide built, and where does its assembly take place?

Sugars are first activated in the cytosol as nucleotide-sugar intermediates (UDP or GDP), which donate their sugar first to dolichol and then to the growing oligosaccharide tree in an orderly sequence. Q+: What makes dolichol suitable for the job, and what is the first sugar's bond to it doing?
A: Dolichol is a polyisoprenoid, long and very hydrophobic: its 22 five-carbon units can span the thickness of a lipid bilayer more than three times, so the attached oligosaccharide is firmly anchored and cannot drift away. Q+: The precursor oligosaccharide transferred to an asparagine contains {{14}} sugars: {{2}} N-acetylglucosamines, {{9}} mannoses and {{3}} glucoses.

Q: How common are N-linked oligosaccharides compared with O-linked ones?
A: N-linked are by far the most common, found on 90% of all glycoproteins. Less frequently an oligosaccharide is linked to the hydroxyl group on the side chain of a serine, threonine, hydroxylysine or hydroxyproline, and the first sugar of these **O-linked** oligosaccharides is also added in the ER. Q: Some proteins need N-linked glycosylation to fold properly, yet where the oligosaccharides sit on the surface does not seem to matter. What resolves that puzzle?
![Cycles of glucose trimming by glucosidase and glucose re-addition by glucosyl transferase, driving repeated binding and release of an incompletely folded glycoprotein by calnexin](assets/img/fig-12-34.png)
A: The oligosaccharide is not there to shape the fold, it is there as a **tag** that reports on the state of folding, so its position is irrelevant as long as it is somewhere accessible. Two ER chaperones, **calnexin** and **calreticulin**, named for needing Ca²⁺, are lectins that read the tag: they bind oligosaccharides on incompletely folded proteins, retain them in the ER and stop them aggregating irreversibly. Both also promote the association of incompletely folded proteins with another ER chaperone that binds cysteines not yet in disulfide bonds.

---

Q+: What exactly is the chemical difference between the tag on a folded protein and the tag on an unfolded one, and what cycles it?
A: A single terminal glucose. Shortly after a new protein acquires its precursor oligosaccharide, ER glucosidases rapidly remove two of the three glucoses, leaving one, and it is this **singly glucosylated** form that calnexin and calreticulin recognize, so every newly made glycoprotein starts out bound to one of them. That last glucose is removed over time, and the de-glucosylated glycoprotein no longer binds either chaperone: if it is folded, it can leave the ER. But a **glucosyl transferase** re-adds the terminal glucose selectively to glycoproteins that are not yet completely folded, which sends them back into the arms of calnexin or calreticulin. Trimming and re-addition therefore drive cycles of release and re-binding that continue until the protein is properly folded.

Q+: A glycoprotein has been cycling on and off calnexin for an unusually long time without folding. What happens to it?
- [x] ER mannosidases trim its terminal mannoses, and lectins route the trimmed protein for degradation
- [ ] It is exported to the Golgi anyway, since the cycle cannot detain it indefinitely
- [ ] The glucosyl transferase stops re-adding glucose, releasing it to fold in the cytosol
- [ ] Calnexin unfolds it completely and starts the folding attempt again from scratch
A: The mannosidases catch up with it. The glucose cycle has no built-in time limit, so the clock is kept by a separate, slow reaction: the longer a protein spends in the cycle, the more likely ER-resident mannosidases are to have removed the terminal mannoses from its N-linked oligosaccharide. Only proteins that fold promptly and leave escape trimming, so the quality control is a race between folding and a slow enzyme rather than a judgment about correctness.

Q: A folding intermediate and a hopelessly misfolded protein can look much the same. How does the ER tell them apart before condemning one to degradation?
A: By timing rather than by inspection. The N-linked oligosaccharide acts as a clock: an ER **mannosidase** slowly trims a particular mannose from the core oligosaccharide tree, and the trimmed structure it leaves behind is what the lumenal lectins of the retrotranslocation apparatus recognize. A protein that folds and leaves the ER faster than the mannosidase can remove that mannose escapes degradation; one that lingers accumulates the mark. The distinction the cell makes is not "is this protein wrong?" but "has this protein been trying for too long?", which is a question a slow enzyme can answer.

Q: Walk through **retrotranslocation** and degradation of a misfolded ER protein.

Q+: The ubiquitin does two jobs here rather than one. What are they?

Q+: The chapter says retrotranslocation resembles post-translational import run backwards. In what respects?

Q: What is the **unfolded protein response**, and what does it turn on?
![Three ER-resident sensors, IRE1, PERK and ATF6, each initiating a different downstream pathway that culminates in an active transcription factor](assets/img/fig-12-36.png)
A: The response to an accumulation of misfolded proteins in the ER, the counterpart of the cytosolic heat-shock response. It stimulates transcription of genes that collectively improve the protein-folding capacity of the ER: ER chaperones, the machinery for retrotranslocation and degradation, factors for protein transport out of the ER, and factors for expansion of the ER itself. Three parallel signaling pathways execute it, each starting from an ER-resident sensor and each, by very different downstream mechanisms, ending in an active transcription factor. Their targets overlap, so between them they tune the transcription of hundreds of genes.

Q: How does **IRE1** work, and what makes it a unique exception to a rule about eukaryotic gene expression?
![IRE1 held inactive by BiP, activated by BiP dissociation and misfolded protein binding, oligomerizing and autophosphorylating, and its ribonuclease splicing a cytosolic mRNA](assets/img/fig-12-37.png)
A: IRE1 is a transmembrane protein kinase in the ER. Misfolded proteins make it oligomerize and phosphorylate itself, the same activation logic as some cell-surface receptor kinases. Oligomeric, phosphorylated IRE1 activates its cytosolic endoribonuclease domain, which removes an intron from one specific cytosolic mRNA by cleaving it at two positions that an RNA ligase then joins. The spliced mRNA is translated into an active transcription regulator. The exception is that this is **mRNA splicing occurring in the cytosol and not catalyzed by the spliceosome**, against the rule that all mRNA splicing happens in the nucleus.

Q+: Two things activate IRE1, not one. What are they?
A: Under normal conditions IRE1 is held inactive by association with the lumenal chaperone **BiP**. When misfolded proteins rise, first BiP dissociates from IRE1 in order to bind and protect those misfolded proteins from aggregating, releasing the brake. Second, misfolded proteins bind IRE1's own lumenal domain and facilitate oligomer formation, pushing the accelerator. The same event thus removes the inhibitor and supplies the activator, which makes the switch sharper than either mechanism alone.

---

Q+: How does the pathway switch itself off?
A: By fixing the thing that turned it on. The transcription factor IRE1 produces activates numerous genes including those for chaperones; elevated chaperone levels reduce the amount of misfolded protein in the ER lumen; and with less misfolded protein, BiP returns to IRE1 and the oligomers disperse. The output of the pathway is the negative feedback on its own input.

Q: What does **PERK** do, and why are its two consequences complementary?
A: PERK is a second transmembrane kinase in the ER whose target is a translation initiation protein. Phosphorylating it reduces translation of new proteins throughout the cell, which cuts the load of protein needing to be folded in the ER: that attacks the problem from the supply side. At the same time, some proteins are preferentially translated when initiation factors are scarce, and one of them is a transcription regulator that helps activate the unfolded protein response genes: that attacks it from the capacity side. A single phosphorylation therefore lowers demand and raises supply at once.

---

Q: **ATF6** is a transcription regulator that begins life unable to regulate any transcription. Explain.
A: It is synthesized as a transmembrane ER protein, and while embedded in the ER membrane it cannot reach the nucleus to activate genes. When misfolded proteins accumulate, ATF6 is transported to the Golgi apparatus, where resident proteases in the Golgi membrane cleave off its cytosolic domain. The freed domain migrates to the nucleus and helps activate the unfolded protein response genes. Activating a latent membrane-embedded transcription factor by regulated proteolysis is the same trick used for the transcription regulator that controls cholesterol biosynthesis.
Tags: er, quality-control, core

Q: The unfolded protein response is usually described as an emergency measure. Give two examples of it working in ordinary physiology.
A: **Insulin after a meal.** Insulin production rises substantially in pancreatic β cells after eating, and the elevated demand on the ER, where insulin is first assembled, partially activates PERK so the cell can throttle insulin synthesis rather than overburden the ER. **B cell differentiation.** IRE1 is activated when B cells begin differentiating into antibody-secreting plasma cells, and dramatically expands the cell's ER content in preparation for the very high levels of immunoglobulin about to be assembled there. In both cases the pathway is adjusting ER capacity to match demand, in advance, rather than repairing damage. The relative importance of the three pathways differs between cell types, which is how each type tailors the response to its own needs.

Q+: What happens if homeostasis cannot be restored?
A: Persistently active signaling from the ER, particularly through PERK, activates genes that initiate apoptosis. The reasoning offered is that in a multicellular organism it is often less detrimental to eliminate a persistently dysfunctional cell than to risk its aberrant interactions with its neighbours.

Q: Phosphatidylcholine is made in three steps at the ER membrane. Why is only the first step described as enlarging the membrane?

Q: Lipids do not flip-flop across a synthetic bilayer, yet phospholipids equilibrate across the ER membrane within minutes. By what factor is that faster than spontaneous flipping, and what does it?

Q+: The Golgi, plasma membrane and other compartments of the secretory and endocytic pathways are asymmetric, and they are fed by vesicles from a symmetric ER. What maintains the asymmetry?

Q: Vesicles constantly exchange membrane between the ER, Golgi, endosomes, lysosomes and plasma membrane, yet each of those membranes has its own distinctive lipid composition. How is that maintained?

Q: What is a **peroxisome**, and why can you sometimes see one in an electron micrograph without staining for anything?
![Electron micrograph of three peroxisomes in a rat liver cell showing paracrystalline electron-dense inclusions](assets/img/fig-12-43.png)
A: A small membrane-enclosed compartment found in virtually all eukaryotic cells and a major site of oxygen utilization. It contains oxidative enzymes such as catalase and urate oxidase at concentrations so high that in some cells the enzyme forms a paracrystalline core, made largely of urate oxidase, dense enough to stand out in the micrograph. The organelle is essentially a bag of oxidative chemistry concentrated to the point of crystallizing.

Q: Where did peroxisomes come from, and what is the hypothesis about what they were originally for?
A: The evolutionary origin is not firmly established, but they are generally thought to be a specialized offshoot of the membrane system making up the secretory and endocytic pathways. One hypothesis is that a peroxisome is the vestige of an ancient organelle that performed all the oxygen metabolism of the primitive ancestors of eukaryotic cells. When oxygen from photosynthetic bacteria first accumulated in the atmosphere it would have been highly toxic to most cells, and such an organelle would have lowered the intracellular oxygen concentration while exploiting its chemical reactivity for useful oxidations. On that view, mitochondria later made peroxisomes less critical by taking over many of the same reactions and coupling them to ATP formation, so the oxidations still done by peroxisomes today are partly the ones mitochondria never claimed.

Q: Write the two reactions that give peroxisomes their name, and say what links them.
A: An oxidase uses molecular oxygen to strip hydrogen atoms from a specific organic substrate, producing hydrogen peroxide:
$$\mathrm{RH_2 + O_2 \rightarrow R + H_2O_2}$$
**Catalase** then uses that H₂O₂ to oxidize another substrate by a peroxidation reaction:
$$\mathrm{H_2O_2 + R'H_2 \rightarrow R' + 2H_2O}$$
The link is that the peroxide made by the first enzyme is the oxidant consumed by the second, so a reactive and dangerous intermediate is generated and spent inside the same sealed compartment. If excess H₂O₂ accumulates anyway, catalase disposes of it directly: $\mathrm{2H_2O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O + O_2}$.
Tags: peroxisomes, core

---

Q+: Where does that peroxidation reaction matter most, and what is the familiar example?
A: In liver and kidney cells, where peroxisomes detoxify various harmful molecules entering the bloodstream, including formic acid, formaldehyde and alcohol. About **25%** of the ethanol we drink is oxidized to acetaldehyde this way.
Tags: peroxisomes

Q: What is **β oxidation**, and where does it happen in different organisms?
A: The breakdown of fatty acid molecules by sequentially shortening the alkyl chain two carbon atoms at a time, converting the fatty acid to acetyl CoA, which peroxisomes then export to the cytosol for use in biosynthetic reactions. In mammalian cells β oxidation occurs in **both** mitochondria and peroxisomes. Q: What are **plasmalogens**, and why do peroxisomal disorders so often show up as neurological disease?

Q: Plants have two major types of peroxisome. What are they, and what is a **glyoxysome** for?

Q: Peroxisomes are described as unusually diverse organelles. Give the yeast example of that plasticity.

Q: Peroxisomal proteins arrive by two different routes. What are they?

Q: What are the peroxisomal import signals, and what is the evidence they are sufficient?

Others carry a slightly longer, partially hydrophobic signal sequence near the N-terminus. Q: Trace the peroxisomal import cycle, and say what is unusual about how far the receptor goes.
A: Import is driven by ATP hydrolysis and carried out by proteins called **peroxins**. The C-terminal sorting signal is recognized in the cytosol by the peroxin **Pex5**, which then accompanies its cargo **all the way into** a protein translocator in the peroxisomal membrane rather than handing it over at the surface. After the cargo is released inside, Pex5 has to be recovered: it is modified with **ubiquitin**, which serves as a handle for an ATPase complex of **Pex1 and Pex6** that uses ATP hydrolysis to pull Pex5 back out to the cytosol, ready to pick up the next cargo.

Q+: Ubiquitin usually means destruction. What is it doing here?
A: Acting purely as a handle. The Pex1-Pex6 ATPase needs something to grip on Pex5 to extract it from the peroxisomal membrane, and a ubiquitin tag provides that grip without committing the protein to a proteasome. The same use of ubiquitin as a mechanical handle rather than a death sentence appears in ER retrotranslocation, where it lets an AAA-ATPase pull a misfolded protein out into the cytosol.

Q: The peroxisomal translocator differs from the Sec61 translocator in one dramatic way. What is it, and what problem does that create?
A: It can transport **fully folded and even oligomeric** proteins across the membrane, where Sec61 demands an unfolded chain threaded through a narrow channel. The peroxisomal translocator is built from at least six different peroxins and is thought to adapt dynamically in size to whatever cargo it has to pass. The unresolved problem is exactly the one Sec61's narrowness was designed around: it is not known how such a large pore can be used without leaking contents between the cytosol and the peroxisome.

Q+: Which peroxin handles the N-terminal signal, as opposed to the C-terminal one?

Q: What is **Zellweger syndrome**, and what does it demonstrate?
A: An inherited human disease caused by mutations in any of about a dozen different peroxins, most commonly Pex1, which impair peroxisomal protein import. The cells of affected individuals contain "empty" peroxisomes: the membrane compartment is there but the enzymes never got in. They accumulate very-long-chain and branched-chain fatty acids, which are normally broken down in peroxisomes, and they are deficient in plasmalogens. The result is severe abnormalities of the brain, liver and kidneys, and death soon after birth. It demonstrates that peroxisomal import is not an optional refinement: the whole organelle is useless without it.

Q+: Which findings would you expect in a child with Zellweger syndrome? Select all that apply.
- [x] Accumulation of very-long-chain fatty acids
- [x] Deficiency of plasmalogens, with abnormal myelination
- [ ] Complete absence of any peroxisomal membrane compartment
- [ ] Accumulation of hydrogen peroxide because catalase is overproduced

Q: Compare the subcompartments of a mitochondrion and a chloroplast, and say what makes the thylakoid different in kind from a crista.

Q: Both mitochondria and chloroplasts carry their own DNA, ribosomes and protein synthesis machinery. Why do they still need an elaborate import system?

Q: Where do mitochondrial signal sequences sit, and which are removed?

Q+: What do the structures of TIM22 and TIM23 suggest?

Q: A handful of mitochondrial proteins are not imported at all. Which, and what problem does that create for the cell?

Q: Protein import into the ER is mostly co-translational; into mitochondria it is mostly post-translational. What visible consequence does that have?

Q: How was mitochondrial import studied, and what did the cold-arrest experiment show?
![The N-terminal signal sequence recognized by TOM receptors while cytosolic hsp70 keeps the protein unfolded, translocation through TIM23 spanning both membranes, and cleavage of the signal sequence in the matrix](assets/img/fig-12-49.png)
A: In a cell-free reaction: a radioactively labeled mitochondrial precursor protein is mixed with purified mitochondria and imported, and by changing the conditions in the tube you can establish the biochemical requirements, trap intermediates, and identify which translocators are used. Most of what is known about the mechanism comes from this. So a precursor passes through both membranes simultaneously rather than in two stages.

Q: Mitochondrial import uses three sources of energy at four discrete sites. Set them out.
![Three energy sources for mitochondrial import: the membrane potential driving the signal sequence through TIM23, ATP hydrolysis by mitochondrial hsp70 pulling the chain into the matrix, and the redox potential driving Mia40-mediated oxidation in the intermembrane space](assets/img/fig-12-50.png)
A:
1. **ATP in the cytosol**, spent by chaperones on cycles of binding and hydrolysis that keep the precursor unfolded before import and then release it so it can pass through TOM.
2. **The membrane potential** across the inner membrane, the electrical component of the electrochemical H⁺ gradient, which drives the positively charged signal sequence through the TIM complex by **electrophoresis**.
3. **ATP in the matrix**, used by mitochondrial hsp70 as an import motor.
4. **The redox potential** of the electron-transport chain, used for certain intermembrane-space proteins.
Not every precursor needs all of them to reach its destination.
Tags: mitochondria, core

Q+: How does the matrix hsp70 motor work, and which ER protein is it the counterpart of?
A: It is bound to the matrix side of the TIM23 complex and behaves like **BiP** at the ER. Having a high affinity for unfolded chains, it binds tightly to the imported protein as soon as it emerges from the translocator into the matrix, then undergoes an ATP-dependent conformational change that exerts a pulling force before releasing. That cycle of binding, pulling and release continues until import through TIM23 is complete. Many matrix proteins are then handed on to another chaperone, mitochondrial **hsp60**, which assists their folding through further cycles of ATP hydrolysis.
Tags: mitochondria, core

---

Q+: Explain how **Mia40** turns a redox potential into import.
A: Certain intermembrane-space proteins containing cysteine motifs partially translocate through TOM, so that part of the chain samples the intermembrane space. There they form a transient covalent **disulfide bond to Mia40**, which stops the protein sliding back through TOM into the cytosol. They are eventually released from Mia40 in an oxidized form with intrachain disulfide bonds, which makes them partially folded and therefore trapped in the intermembrane space: the fold itself is the ratchet. Mia40 is reduced in the process and is reoxidized by passing its electrons to the electron-transport chain in the inner membrane, which is how the redox potential ends up paying for the import.
Tags: mitochondria, core

Q+: An isolated mitochondrion is given ATP but its inner membrane is uncoupled so that no membrane potential can be maintained. What happens to import of a matrix protein?
- [x] It stalls, because the signal sequence cannot be driven through the TIM complex
- [ ] It proceeds normally, since ATP is the main energy source for import
- [ ] It proceeds but the signal sequence is not cleaved
- [ ] It reverses, exporting matrix proteins to the cytosol
A: It stalls at the TIM step. The membrane potential is not a general fuel here, it does one specific job: driving the positively charged signal sequence through the TIM complex by electrophoresis. ATP can keep the precursor unfolded in the cytosol and can still power matrix hsp70, but hsp70 only gets a grip once a segment of chain has emerged into the matrix, and without the membrane potential nothing arrives for it to hold. This is why an uncoupler blocks mitochondrial import even when ATP is plentiful.

Q: There are three routes into the inner mitochondrial membrane. Work through each.
![Four routes to the inner membrane: stop-transfer via TIM23, internal-signal insertion via TIM22 with intermembrane-space chaperones, OXA insertion of mitochondrially encoded proteins, and OXA insertion of nuclear-encoded proteins after full import](assets/img/fig-12-51.png)
A:
- **(A) Stop-transfer through TIM23.** A precursor starts into the matrix using TOM and TIM23, but only the N-terminal signal sequence actually enters the matrix. A hydrophobic sequence strategically placed just after it is recognized by TIM23 as a transmembrane domain, which inserts it into the inner membrane and stops further translocation, perhaps through a lateral gate analogous to Sec61's. The rest of the protein enters the intermembrane space through TOM, and the signal sequence is cleaved in the matrix.
- **(B) TIM22, for metabolite transporters.** This family of multipass transporters carries small molecules across the inner membrane, supplying substrates to matrix enzymes such as those of the citric acid cycle and exporting their products. They use **internal** signal sequences, snake through TOM as a loop, bind intermembrane-space chaperones that guide them to TIM22, and there partition their hydrophobic regions into the inner membrane. This step needs the membrane potential to get the appropriate regions to the matrix side, so the transporter ends up with the correct topology.
- **(C and D) OXA.** OXA can only be reached from the matrix side. It inserts the few proteins encoded and translated in the matrix, and also nuclear-encoded proteins that first translocate all the way in via TOM and TIM23: cleaving the N-terminal signal sequence unmasks an adjacent hydrophobic signal at the new N-terminus, which OXA then uses.

Q: Why can the TOM complex not insert a porin into the outer membrane, and what does the job instead?

Q+: What does the SAM complex's homology tell you, and what is the topological correspondence?
A: One of SAM's central subunits is homologous to a bacterial outer membrane protein that inserts β-barrels into the bacterial outer membrane, the **BAM** complex. In bacteria that insertion happens from the periplasmic space, which is the topological equivalent of the mitochondrial intermembrane space. A conserved pathway operating from the equivalent side of the equivalent membrane is further evidence for the endosymbiotic origin of mitochondria.

Q+: There is a chicken-and-egg observation buried in this section. What is it?
A: The central subunits of the TOM and SAM complexes are themselves β-barrel proteins. Making a new copy of either translocator therefore requires a preexisting TOM and SAM to import and insert it. This is the concrete version of the chapter's earlier claim that an organelle cannot be built de novo: the machinery that builds mitochondria can only be delivered by machinery already in mitochondria.

Q: Chloroplast import resembles mitochondrial import in several ways, yet the chapter calls the similarity **convergent** evolution. What is the argument?
A: The similarities are at the level of requirements rather than parts. Both processes are post-translational, use separate translocation complexes in each membrane, require energy, and use multiple types of signal sequence to direct a precursor to the right subcompartment. But many of the protein components forming the translocation complexes differ, and the energetics differ: mitochondria harness the electrochemical H⁺ gradient across their inner membrane, whereas chloroplasts have such a gradient across their **thylakoid** membrane but not their inner membrane, and so use GTP and ATP hydrolysis to power import across the double-membrane envelope. The functional resemblance reflects the common demands of translocating across a double membrane, not common ancestry of the machinery.

Q+: A plant cell has both mitochondria and chloroplasts, and their signal sequences superficially resemble each other. What shows the two are nevertheless kept apart?
A: A swapping experiment. A cytosolic protein joined to the N-terminal signal sequence of a mitochondrial protein goes specifically to the plant cell's mitochondria; the same protein joined to the N-terminal signal sequence of a chloroplast protein ends up in chloroplasts. So the import receptors on each organelle do discriminate between the two kinds of signal, however similar they look to us.

Q: Some chloroplast proteins have to cross **three** membranes. How is that managed?

Q: What holds the nuclear envelope together structurally, and what is the **nuclear lamina** made of?

Q+: The inner and outer nuclear membranes are continuous, yet their protein compositions differ. What is the outer one doing?

Q: What crosses the nuclear pores in each direction, and in what sense is the traffic selective in both?
A: **In:** the many proteins that function in the nucleus, including histones, DNA polymerases, RNA polymerases, transcriptional regulators and RNA-processing proteins, all made in the cytosol. **Out:** all the RNAs that function in the cytosol, including mRNAs, rRNAs, tRNAs and miRNAs, after being synthesized and processed in the nucleus. Export is as selective as import: an mRNA is exported only after it has been properly modified by the nuclear RNA-processing reactions, so the pore is also a checkpoint on maturity, not just an address filter.

Q+: Ribosome assembly needs several selective transport steps in sequence. Trace them.
A: Ribosomal proteins are synthesized in the cytosol, imported into the nucleus, and assembled there with newly made ribosomal RNA. Only after that assembly are the pre-ribosomal particles exported back to the cytosol, where they complete their maturation into functional ribosomes. Certain assembly and transport factors then return to the nucleus to help assemble the next one. Each crossing is a separate selective step, and the ordering is what ensures a subunit is never exported before it has its RNA.

Q: How big is a **nuclear pore complex**, and what is it built from?

The assembled complex holds 500 to 1000 protein molecules, with an estimated mass of 66 million daltons in yeast and 125 million daltons in vertebrates. Most nucleoporins are made of repetitive protein domains of only a few different types, which evolved through extensive gene duplication.

Q+: Some scaffold nucleoporins have an unexpected family resemblance. To what, and what does the chapter make of it?
A: To vesicle coat protein complexes such as **clathrin** and the **COPII coat**, which shape transport vesicles. The scaffold nucleoporins that abut the membrane are evolutionarily and structurally related to them, and one protein is even used as a common building block in both NPCs and vesicle coats. The suggestion is that an ancestral membrane-bending protein, which helped shape the elaborate membrane systems of early eukaryotic cells, gave rise to a family of proteins that stabilize sharp membrane bends wherever they occur: at a nuclear pore, where the inner and outer membranes turn through each other, and at the neck of a budding vesicle.

Q: How many nuclear pore complexes does a cell have, and how much traffic does one carry?
A: A typical mammalian cell has 3000 to 4000, but the number varies widely with the cell's demands: a few hundred in glial cells, almost 20,000 in Purkinje neurons. Each NPC can transport about **1000 macromolecules per second**, and can transport in both directions at the same time.

Q: The internal diameter of a nuclear pore is about 40 nm, big enough for a ribosomal subunit or a virus particle. Why does it not simply leak?
A: Because it is not empty. The pore is filled with unstructured protein regions contributed by the channel nucleoporins, carrying numerous repeats of **phenylalanine-glycine (FG)** motifs whose weak affinity for one another creates a gel-like mesh. That mesh acts as a sieve, restricting the diffusion of large macromolecules while letting small ones through. The barrier is therefore made of something soft and reversible rather than a physical constriction, which is exactly what allows a receptor-bound cargo to push through it while an unescorted protein of the same size cannot.

Q+: Which of these could cross the nuclear envelope without any transport receptor?
- [x] A metabolite of 400 daltons
- [ ] An RNA polymerase subunit of 150,000 daltons
- [ ] An assembled ribosomal subunit, about 30 nm across
- [ ] Histone octamers assembled in the cytosol

A polymerase subunit of 150,000 daltons and a 30 nm ribosomal subunit are both far beyond it, and the exclusion of the ribosome is exactly what confines protein synthesis to the cytosol. Even small proteins such as histones, which could in principle diffuse, mostly use receptors anyway because it is faster.

Q: Sort the nucleoporins into their three classes by where they sit.
A: **Transmembrane ring proteins** span the nuclear envelope and anchor the NPC to it. **Scaffold nucleoporins** form layered ring structures, some of them membrane-bending proteins that stabilize the sharp curvature where the envelope is penetrated. **Channel nucleoporins** line the central pore, and although they have folded domains that anchor them in place, many also carry extensive intrinsically disordered regions rich in FG repeats, whose weak mutual interactions form the gel that blocks passive diffusion of large macromolecules.

Q+: The NPC core is symmetric across the envelope, so the nuclear and cytosolic faces look identical. Where does that symmetry break?
A: In the fibrils. Fibrils protrude from both the cytosolic and the nuclear sides, but they differ: on the **nuclear** side they converge at their distal ends to form a basketlike structure, which the cytosolic fibrils do not. The twofold transverse symmetry is a property of the core, not of the whole complex.
Tags: nucleus, transport

Q+: Why has the precise arrangement of nucleoporins been so hard to pin down?
A: Because atomic resolution analyses have been hindered by the sheer size of the NPC, by its flexible nature, and by the difficulty of purifying enough homogeneous material. The current models come from combining electron microscopy, computational analysis, and crystal structures of nucleoporin subcomplexes, and the precise arrangement remains a matter of intense debate.
Tags: nucleus, transport, methods

Q: How was the effective size of the nuclear pore's sieve measured, and what is the cutoff?
A: By injecting labeled water-soluble molecules of different sizes into the cytosol and measuring their rate of diffusion into the nucleus. Molecules of 5000 daltons or less diffuse in so fast that the envelope can be considered freely permeable to them, and the barrier becomes progressively more restrictive as size rises, until proteins above about **40,000 daltons or 5 nm in diameter** cannot enter by passive diffusion at all. That is what lets nucleus and cytosol maintain different protein compositions, and it is why a mature cytosolic ribosome, at about 30 nm, cannot diffuse through an NPC, confining protein synthesis to the cytosol.
Tags: nucleus, transport, core

Q: What is a **nuclear localization signal**, and what is the classic experiment demonstrating one?
![Immunofluorescence micrographs of SV40 T-antigen with its normal lysine-rich nuclear localization signal concentrated in the nucleus, and with a threonine substituted for a lysine remaining in the cytosol](assets/img/fig-12-56.png)
A: A sorting signal responsible for the selectivity of active nuclear import, most commonly one or two short sequences rich in the positively charged amino acids lysine and arginine, with the precise sequence varying between proteins. The classic demonstration used SV40 virus **T-antigen**: the normal protein carries a lysine-rich sequence and is imported into the nucleus, while a version in which a single **threonine replaces a lysine** stays in the cytosol. One residue is the difference between the two immunofluorescence images.

Q+: Where in a protein can an NLS sit, and what happens with a multiprotein complex?
A: Almost anywhere in the amino acid sequence. NLSs are thought to form loops or patches on the protein surface, and many work even when linked as short peptides to the surface of a cytosolic protein, which says the precise position within the sequence does not matter. For a multicomponent complex, as long as **one** subunit displays a nuclear localization signal the entire complex is imported. Some nuclear proteins carry different types of signal, not all of them yet characterized.

Q: Transport through an NPC differs fundamentally from transport through the translocators of the ER, mitochondria and chloroplasts. Set out the contrast.
A:
| | Nuclear pore complex | ER, mitochondrial, chloroplast translocator |
|---|---|---|
| Pore | Large, constitutively open, filled with a mesh | Small aqueous pore, typically gated by the protein being transported |
| Cargo state | Fully folded proteins and large multiprotein complexes | Usually extensively unfolded |
| Direction | Either direction through the same pore | Unidirectional |
The NPC solves selectivity with a soft chemical barrier that the right receptor can dissolve, rather than with a narrow physical channel that only an unfolded chain can thread.

Q: How was active import through an NPC actually visualized?
![Electron micrographs of colloidal gold spheres coated with nuclear localization signals, first at the cytosolic fibrils, then at the centre of the NPC, then on the nuclear face](assets/img/fig-12-57.png)
A: By coating colloidal gold particles 5 to 10 nm in diameter with peptides containing nuclear localization signals, injecting them into the cytosol of living cells, then fixing cells at various times and looking by electron microscopy. The particles appear first near the tentacle-like fibrils extending from the scaffold nucleoporins into the cytosol, then at the centre of the NPC on the cytosolic face, then on the nuclear face. These particles are far larger than the pore's passive diffusion limit, so the sequence shows that an NLS confers on a large particle the ability to navigate a barrier that would otherwise be impermeable to it.

Q: What are **karyopherins**, and how do adaptor proteins extend what they can carry?
![Different nuclear import receptors binding different nuclear localization signals, and a cargo protein requiring an adaptor that bridges it to an import receptor](assets/img/fig-12-58.png)
A: A large family of nuclear transport receptors: 14 genes in yeast and significantly more in animal cells. Family members mediating import are **nuclear import receptors**, those mediating export are **nuclear export receptors**, and each import receptor binds and transports the subset of cargo proteins whose nuclear localization signal it recognizes. Where a cargo's signal fits no receptor directly, an **adaptor** bridges the two. Adaptors are structurally related to import receptors, suggesting common ancestry, and they recognize NLSs on cargo while carrying an NLS of their own that binds them to an import receptor. Between a variety of receptors and adaptors, cells can recognize the broad repertoire of nuclear localization signals in use.

Q+: The adaptor's own NLS is not always available. Why does that matter?
A: It becomes exposed only when the adaptor is loaded with a cargo protein. An empty adaptor is therefore invisible to the import receptor, so receptors are not occupied by adaptors carrying nothing. The signal reports the loaded state rather than merely the identity of the molecule.

Q: A receptor-cargo complex is far too big for the FG mesh. How does it get through?
![An import receptor with cargo binding FG repeats and locally dissolving the gel-like mesh to diffuse through the nuclear pore](assets/img/fig-12-59.png)
A: By dissolving the barrier locally rather than pushing past it. Import receptors are soluble cytosolic proteins carrying **multiple low-affinity binding sites for FG repeats**. The FG repeats on the cytosol-facing fibrils recruit the receptor and its cargo to the NPC in the first place; then the receptor binds the FG repeats forming the mesh inside the pore, and in doing so disrupts the weak interactions the repeats were making with each other. The gel locally liquefies around the complex, which can then diffuse into and within the pore.
Tags: nucleus, transport, core

Q+: An artificial FG gel was made in a test tube. What did it show, and does the number add up?
A: A gel of unstructured polypeptides containing FG repeats restricts the diffusion of inert cargoes in a size-dependent way very like an NPC does, and diffusion into it is more than **1000-fold faster** for cargoes bound to an import receptor. At that rate a cargo-receptor complex would cross the width of an NPC in a few milliseconds, which is consistent with the transport rates measured in cells. Reconstituting the barrier from its FG repeats alone is strong evidence that the repeats are the barrier.

Q: A receptor-cargo complex diffuses through the NPC in both directions at random. So where does the **direction** of nuclear import come from?
![Ran GAP in the cytosol and Ran GEF bound to chromatin in the nucleus, creating a Ran-GDP cytosol and a Ran-GTP nucleus, and Ran-GTP causing an import receptor to release its cargo](assets/img/fig-12-60.png)
A: From selective release, not selective movement. The import receptor only accelerates diffusion, giving the cargo access to the nuclear compartment; the movement itself is undirected. Directionality comes from the fact that the cargo is discharged **only on the nuclear side**, and that is enforced by the GTPase **Ran**. Ran-GTP binds the import receptor and causes it to let go of its cargo, and since Ran-GTP is only abundant in the nucleus, cargo can only be dropped there. The random back-and-forth diffusion is rectified rather than replaced.
Tags: nucleus, transport, core

---

Q+: Why is Ran-GTP confined to the nucleus and Ran-GDP to the cytosol?
A: Because Ran's two regulators are separated. A cytosolic GTPase-activating protein (**Ran GAP**) triggers GTP hydrolysis and so converts Ran-GTP to Ran-GDP, while a nuclear guanine nucleotide exchange factor (**Ran GEF**) promotes exchange of GDP for GTP and so converts Ran-GDP to Ran-GTP. They stay in their compartments because of preferential association with different structures: Ran GAP with the cytosolic cytoskeleton, Ran GEF with nuclear chromatin. The gradient of the two conformational forms across the envelope is what drives transport in the right direction.
Tags: nucleus, transport, core

---

Q+: Complete the import cycle: what happens to the empty receptor?
A: Having discharged its cargo in the nucleus, the empty import receptor still has Ran-GTP bound, and travels back out through the pore by the same facilitated diffusion. On reaching the cytosol, Ran GAP triggers hydrolysis of the bound GTP, and the resulting Ran-GDP has no affinity for the import receptor, so it falls off and frees the receptor for another round. Ran-GDP permits cargo binding in the cytosol; Ran-GTP forces cargo release in the nucleus.
Tags: nucleus, transport, core

---

Q+: How does Ran-GDP itself get back into the nucleus?
A: By its own import receptor, specific for the GDP-bound conformation of Ran. That receptor is structurally unrelated to the main family of nuclear transport receptors, though it too binds FG repeats in the channel nucleoporins.
Tags: nucleus, transport

---

Q: Nuclear export uses the same receptors, the same pores and the same Ran gradient as import. What single difference reverses the direction?
![Nuclear import and nuclear export both running on the Ran GTPase cycle, with Ran-GTP in the nucleus and Ran-GDP in the cytosol setting the direction of each](assets/img/fig-12-61.png)
A: The nature of cargo binding by the receptor. In nuclear **import**, cargo binding is mutually exclusive with Ran-GTP, so the receptor must let go where Ran-GTP is high. In nuclear **export**, cargo binding **requires** Ran-GTP, so the receptor can only load where Ran-GTP is high, which is the nucleus, and must let go where Ran-GTP is destroyed, which is the cytosol. The places where cargo is picked up and released are exactly swapped, and nothing else has to change.
Tags: nucleus, transport, core

---

Q+: Set out the export cycle in full.
A: Ran-GTP in the nucleus promotes binding of cargo, carrying a **nuclear export signal**, to the export receptor, either directly or through an adaptor. The loaded receptor moves through the NPC along the FG repeats. On reaching the cytosol it meets Ran GAP, which induces hydrolysis of the bound GTP to GDP; the receptor then flips its conformation and releases both its cargo and Ran-GDP into the cytosol. Free export receptors and free Ran-GDP then use the nuclear import pathway to get back in and complete the cycle.
Tags: nucleus, transport, core

---

Q: Nuclear import concentrates specific proteins in the nucleus, which increases order in the cell. What pays for that?
A: GTP hydrolysis by Ran, which is required for both import and export. Concentrating a chosen set of proteins on one side of a membrane is a decrease in entropy and cannot happen spontaneously, so the cell couples it to a chemical reaction that is downhill. Note where the energy is actually spent: not on pushing cargo through the pore, which is diffusion, but on maintaining the Ran-GTP gradient that decides where cargo may be released.
Tags: nucleus, transport, thermodynamics, core

Q+: The cytosol holds mainly {{Ran-GDP}} because {{Ran GAP}} is anchored there on the cytoskeleton, while the nucleus holds mainly {{Ran-GTP}} because {{Ran GEF}} is bound to chromatin.
A: Neither regulator is transported to where it is needed on demand; each simply sticks to a structure that exists in only one compartment, and the gradient follows. That is also why the gradient survives nuclear envelope breakdown in a usable form: Ran GEF stays on the chromatin, so mitotic chromosomes carry a cloud of Ran-GTP around them that marks their position in a cell with no compartments left.

Q: mRNA is exported from the nucleus by a mechanism that uses neither export receptors nor Ran. What does it use instead, and what makes it one-way?
A: The spliced and processed mRNA is assembled with several nuclear RNA-binding proteins, some able to bind the nuclear side of NPCs and others able to bind FG repeats. This export-competent **mRNP** complex navigates the FG mesh on its own account. Directionality comes at the far end: a helicase complex sitting on the cytosolic side of the NPC uses ATP hydrolysis to strip several proteins off the mRNP, including the FG-repeat-binding protein. Without that protein the exported mRNA can no longer enter the pore, so it cannot go back. The stripped RNA-binding proteins are rapidly reimported, using the ordinary import receptor and Ran system, for another round. Note that snRNAs, miRNAs and tRNAs do use export receptors and the Ran-GTP gradient; mRNA is the exception.
Tags: nucleus, transport, core

Q: Some proteins shuttle continuously between nucleus and cytosol. What sets where such a protein is found at steady state?
A: The relative rates of its import and its export, not any absolute property of the protein. If import exceeds export it is mainly nuclear; if export exceeds import it is mainly cytosolic. Changing either rate, or both, relocates the protein. Shuttling arises either because a protein is small enough to diffuse through the pore but carries a signal that keeps retrieving it, or because it carries both a nuclear localization signal and a nuclear export signal.
Tags: nucleus, transport, core

Q+: How do cells actually change those rates?
A: Usually by regulating the localization and export signals themselves, turning them on or off, most often by phosphorylation of amino acids close to the signal sequences. Alternatively, a protein can be held by inhibitory cytosolic partners that either anchor it in the cytosol through interactions with the cytoskeleton or specific organelles, or **mask** its nuclear localization signal so that import receptors cannot see it. An appropriate stimulus releases the anchor or the mask and the protein goes in.
Tags: nucleus, transport, core

Q: How does a *Drosophila* embryo use regulated nuclear transport to tell its back from its belly?

A: The early embryo is a syncytium, many nuclei sharing one cytoplasm and arranged around the periphery just under the plasma membrane. Q: Trace how **NF-AT** is controlled, and say where a famous class of drugs acts.
![NF-AT held phosphorylated in the cytosol of a resting T cell, dephosphorylated by calcineurin at high Ca2+, imported to activate transcription, and returned to the cytosol when Ca2+ falls](assets/img/fig-12-63.png)
A: In a resting T cell, the nuclear factor of activated T cells sits in the cytosol in a phosphorylated state. When the T cell is activated by foreign antigen, the intracellular Ca²⁺ concentration rises, and at high Ca²⁺ the protein phosphatase **calcineurin** binds NF-AT and dephosphorylates it. That dephosphorylation does two complementary things: it exposes nuclear import signals and it blocks a nuclear export signal. The NF-AT-calcineurin complex is therefore imported, and NF-AT activates transcription of the many genes needed for T cell activation. When Ca²⁺ falls, NF-AT is released from calcineurin, rephosphorylated, its import signals inactivated and its export signal re-exposed, and it returns to the cytosol. **Cyclosporin A and FK506**, among the most potent immunosuppressive drugs, work by inhibiting calcineurin's ability to dephosphorylate NF-AT, blocking nuclear accumulation and so blocking T cell activation.

Q+: A patient on cyclosporin A has a T cell that has just been stimulated by antigen, and its cytosolic Ca²⁺ has risen normally. Where is NF-AT?
- [x] In the cytosol, still phosphorylated
- [ ] In the nucleus, activating transcription as usual
- [ ] In the cytosol, dephosphorylated but unable to be imported
- [ ] Degraded, because it cannot reach its site of action
A: In the cytosol and still phosphorylated. Cyclosporin A acts upstream of everything the Ca²⁺ signal would have achieved: it blocks calcineurin's ability to dephosphorylate NF-AT, so the phosphates stay on, the nuclear import signals stay hidden, the nuclear export signal stays exposed, and the protein remains where it started. The Ca²⁺ rise is real and the receptor signaling worked, which is what makes the drug so specific: it leaves the whole detection apparatus intact and severs only the last step, the one that moves the regulator across the envelope.

Q+: Why is it worth noticing that the switch both exposes an import signal and blocks an export signal?
A: Because steady-state localization is set by the ratio of the two rates, a change to only one of them shifts the balance while a change to both moves it much further. Raising import and abolishing export at the same time converts a protein that was reliably cytosolic into one that is reliably nuclear, with the same single chemical event, and makes the switch fully reversible when it is undone.

Q: How does a cell detect that it is short of cholesterol, and how does that reach the nucleus?
![SREBP held in the ER by the INSIG-SCAP complex when cholesterol is high, and at low cholesterol travelling to the Golgi where two proteases release its cytosolic transcription regulatory domain](assets/img/fig-12-64.png)
A: **SREBP** (sterol response element binding protein), the transcription regulator controlling expression of cholesterol biosynthetic enzymes, is synthesized as an ER membrane protein. It is held in the ER by a complex of **INSIG** and **SCAP**, and SCAP is the sensor: it binds cholesterol. When cholesterol is plentiful the site is occupied and the complex stays put. When the cholesterol-binding site on SCAP is **empty**, SCAP changes conformation and dissociates from INSIG, freeing the SCAP-SREBP complex to be packaged into transport vesicles bound for the Golgi. There, two Golgi-resident proteases cleave SREBP at site 1 and site 2 to free its cytosolic domain, which moves into the nucleus and activates the promoters of genes for cholesterol uptake and synthesis. So more cholesterol is made exactly when its concentration falls below a threshold.

Regulated intramembrane proteolysis is the shared trick: keeping the regulator built, folded and instantly available, but tethered where it cannot act.

Q: What has to be taken apart for the nuclear envelope to break down at mitosis, and what starts the process?
![Phosphorylation of lamins and NPC proteins dismantling the nuclear lamina and envelope at prophase, and dephosphorylation reversing it at telophase](assets/img/fig-12-65.png)
A: Three things: the nuclear lamina, the NPCs, and the membranes of the nuclear envelope. The trigger is the **cyclin-dependent kinase (Cdk)** activated at the onset of mitosis, which phosphorylates nucleoporins, lamins and inner nuclear membrane proteins so as to disrupt their interactions with each other and with chromatin. Envelope membrane proteins, no longer tethered to pore complexes, lamina or chromatin, simply disperse throughout the ER membrane. The purpose is to let microtubules reach the replicated chromosomes for segregation.
Tags: nucleus, mitosis, core

---

Q+: Phosphorylation loosens the connections, but something still has to pull the envelope off the chromosomes. What does?
A: The microtubule motor protein **dynein**, which is recruited to the outer nuclear membrane early in mitosis and exerts a pulling force. Transmembrane proteins that tether the outer nuclear membrane to the inner membrane and to the lamina transduce that force through the envelope and pull it off the chromatin. Once the barrier is gone, nuclear proteins not bound to membranes or chromosomes intermix completely with cytosolic proteins.
Tags: nucleus, mitosis

---

Q+: Is nuclear envelope breakdown universal?
A: No. Mitotic breakdown of the nuclear envelope occurs in all metazoan cells, but in many other species, yeasts among them, the envelope remains intact through mitosis and the nucleus divides by fission.
Tags: nucleus, mitosis

---

Q: When the envelope breaks down, Ran GEF stays bound to chromatin. What follows from that?
A: Ran molecules near the chromatin stay mainly in their GTP-bound form, while those further away are converted to Ran-GDP by cytosolic Ran GAP. So the mitotic chromosomes are surrounded by a **cloud of Ran-GTP**, a positional signal in a cell that no longer has any compartment boundaries. That cloud is important for assembling the mitotic spindle, and later it is the positional marker that tells nuclear envelope components where to reassemble.
Tags: nucleus, mitosis, core

---

Q+: How does the Ran-GTP cloud direct reassembly?
A: After chromosome segregation, Cdk is inactivated and nucleoporins, lamins and nuclear membrane proteins are dephosphorylated. Some NPC proteins had become bound to nuclear import receptors during breakdown, and near the chromosomes the high Ran-GTP concentration releases them from those receptors, exactly as it would release an ordinary import cargo. The freed NPC proteins attach to the chromosome surface and assemble into new NPCs. At the same time, dephosphorylated lamins bind chromatin again and recruit ER membranes through the inner nuclear membrane proteins they interact with.
Tags: nucleus, mitosis, core

---

Q: Reassembling the nuclear envelope is described in this chapter as a case of **engulfment**. Trace it, and say what makes it selective.
A: The lamin network begins to re-form around regions of individual decondensing daughter chromosomes and recruits membranes carrying the lamin receptors that used to be in the inner nuclear membrane. The ER then progressively wraps around the entire group of chromosomes until it forms a sealed envelope, engulfing the chromosomes and the proteins bound to them, with lateral fusion of the membrane sheets closing it up. Selectivity comes from the geometry rather than from any translocator: the new inner envelope is closely applied to the chromosome surface, so it encloses only what was already bound to the mitotic chromosomes and excludes everything else. Import receptors then repopulate the nucleus through the NPCs, and because Ran-GTP is now inside and Ran-GDP outside, that import is unidirectional. Large proteins with no nuclear localization signal, ribosomes included, are left out.
Tags: nucleus, mitosis, core

---

Q+: Why can nuclear proteins be reimported after every mitosis, when an ER signal sequence is used only once?
A: Because nuclear localization signals are **not removed**. An N-terminal ER signal sequence is cleaved by signal peptidase as soon as it has done its job, so the mature protein can never be targeted again, which is fine because it will never be back in the cytosol. A nuclear protein, by contrast, is dumped into the common cytoplasm at every mitosis and has to find its way home again, so it keeps its signal permanently.
Tags: nucleus, sorting, core

Q: The chapter states that each NPC transports about 1000 macromolecules per second, and never says how that was measured or how efficient a single transport attempt is. What do single-molecule measurements show?
A: Individual cargoes can be watched entering and leaving one pore by fluorescence microscopy. The **interaction time**, how long a cargo lingers at the NPC, is 5 to 10 ms for most protein cargoes, with the whole reported range running from about 33 ms down to under 1 ms depending on the molecule and the conditions; an NLS-2xGFP cargo measured $7.8 \pm 0.4$ ms. That is of the same order as the roughly 4.5 ms that problem 12-17 gets from the diffusion coefficient through an artificial FG gel, so the mesh model and the observed rates agree.
The striking number is the **transport efficiency**: of the cargoes that enter the pore from the cytoplasmic side, only about **half** come out on the nuclear side at low importin-β, the rest diffusing back out the way they came. This is exactly what the chapter's own model predicts and does not spell out: movement through the pore is undirected diffusion, so a cargo has no obligation to finish the crossing, and the ratchet is applied only at the far end. Raising the importin-β concentration shortens the interaction time to about 1 ms and pushes efficiency higher.

Q+: The chapter gives the internal diameter of an NPC as about 40 nm. Is that a fixed property of the structure?
A: No, and the figure is closer to the constricted state than to the resting one. Cryo-electron tomography with subtomogram averaging, applied to NPCs inside intact cells rather than in isolated nuclear envelopes, found that the central channel is nearly **60 nm** across in exponentially growing cells, against about 40 nm in isolated preparations. The dilation is reversible: NPCs constrict when cells are energy-depleted or subjected to hypertonic osmotic stress, with large-scale movement and bending of the scaffold roughly halving the volume of the central channel. The link appears to be tension in the nuclear envelope, which the NPC scaffold responds to, so the pore is a mechanosensitive structure and not a rigid grommet. Measured in yeast and in *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii*.

Q: The chapter says that an artificial FG gel restricts diffusion in a size-dependent way like an NPC does, but does not say why that experiment settles anything. What was the argument about?
A: Whether the FG repeats form a genuine cohesive **phase** that has to be dissolved, or whether they merely crowd the pore and get in the way. The reconstitution is the direct test: if you can build a gel out of nothing but FG-repeat domains, and it reproduces the NPC's permeability barrier, including exclusion of inert proteins above the right size and rapid entry of importin-bound cargo, then the FG repeats are sufficient on their own and no other feature of the pore is needed to make the barrier. The measurements behind problem 12-17 come from that line of work, and the chapter's statement that a receptor "locally dissolves the gel-like mesh" is the selective phase model stated as fact. The definitive reconstitution is [[hlsmann-2012-permeability-reconstituted-nuclear]].

Q: True or false? Like the lumen of the ER, the interior of the nucleus is topologically equivalent to the outside of the cell.
A: **False**, and the trap is that a neighbouring space really is. The nuclear interior is topologically equivalent to the **cytosol**: the nuclear pore complexes provide a continuous aqueous passage between them, so a molecule crosses no membrane going from one to the other, which is also why the two intermix during mitosis. The space that *is* equivalent to the outside of the cell is the **perinuclear space** between the inner and outer nuclear membranes, which is continuous with the ER lumen. So the nucleus is wrapped in a compartment that is topologically outside the cell, without being outside it itself.

Q: True or false? ER-bound and free ribosomes, which are structurally and functionally identical, differ only in the proteins they happen to be making at a particular time.
A: **True**. There are no two classes of ribosome. A ribosome is drawn to the ER membrane only because the mRNA it is translating encodes an ER signal sequence, which SRP recognizes as it emerges; once translation terminates, the subunits are released and rejoin the common cytosolic pool, free to be recruited to any mRNA next time. The polyribosome stays attached because the mRNA does, not because its ribosomes belong there.

Q: True or false? The signal sequence binds to a hydrophobic site on the ribosome causing a slowdown in protein synthesis, which resumes when SRP binds to the signal sequence.
A: **False**, and it has the causality backwards at both ends. The hydrophobic binding site for the signal sequence belongs to **SRP**, not to the ribosome: a methionine-lined pocket positioned near the ribosomal exit tunnel. And SRP binding is what **causes** the slowdown, because once SRP has engaged the signal sequence its other end binds at the interface between the ribosomal subunits, the same site elongation factors use. Synthesis resumes at full speed only later, after the SRP receptor has delivered the complex to a translocator and SRP has been released.

Q: True or false? Peroxisomes are found in only a few specialized types of eukaryotic cell.
A: **False**. Peroxisomes are found in virtually all eukaryotic cells. What varies is their number, size and enzyme content, which are tuned to the cell's chemistry: yeast grown on sugar has a few small ones and the same yeast grown on methanol or on fatty acids builds numerous large ones, and plants keep two different kinds. The variation is presumably what makes them look specialized. That they are not optional is clear from Zellweger syndrome, where import failure leaves empty peroxisomes and the affected infants die soon after birth.

Q: True or false? The two signal sequences required for insertion of nucleus-encoded proteins into the mitochondrial inner membrane via the TIM23 complex are cleaved off the protein in different mitochondrial compartments.
A: **False**, because only one of the two is cleaved at all. The N-terminal matrix-targeting signal sequence is removed by the signal peptidase in the **matrix**. The second signal is the hydrophobic segment that follows it, and it is not a peptide to be discarded: TIM23 recognizes it as a transmembrane domain, stops translocation, and releases it laterally into the inner membrane, where it stays as the anchor holding the mature protein in place. A signal sequence that becomes part of the finished protein cannot also be cleaved off somewhere.

Q: True or false? To avoid the collisions that would occur if two-way traffic through a single pore were allowed, nuclear pore complexes are specialized so that some mediate import while others mediate export.
A: **False**. Every NPC handles both directions, and can do so at the same time. Collisions are not the problem the question assumes because an NPC is not a narrow single-file channel: the central channel is tens of nanometres across and filled with a soft FG mesh through which many receptor-cargo complexes diffuse past one another at once. The core of the NPC is even built symmetrically across the envelope, so its nuclear and cytosolic faces look the same, which is hard to reconcile with a pore dedicated to one direction. Direction is imposed chemically by where the Ran-GTP is, not structurally by which pore you use.

Q: Inserting phosphorylated LAT into an artificial bilayer with Grb2 and Sos1 produces micrometer-sized condensates below the membrane, but a Grb2 with only one SH3 domain instead of two gives none. Grb2ΔSH3 is still multivalent, so why does it fail?
![Key components of T cell receptor condensate formation: pLAT with three phosphotyrosines, Sos1 with four SH3-binding sites, Grb2 with SH2 and two SH3 domains, Grb2 with only one SH3 domain, and the condensates seen with each](assets/img/fig-q12-01.png)
A: Because what matters is not total valency but valency **toward each partner**, and Grb2ΔSH3 has a valency of one toward Sos1. A condensate is an extended cross-linked network, and to build one every component along a path has to be able to continue it in more than one direction. Full-length Grb2 has an SH2 domain that binds a phosphotyrosine on pLAT and **two** SH3 domains that bind Sos1, so a single Grb2 can hold onto one partner while still offering another connection onward: it is a branch point. Grb2ΔSH3 has SH2 plus one SH3, so it can attach exactly one Sos1 to exactly one pLAT site and then the chain stops. It is bivalent, which makes it a linker, but a linker is a chain terminator once its two sites are filled, so you get small discrete complexes rather than a percolating network. Sos1's four SH3-binding sites can only be exploited if the SH3-bearing partner is itself capable of branching.

Q: What is the fate of a protein with no sorting signal?
A: It stays in the cytosol as a permanent resident, and that is the correct outcome rather than a failure. The cytosol is the default destination, since every protein starts there on a cytosolic ribosome and only leaves if something recognizes an address on it. A signal is required to get **out**, so the absence of one is itself an instruction. The cytosol is not a small place to be left in either: it is a little more than half the cell's volume and the site of most intermediary metabolism.

Q: In a cell dividing every 24 hours, the equivalent of one new plasma membrane must pass through the ER each day. Given that the ER membrane is 20 times the area of a plasma membrane, and that plasma membrane proteins spend on average 30 minutes in the ER before exiting, what is the ratio of plasma-membrane-bound proteins to other membrane proteins in the ER?
A: About **1 to 960**, so they are rare: roughly one in a thousand of the ER's membrane proteins is on its way to the cell surface at any moment. The reasoning is that at steady state the amount present equals flux times residence time. The flux is one plasma membrane's worth per 24 hours and the residence time is 0.5 hours, so the ER holds $1 \times 0.5/24 = 1/48$ of a plasma membrane's worth of such protein. Since the protein-to-lipid ratio is the same in both membranes, protein content is proportional to area, and the ER's total is 20 plasma-membrane-equivalents. That gives $(1/48) / (20 - 1/48) = 1/959$.
The point is worth keeping: the ER is overwhelmingly occupied with its own residents and with proteins bound for other destinations, and the plasma membrane, which dominates textbook diagrams of a cell, is a minor customer.

Q: A multipass transmembrane protein has five boxed segments, with the signal peptidase cleavage site marked immediately after the first. In which compartments do the N- and C-termini of the mature protein end up?
![A multipass transmembrane protein drawn as five numbered membrane-spanning segments from N to C, with an arrow marking the cleavage site after segment 1](assets/img/fig-q12-02.png)
A: **Both in the ER lumen.** Segment 1 is a cleavable N-terminal signal sequence, so it does not survive in the mature protein and must not be counted as a transmembrane segment. Signal peptidase has its active site on the lumenal face, so cleavage leaves the new N-terminus in the ER lumen. That fixes the whole alternation, because each following segment must insert in the orientation opposite to the last:
- N-terminus in the lumen, then segment 2 crosses lumen to cytosol
- loop in the cytosol, segment 3 crosses cytosol to lumen
- loop in the lumen, segment 4 crosses lumen to cytosol
- loop in the cytosol, segment 5 crosses cytosol to lumen
- C-terminus in the lumen
Four transmembrane segments remain, an even number, so the two ends necessarily lie on the same side. The trap is counting five segments and concluding the termini must be on opposite sides.

Q: All new phospholipids are added to the cytosolic leaflet of the ER membrane, yet the ER's two leaflets end up with a symmetrical distribution while the plasma membrane, which gets all its components from the ER, is strongly asymmetrical. How is each achieved?
A: By two different translocators doing opposite jobs. Symmetry in the ER is produced by a **scramblase**, which is nonselective about head group, needs no energy, and moves phospholipids in both directions until the two leaflets are equilibrated. It probably works by offering a hydrophilic path through the hydrophobic interior so the head group can pass, and it speeds flipping by something like 100,000-fold over the spontaneous rate. Asymmetry in the plasma membrane is produced and maintained by **flippases**, which are selective for particular phospholipids and move them unidirectionally, and which therefore cannot work passively: they couple transport of the head group to ATP hydrolysis and are active transporters.
Note that symmetry is the state a membrane reaches when a barrier is removed, whereas asymmetry has to be paid for continuously. That is why the ER needs only an unenergized channel and the plasma membrane needs a pump.

Q: Cells with functional peroxisomes incorporate 9-(1'-pyrene)nonanol (P9OH) into their membrane lipids, and UV light then kills them by generating reactive oxygen species. Cells without peroxisomes lack the enzyme that does the incorporating. How would you use P9OH to select cells missing peroxisomes?
A: Grow a mutagenized population in the presence of P9OH, then irradiate with UV and recover the survivors. Any cell with working peroxisomes will have built the pyrene into its membrane lipids and will be killed when UV excites it and generates reactive oxygen species in situ; a cell that cannot make peroxisomes never incorporates the compound, has no membrane-embedded photosensitizer, and lives. This is a **positive selection**, which is what makes it powerful: rather than screening colonies one at a time for a missing organelle, you kill everything except what you want. The underlying enzymology is the peroxisomal ether-lipid pathway, the same one that makes plasmalogens, which is why a fatty alcohol like P9OH gets into lipids only where peroxisomes are functional.

Q: Yeast *Ura3*, normally a cytosolic enzyme essential for uracil synthesis, was modified to carry a mitochondrial matrix import signal, and cells carrying it in place of the normal gene were grown without uracil. Most died; the rare survivors were defective in mitochondrial import. Explain the selection.
A: The modification moves an enzyme away from the substrate it needs. Uracil synthesis happens in the **cytosol**, so importing Ura3 into the matrix sequesters it behind two membranes where it is useless; with no uracil in the medium, a cell with intact import starves for uracil and dies. A cell whose import machinery is broken fails to carry out the very step that was doing the damage, so the modified Ura3 stays in the cytosol and does its normal job, and that cell grows. The trick converts "has a defect in mitochondrial import", which is invisible, into "can grow without uracil", which is trivially selectable, and it is how TIM complex components were first identified.

Q+: The selection could not have recovered cells with *no* mitochondrial import at all. Why not?
A: Because mitochondrial import is essential: about 99% of mitochondrial proteins are made in the cytosol and imported, so a cell that could import nothing would have no functional mitochondria and would not grow either. The survivors therefore have to be partially defective, impaired enough to leave some Ura3 in the cytosol while still importing enough of everything else to live. Any selection of this kind recovers hypomorphs rather than nulls.

Q: Dihydrofolate reductase carrying a mitochondrial targeting sequence is efficiently imported, but if it is first incubated with methotrexate, which binds tightly in its active site, it stays in the cytosol. Why does ligand binding block import?
A: Because import requires the protein to be **unfolded**, and methotrexate will not let it unfold. A mitochondrial precursor has to thread through the narrow channels of TOM and TIM as an extended chain, which is why chaperones keep it unfolded in the cytosol in the first place. Methotrexate binds the folded active site with very high affinity, and a tightly bound ligand stabilizes the native conformation: unfolding now costs the binding energy of the drug as well as the folding energy of the protein, so the precursor stays folded, cannot be threaded, and remains outside. The targeting is untouched, and in fact the signal sequence can enter the translocator and stick there with the folded domain jammed against the outside of the pore, which is how such constructs are used to trap translocation intermediates deliberately. This experiment is the standard demonstration that mitochondrial import is a translocation of an unfolded chain rather than a transfer of a folded protein, and it is the sharpest contrast with the nuclear pore, which imports fully folded proteins routinely.

Q: Why do mitochondria need a special translocator to import proteins across the outer membrane, when that membrane already contains large pores formed by porins?
A: Because porins are large by the standards of ions and metabolites, not of proteins. A porin channel passes inorganic ions and small molecules of up to roughly a thousand daltons but is far too narrow for a polypeptide, so nothing that needs importing could use one. The second reason matters more: a porin is an open, unselective hole, and selectivity is the entire point of an import system. TOM recognizes signal sequences and admits only proteins that carry them, then hands each one on to the machinery for its particular destination. A channel wide enough and open enough for proteins to wander through would abolish the compartment rather than serve it, which is the same reason Sec61 keeps a plug in its pore.

Q: If 32 million histone octamers are needed to package the human genome, how many histone molecules must cross each nuclear pore per second in a cell whose nucleus has 3000 pores and which divides once per day?
A: About **one per second per pore**. Each octamer is eight histone molecules, so $32 \times 10^6 \times 8 = 2.56 \times 10^8$ histone molecules must be imported per cell cycle. A day is 86,400 seconds, giving about 2960 histones per second for the whole nucleus, and dividing among 3000 pores gives 0.99 per pore per second.
That is a reassuring answer rather than an alarming one. Against an NPC's capacity of roughly 1000 macromolecules per second, packaging an entire human genome's worth of new chromatin occupies about a thousandth of the available traffic. The pores are not working hard to do it, which is worth knowing because the sheer size of the histone requirement makes it sound as though it ought to be the limiting step.

Q: FG repeat domains at 50 mM form a gel that excludes MBP-mCherry but readily admits importin-MBP-GFP, for which the diffusion coefficient through the gel is about 0.1 μm²/sec. Using $t = x^2/2D$, how long would it take to cross a yeast nuclear pore 30 nm thick, and is that fast enough?
![Diffusion of MBP-mCherry and of importin-MBP-GFP from solution into a gel of FG repeats at 30 seconds, 10 minutes and 30 minutes](assets/img/fig-q12-03.png)
A: About **4.5 milliseconds**. With $x = 30$ nm $= 0.03$ μm and $D = 0.1$ μm² s⁻¹, $t = (0.03)^2 / (2 \times 0.1) = 0.0009/0.2 = 4.5 \times 10^{-3}$ s.
Yes, that is fast enough, and the comparison is worth making carefully. An NPC handles around 1000 macromolecules per second, which is one every millisecond on average, so a 4.5 ms crossing would be too slow only if the pore could carry one cargo at a time. It cannot be thought of that way: the channel is tens of nanometres across and many complexes are in transit simultaneously, so a few milliseconds per crossing is entirely compatible with the observed throughput. Single-molecule measurements in cells give interaction times in the same 5 to 10 ms range, which is a genuine check on the model rather than a coincidence, since the gel experiment used nothing but purified FG domains in a tube.

Q: Radioactive nucleoplasmin, a large pentamer, was injected into frog oocytes as the intact protein, as heads alone, as tails alone, or as heads carrying a single tail. Every form except heads accumulated in the nucleus when injected into the cytoplasm, and every form was retained in the nucleus when injected there. How does that distinguish active transport from passive diffusion plus binding?
![Intact nucleoplasmin, heads with one tail, heads only and tails only, each injected into the nucleus or the cytoplasm of a frog oocyte, with the resulting location of the protein](assets/img/fig-q12-04.png)
A: The heads are the control that separates the two models, because they do the second thing without doing the first. Retention of heads injected into the nucleus shows that the head domain binds something in the nucleus and is held there. A "passive diffusion plus binding" model says accumulation is exactly that: get in by diffusion, then be trapped by binding to a nuclear component. On that model heads should accumulate from the cytoplasm too, since they demonstrably have the binding. They do not. So binding to a nuclear component is not what drives accumulation.
What the heads lack is the **tail**, and a single tail is enough: heads with one tail accumulate like the intact protein. The tail therefore carries a nuclear localization signal that actively triggers transport, and it is needed on only one subunit of the pentamer for the whole assembly to be carried in. This is also the experiment showing that the pore imports a large folded oligomer intact, which no translocator of the ER or mitochondrial type could do.
