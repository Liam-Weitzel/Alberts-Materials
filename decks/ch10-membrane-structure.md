Q: Beyond keeping the cell's contents in, what jobs does a cell membrane do?
A: It maintains the essential differences between the cytosol and the extracellular environment, and inside a eukaryotic cell the membranes of each organelle do the same for its contents. Ion gradients built across membranes by specialized proteins can be used to synthesize ATP, to drive the transport of selected solutes, or, in nerve and muscle, to produce and transmit electrical signals. The plasma membrane also carries receptors that sense signals from outside. Those receptors are an unusual kind of traffic: they transfer information across the membrane rather than molecules.

---

Q: What general structure do all biological membranes share, however different their functions?
![An electron micrograph of a red blood cell plasma membrane in cross section showing its bilayer, beside a three-dimensional schematic of a lipid bilayer with embedded protein molecules and cholesterol](assets/img/fig-10-01.png)
A: Each is a very thin film of lipid and protein molecules held together mainly by noncovalent interactions. The lipids form a continuous double layer about 5 nm thick, which provides the basic fluid structure and acts as an essentially impermeable barrier to most water-soluble molecules. The proteins, most of which span the bilayer, mediate nearly everything else: transporting specific molecules across, catalysing reactions such as ATP synthesis, linking the cytoskeleton to the extracellular matrix or to a neighbouring cell, and detecting signals. The structure is dynamic, with most of its molecules moving about in the plane of the membrane.

Q: In 1773 Benjamin Franklin wrote that "not more than a Tea Spoonful" of oil had calmed perhaps half an acre of a pond on Clapham Common. What does that imply about the thickness of the oil film?
A: That it was no more than a couple of nanometers thick, about one molecule. Half an acre is roughly 2000 m², and if the teaspoon held 5 mL, the film can have been at most $5 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^3 / 2023\ \text{m}^2 \approx 2.5 \times 10^{-9}$ m, or 2.5 nm. A smaller spoon, or a larger patch of calm water, makes it thinner still, and both figures are Franklin's rough estimates. The point is the order of magnitude: the oil spread until it could spread no further, as a film one molecule deep, which is about the length of a fatty acid chain.
Tags: history

---

Q+: How did Agnes Pockels and Irving Langmuir turn that observation into a measurement?
A: Pockels, working at home with a trough she built from an old balance and household objects, measured how surface films change the surface tension of water as they are compressed. Rayleigh translated her letter and sent it to *Nature*, where it appeared in 1891 (the chapter's reference list misprints her name as "Pockets"). The popular story that she discovered the effect washing dishes in the kitchen sink is regarded by historians as a myth. Langmuir developed the trough and showed in 1917 that films of fatty acids are one molecule thick, with the polar group in the water and the hydrocarbon chain pointing up. He also found that the area occupied per molecule is the same whatever the chain length, which is only possible if the chains are standing on end rather than lying flat.
Tags: history

---

Q+: In 1925 Gorter and Grendel used exactly this kind of trough to conclude that cells are covered by a lipid bilayer. What did they do, and what was wrong with it?
A: They extracted the lipids from red blood cells of humans and five other mammals, spread them as a monolayer on a trough, and compared its area with the total surface area of the cells. The monolayer covered about twice the area, so they concluded the lipid layer was two molecules thick. The conclusion was right, but as it is usually told, it rested on two errors that cancelled. The acetone extraction recovered only about three quarters of the lipid, and the cell surface area they measured on dried smears, about 99 μm², fell well short of the roughly 140 μm² of a living red cell, which is also the area implied by the book's figures of $7 \times 10^8$ lipids at $5 \times 10^6$ per μm².

Q: It is estimated that about {{30%}} of the proteins encoded in an animal's genome are membrane proteins.
A: It takes many kinds of membrane protein for a cell to function and interact with its environment. The same figure comes up again later in the chapter from a different direction, as the fraction of an organism's proteins predicted from hydropathy plots to be transmembrane proteins.

Q: How much of a typical animal cell membrane is lipid, and how densely are the lipid molecules packed?
A: Lipids make up about 50% of the mass of most animal cell membranes, with nearly all of the remainder being protein. There are approximately $5 \times 10^6$ lipid molecules in a 1 μm × 1 μm area of bilayer, and about $7 \times 10^8$ in the plasma membrane of a single red blood cell.

---

Q+: Use those two numbers to estimate the surface area of a red blood cell.
A: About **140 μm²**. Dividing $7 \times 10^8$ lipid molecules by $5 \times 10^6$ per μm² gives 140 μm². That is a sensible size for a cell roughly 8 μm across with a flattened biconcave shape, and it is a useful check that the two figures quoted in the book are consistent with each other.

Q: What does it mean that every lipid in a cell membrane is **amphiphilic**, and what does a phospholipid look like?
![Phosphatidylcholine drawn as a chemical formula, a space-filling model, a schematic with a polar head of choline, phosphate and glycerol above two hydrocarbon tails, one kinked by a double bond, and as a simple symbol](assets/img/fig-10-02.png)
A: Each has a hydrophilic, polar end and a hydrophobic, nonpolar end. The most abundant membrane lipids are the phospholipids, which have a polar head group containing a phosphate and two hydrophobic hydrocarbon tails. In animal, plant and bacterial cells the tails are usually fatty acids of 14 to 24 carbon atoms. Typically one tail is saturated and the other is unsaturated, carrying one or more *cis*-double bonds, each of which puts a kink in the tail. The length and saturation of the tails decide how the molecules pack against one another, and so how fluid the membrane is.

---

Q: What defines a **glycerophospholipid**, and which three are most abundant in mammalian membranes?
![Formulas and symbols for phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylcholine, sphingomyelin and sphingosine, with different head groups coloured differently](assets/img/fig-10-03.png)
A: A three-carbon glycerol backbone. Two long-chain fatty acids are linked through ester bonds to adjacent carbons of the glycerol, and the third carbon is attached to a phosphate group, which in turn carries one of several types of head group. Combining different fatty acids with different head groups gives many distinct glycerophospholipids. Phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylserine and phosphatidylcholine are the most abundant in mammalian cell membranes.

---

Q+: How does a **sphingolipid** differ, and how is sphingomyelin built?
A: It is built from sphingosine rather than glycerol. Sphingosine is itself a long fatty acid-like tail with an amino group and two hydroxyl groups at one end, so it supplies one of the two tails on its own. In sphingomyelin, the most common sphingolipid, a fatty acid tail is attached to the amino group and a phosphocholine group to the terminal hydroxyl. Together, phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylserine and sphingomyelin make up more than half the mass of lipid in most mammalian cell membranes.

Q+: At physiological pH, which of the four major phospholipids of the mammalian plasma membrane carries a net charge?
- [ ] Phosphatidylcholine
- [ ] Phosphatidylethanolamine
- [x] Phosphatidylserine
- [ ] Sphingomyelin
A: Phosphatidylserine, which is net negative. The phosphate contributes one negative charge in all four, and each head group has one positively charged amine, but serine brings an extra carboxyl group that carries a second negative charge. The other three have one positive and one negative charge and are electrically neutral overall. That single net charge turns out to matter a great deal, because of which side of the membrane phosphatidylserine is kept on.

Q: What is a **sterol**, and how does cholesterol sit in a lipid bilayer?
![The structure of cholesterol as a formula, a space-filling model and a schematic, with a small polar hydroxyl head, a rigid planar steroid ring structure and a short nonpolar hydrocarbon tail](assets/img/fig-10-04.png)
A: A rigid ring structure related to the steroids, with a single polar hydroxyl group and a short nonpolar hydrocarbon chain. Fungi, plants and animals each have their own sterols, distinguished mainly by the side chain on the ringed scaffold, and cholesterol is the major one in animal cells. Eukaryotic plasma membranes contain especially large amounts, up to one sterol molecule for every phospholipid. Cholesterol orients itself with its hydroxyl group close to the polar head groups of neighbouring phospholipids, so its flat rings lie alongside the upper part of their hydrocarbon tails.
![A to-scale drawing of a cholesterol molecule between two phospholipids in one monolayer, marking a cholesterol-stiffened region near the head groups and a more fluid region deeper in, beside a space-filling model of a bilayer](assets/img/fig-10-05.png)

Q: A hydrophobic molecule dispersed in water forms no unfavourable bonds with anything. Why does it still raise the free energy?
![Acetone forming hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions with surrounding water molecules, compared with 2-methylpropane, which cannot, surrounded by water molecules forced into an icelike cage](assets/img/fig-10-06.png)
A: Because of what it does to the water. Unable to interact favourably with the molecule, the adjacent water molecules reorganize into icelike cages around it. Those cages are more ordered than the surrounding liquid water, so forming them lowers the entropy, and that entropic cost is what raises the free energy. The cost is minimized if the hydrophobic molecules, or the hydrophobic portions of amphiphilic molecules, cluster together so that the fewest water molecules are affected. A polar molecule such as acetone avoids the problem entirely by forming hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions with the water, which is why it dissolves.

---

Q: Amphiphilic molecules in water form either micelles or bilayers. What decides which?
![Cone-shaped amphiphilic molecules packing into a spherical micelle and cylinder-shaped ones packing into a bilayer, with cross sections of a micelle and a lipid bilayer in water](assets/img/fig-10-07.png)
A: The shape of the molecule. Both arrangements hide the hydrophobic tails from water while exposing the heads, but the geometry has to work. Cone-shaped molecules, with a head wider than the tails, pack naturally into spherical micelles with the tails inward. Cylinder-shaped molecules such as phospholipids, with two tails making the hydrophobic part as wide as the head, cannot pack into a small sphere and instead form double-layered sheets with the tails sandwiched between two layers of heads. Real micelles are thought to be far more irregular than the tidy drawings suggest.

---

Q: Why does a lipid bilayer seal itself, and what unavoidable consequence follows?
![A planar phospholipid bilayer exposing its hydrophobic tails to water along its edges, curling up and closing into a sealed sphere that shields the tails, going from energetically unfavourable to favourable](assets/img/fig-10-08.png)
A: Any free edge of a bilayer exposes hydrophobic tails to water, which is energetically unfavourable, so a small tear is patched spontaneously as the lipids rearrange to eliminate the edge. The same prohibition on free edges applies to the sheet as a whole: the only way a bilayer can have no edges at all is to close in on itself and form a sealed compartment. The formation of an enclosed space, fundamental to making a living cell, therefore follows directly from the shape and amphiphilic nature of the phospholipid molecule, with no machinery required.

Q: What is a **liposome**, and what is it used for?
![A drawing of a small spherical liposome in cross section with water inside and out, beside a cryo-electron micrograph of an unstained synthetic phospholipid vesicle about 100 nm across](assets/img/fig-10-09.png)
A: A synthetic lipid bilayer in the form of a spherical vesicle. Liposomes are commonly used as model membranes, especially for studying membrane proteins that have been incorporated into them. The micrograph shows one that was neither fixed nor stained, just rapidly frozen in water at liquid-nitrogen temperature, so what is seen is close to the real object. Artificial bilayers can also be made as planar lipid films.

Q: What is **flip-flop**, and why does it happen so rarely?
![A computer simulation of a phosphatidylcholine bilayer showing disordered head groups and tangled tails with water on both sides, and a diagram of lipid motions: lateral diffusion, flexion, rotation, and the rare flip-flop](assets/img/fig-10-10.png)
A: The migration of a lipid molecule from the monolayer, or leaflet, on one side of the bilayer to the monolayer on the other. For any individual phospholipid it happens on a time scale of hours, because the hydrophilic head group would have to enter and pass through the hydrophobic core, which is energetically disfavoured. Cholesterol is the exception and flip-flops rapidly, since it has only a single hydroxyl group to accommodate transiently in the core.

---

Q+: Within a monolayer, by contrast, how fast do lipids move?
A: Very fast. A lipid molecule exchanges places with its neighbours about $10^7$ times per second, giving a lateral diffusion coefficient of about $10^{-8}$ cm²/s. That means an average lipid diffuses the length of a large bacterial cell, about 2 μm, in roughly 1 second. The numbers agree with the formula for diffusion in two dimensions, where mean squared displacement is $4Dt$: $\sqrt{4 \times 10^{-8} \text{ cm}^2\text{/s} \times 1 \text{ s}} = 2 \times 10^{-4}$ cm, which is 2 μm. Lipids also rotate rapidly about their long axis and have flexible hydrocarbon chains.

---

Q+: The computer simulation of a bilayer looks nothing like the neat rows in textbook drawings. What does it show?
A: A model of 100 phosphatidylcholine molecules, started in a regular arrangement, had the position of every atom calculated after 300 picoseconds of simulated time. The result is very disordered, with an irregular, ragged surface of variously spaced and oriented head groups on both sides, and tails from one monolayer reaching those in the other if they are long enough. The model accounts for almost every measurable property of a synthetic bilayer, including its thickness, lipids per unit area, depth of water penetration and the unevenness of its surfaces. That liquidity is also what lets lipids rapidly patch transient holes opened by mechanical stress.

Q: New phospholipids are made in only one monolayer, mainly the cytosolic leaflet of the ER, and phospholipids hardly ever flip-flop on their own. What problem does that create, and how is it solved?
A: If the new molecules stayed where they were made, only one leaflet would grow and the membrane would expand asymmetrically. The problem is solved by a special class of membrane proteins, the **phospholipid translocators**, which catalyse rapid flip-flop from one monolayer to the other. They come in two kinds, **flippases** and **scramblases**, discussed in Chapter 12. Studies of labelled lipids in real membranes and living cells confirm that, as in synthetic bilayers, phospholipids are otherwise confined to their own monolayer.

Q: Liposomes are fluid, yet two of them suspended in water do not fuse. What keeps them apart, and what does that mean for organelles?
A: Water. The polar head groups bind water molecules and ions, and those have to be displaced before two bilayers can come close enough to fuse. Biological membranes have an even larger hydration shell because of the proteins embedded in or attached to them. The shell insulates the many internal membranes of a eukaryotic cell from one another and prevents uncontrolled fusion, which is what keeps compartments intact. Every membrane fusion event in a cell is instead catalysed: tightly regulated tethers bring the right membranes together, and fusion proteins force out the water layer between them.

---

Q: Why does the fluidity of a cell membrane have to be precisely regulated?
A: Because the membrane's functions depend on it. Fluidity lets membrane proteins interact rapidly and transiently, and some membrane transport processes and enzyme activities stop altogether when the bilayer's viscosity is experimentally raised beyond a threshold. Fluidity depends on both the composition of the bilayer and its temperature.

Q: What is the **phase transition** of a lipid bilayer, and what lowers the temperature at which it happens?
![Unsaturated hydrocarbon chains with cis-double bonds packing loosely and irregularly, compared with saturated chains packing tightly and evenly](assets/img/fig-10-11.png)
A: A synthetic bilayer made of a single type of phospholipid changes from a liquid to a two-dimensional rigid crystalline, or gel, state at a characteristic temperature. The transition temperature is lower, meaning the membrane is harder to freeze, if the hydrocarbon chains are short or contain double bonds. Shorter chains interact less with one another, in the same monolayer and across to the opposite one. *Cis*-double bonds put kinks in the chains that make them harder to pack together. Because the unsaturated chains are more spread apart, bilayers containing them are also thinner. Real membranes, being complex mixtures, are adjusted to stay liquid just above their phase-transition point.

Q+: A culture of bacteria growing at 37°C is shifted to 20°C. What change in its membrane lipids would you expect over the following hours?
- [x] More fatty acids with *cis*-double bonds
- [ ] Longer, fully saturated fatty acid tails
- [ ] More cholesterol inserted into the plasma membrane
- [ ] Fewer *cis*-double bonds, to stiffen the membrane
A: More *cis*-double bonds. Organisms whose temperature follows that of their environment, such as bacteria and yeasts, adjust the fatty acid composition of their membranes to keep fluidity roughly constant. As the temperature falls, the drop would otherwise push the bilayer toward its gel state, so they synthesize fatty acids with more *cis*-double bonds to counteract it. Longer saturated chains would do the opposite. The cholesterol option is a trap twice over: bacterial membranes contain none, and cholesterol does not simply make membranes more fluid.

Q: Cholesterol tightens the packing of lipids in a bilayer, yet it does not make the membrane less fluid. How can both be true?
A: Because it acts differently on different parts of the phospholipid tails. Its rigid, platelike steroid rings interact with the first few CH₂ groups of the neighbouring hydrocarbon chains, just below the head groups, and reduce their mobility. That makes the bilayer less deformable in that region and decreases its permeability to small water-soluble molecules, so cholesterol strengthens the membrane as a barrier. At the same time it prevents the hydrocarbon chains from coming together and crystallizing, which is what would otherwise make the membrane freeze.

Q+: A purified membrane fraction turns out to be about 70% phosphatidylethanolamine, with no detectable cholesterol, phosphatidylcholine or sphingomyelin. Where did it most likely come from?
- [ ] Myelin

The absence of phosphatidylcholine together with the dominance of a single phospholipid points to a bacterial plasma membrane.

Q: How do archaeal membrane lipids differ from those of bacteria and eukaryotes, and what trick do thermophilic archaea use?
A: Instead of fatty acids, archaeal lipids usually carry prenyl chains 20 to 25 carbons long. Prenyl chains and fatty acid chains are similarly hydrophobic and flexible, so the bilayer behaves much the same. In thermophilic archaea the longest lipid chains span both leaflets, tying the two monolayers together and making the membrane particularly stable to heat. The general point is that lipid bilayers can be built from molecules with similar features but quite different molecular designs.

---

Q+: The difference between archaeal and other membrane lipids goes deeper than the chains. What is the "lipid divide"?
A: Archaeal lipids are isoprenoid chains joined to glycerol by **ether** linkages, and the glycerol backbone is glycerol-1-phosphate. Bacterial and eukaryotic lipids are fatty acids joined by **ester** linkages to glycerol-3-phosphate, which is the mirror-image form. The membrane-spanning lipids of thermophiles are tetraethers such as caldarchaeol, in which each molecule reaches across the whole membrane, so the membrane is effectively a monolayer. The divide is not quite absolute: some bacteria have been found carrying a pathway for making archaeal-type lipids.

Q: How many different lipid species does a eukaryotic cell membrane contain, and why so many?
A: Mass spectrometry puts it at perhaps 500 to 2000 species, with even the simple plasma membrane of a red blood cell containing well over 150. Some of this is combinatorial variation in head groups, chain lengths and desaturation within the major classes. Heterogeneity itself seems to be useful: it antagonizes phase transitions, and it may help membrane-spanning proteins fit better into the bilayer so that it does not leak around them. On top of that there are structurally distinct minor lipids with specific jobs, of which the inositol phospholipids are the best-known example. Present only in small amounts, they guide membrane traffic and cell signalling, and a large number of enzymes make and destroy them locally to create both small signalling molecules and docking sites that recruit specific proteins from the cytosol.

Q: A lipid bilayer is a two-dimensional fluid, so you might expect its lipids to be randomly mixed. When do they separate into domains instead, and what does that require energetically?
![Two giant fluorescent liposomes: one made of phosphatidylcholine and sphingomyelin stains uniformly, while one that also contains cholesterol shows many separate dark and bright patches](assets/img/fig-10-12.png)
A: Random mixing is the default expectation because the van der Waals attractions between neighbouring hydrocarbon tails are not selective enough to hold particular groups of lipids together. With certain mixtures, though, artificial bilayers undergo phase transitions that segregate lipids laterally into separate domains. Giant liposomes made from a 1:1 mixture of phosphatidylcholine and sphingomyelin form uniform bilayers, while a 1:1:1 mixture of phosphatidylcholine, sphingomyelin and cholesterol separates into two phases, revealed by a dye that partitions preferentially into one of them. For that to happen, the attractive forces between particular lipids must outweigh the entropic cost of concentrating them. The domains in these giant liposomes are far larger than anything expected in a cell membrane.

Q: What is a **lipid raft**, and why has its existence in living cells been argued over for so long?
![A model of a raft domain in which cholesterol, glycolipids, GPI-anchored proteins and transmembrane glycoproteins cluster together in a thicker patch of bilayer](assets/img/fig-10-13.png)
A: A proposed specialized domain of the plasma membrane into which particular lipids and proteins segregate. The argument arises because, although many lipids and membrane proteins are not uniformly distributed, large-scale lipid phase segregations of the kind seen in artificial bilayers are rarely observed in living cells. What is observed is specific proteins and lipids concentrating temporarily and dynamically, helped by protein-protein interactions. In the current model, weak protein-protein, protein-lipid and lipid-lipid interactions reinforce one another to gather cholesterol, sphingolipids, glycolipids, GPI-anchored proteins and some transmembrane proteins into the same region. Such clusters range from nanoclusters of a few molecules, perhaps only a few nanometers across, up to structures visible by electron microscopy such as caveolae.

Q+: Rafts are drawn as slightly thicker than the surrounding membrane. Why would their composition make them so?
- [x] They are enriched in sphingolipids and glycolipids with long, straight hydrocarbon chains, packed together with cholesterol
- [ ] They are enriched in phosphatidylserine, whose negative charge repels the opposite monolayer
- [ ] GPI anchors add a third lipid layer beneath the outer monolayer
- [ ] They contain more unsaturated lipids with *cis*-double bonds
A: Long, straight chains packed with cholesterol. Lipids with unsaturated chains spread apart and give thinner bilayers, as Figure 10-11 notes, so a patch enriched in straight-chained sphingolipids and glycolipids, held in an ordered arrangement alongside cholesterol, should be thicker. The unsaturated option has it backwards, and phosphatidylserine sits in the inner monolayer rather than being characteristic of rafts.

Q+: What might rafts be for?
A: Organizing and concentrating membrane proteins. The two roles the book suggests are gathering proteins for transport together in membrane vesicles, and bringing proteins into assemblies that work together, as when they convert extracellular signals into intracellular ones. The tendency of lipid mixtures to undergo phase transitions, seen so clearly in artificial bilayers, may help create these rafts even if full phase separation does not occur in cells.

Q+: Sean Munro's 2003 essay "Lipid rafts: elusive or illusive?" made the sceptical case. What was it?
A: That most of the evidence for rafts was indirect and open to other interpretations. The usual criteria were that a protein resisted extraction with cold Triton X-100 and that its behaviour was sensitive to cholesterol depletion, but detergent resistance need not reflect how proteins are arranged in an undisturbed membrane. Direct imaging had not agreed on how big rafts are, or even whether they exist. Munro also pointed out that at the plasma membrane's levels of cholesterol and sphingolipid, an ordered phase ought to cover most of the outer leaflet rather than forming small islands, and that no mechanism was known for making domains in the inner leaflet. He did not claim rafts were demonstrably wrong, only that alternative models were equally plausible, which is roughly where the book's cautious wording leaves things. [[munro-2003-lipid-rafts]]
Tags: rafts

Q: What are **lipid droplets** for, and how do they differ between cell types?
A: They store excess lipids, which can be retrieved as building blocks for membrane synthesis or as fuel for energy generation. Fat cells, or adipocytes, are specialized for storage and contain a single giant droplet filling most of their cytoplasm. Most other cells have many smaller droplets, whose number and size vary with the cell's metabolic state. Fatty acids can be liberated from droplets on demand and exported to other cells through the bloodstream.

---

Q+: Every other membrane-bounded compartment of the cell is enclosed by a bilayer. Why is a lipid droplet surrounded by a single monolayer of phospholipids?
A: Because there is no water inside it. A droplet stores neutral lipids, triacylglycerols and cholesterol esters, which have no hydrophilic head groups and so aggregate into a three-dimensional hydrophobic blob rather than a bilayer. To sit in the aqueous cytosol, the blob needs only its outer surface covered: phospholipids arrange themselves with their hydrophobic acyl chains facing into the droplet and their head groups facing the cytosol. A bilayer exists to shield tails from water on both sides, and a droplet has water on one side only. The surface also carries a large variety of proteins, some of them enzymes of lipid metabolism.

---

Q: Lipid droplets form from the ER membrane when cells are exposed to high concentrations of fatty acids. Walk through the model of how one buds.
![Neutral lipids accumulating as a lens between the two monolayers of the ER membrane at a ring of seipin with an assembly factor, then budding into the cytosol as a growing droplet coated with a phospholipid monolayer and associated proteins](assets/img/fig-10-14.png)
A: Triacylglycerols and cholesterol esters are made by enzymes in the ER membrane and accumulate between its two monolayers, self-aggregating into a lens. Multiple copies of the transmembrane protein **seipin** assemble into a ring together with a lipid droplet assembly factor. In the presence of triacylglycerols, seipin releases the assembly factor, which moves to the cytosolic monolayer and helps the droplet bud outward, fill with nonpolar lipids and pinch off as an organelle wrapped in a single phospholipid monolayer and its proteins. In adipocytes, droplets can then fuse and reach a gigantic size.

---

Q+: In liver cells and enterocytes, droplets go somewhere else. Where, and why?
A: They bud into the lumen of the ER rather than into the cytosol, and from there they are secreted as lipoprotein particles. Those particles carry metabolic energy, in the form of triglycerides, through the body. Enterocytes are the absorptive cells of the gut, so they are packaging the fat absorbed from a meal, while the liver exports fat it has made or stored.

Q: How are the phospholipids of a human red blood cell membrane distributed between its two monolayers?
![The asymmetric distribution of phospholipids and glycolipids in the red blood cell bilayer, with glycolipids and choline-containing lipids in the outer monolayer and phosphatidylethanolamine and negatively charged phosphatidylserine in the inner monolayer](assets/img/fig-10-15.png)
A: Very unevenly. Almost all the phospholipids with choline in their head group, phosphatidylcholine and sphingomyelin, are in the outer monolayer. Almost all those with a terminal primary amino group, phosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidylserine, are in the inner monolayer. Since phosphatidylserine is the negatively charged one, there is a significant difference in charge between the two halves of the bilayer. Glycolipids are all in the outer monolayer, while cholesterol is distributed roughly equally between the two. The asymmetry is generated and maintained by membrane-bound phospholipid translocators.

Q+: A fluorescently labelled protein that binds specifically to phosphatidylserine is added to a suspension of healthy, intact red blood cells. What will it label?
- [ ] The whole outer surface uniformly
- [ ] Only the glycolipid-rich patches
- [x] Almost nothing
- [ ] Only the cholesterol-rich regions
A: Almost nothing. In a healthy cell nearly all the phosphatidylserine is held in the inner, cytosolic monolayer, where a protein added from outside cannot reach it. That is exactly what makes exposed phosphatidylserine useful as a signal: finding it on the outside of a cell means something has gone wrong with the machinery that keeps it in.

Q: Lipid asymmetry matters most in converting extracellular signals into intracellular ones. How do protein kinase C and PI 3-kinase depend on the lipids of the cytosolic monolayer?
A: **Protein kinase C (PKC)**, activated in response to various extracellular signals, binds the cytosolic face of the plasma membrane where phosphatidylserine is concentrated, and it requires that negatively charged phospholipid for its activity. **Phosphatidylinositol (PI)**, a minor phospholipid concentrated in the cytosolic monolayer, works differently: its head group has to be modified first. Lipid kinases add phosphate groups at distinct positions on the inositol ring, creating binding sites that recruit specific proteins from the cytosol to the membrane at a particular time and place. **PI 3-kinase** is activated by extracellular signals and recruits intracellular signalling proteins to the plasma membrane this way, and similar lipid kinases in intracellular membranes recruit proteins that guide membrane transport.
Tags: asymmetry, signaling

---

Q+: How does **phospholipase C** turn a membrane lipid into a messenger?
A: It is one of several phospholipases in the plasma membrane that are activated by extracellular signals to cleave specific phospholipids, generating fragments that act as short-lived intracellular messengers. Phospholipase C cleaves an inositol phospholipid in the cytosolic monolayer into two fragments. One stays in the membrane and helps activate protein kinase C. The other is released into the cytosol and stimulates the release of Ca²⁺ from the endoplasmic reticulum. (The fragments are diacylglycerol and inositol trisphosphate, taken up properly in Chapter 15.)
Tags: asymmetry, signaling

---

Q: How do animals use phospholipid asymmetry to tell live cells from dead ones?
A: When an animal cell undergoes apoptosis, phosphatidylserine, normally confined to the inner monolayer of the plasma membrane, rapidly appears in the outer monolayer. Neighbouring cells such as macrophages recognize the exposed phosphatidylserine as a signal to phagocytose the dead cell and digest it. The book attributes the translocation to impairment of the active mechanisms that generate and maintain the asymmetry.
Tags: asymmetry

Q+: Is switching off the flippase enough to put phosphatidylserine on the surface of a dying cell?
A: Not by itself, and not quickly. Work from Shigekazu Nagata's lab in 2013 and 2014 showed that apoptosis does two things, both through caspase cleavage. Caspases cleave the flippase ATP11C, which normally pumps phosphatidylserine back into the inner leaflet; cells carrying a caspase-resistant ATP11C did not expose phosphatidylserine during apoptosis and were not engulfed. Caspases also cleave and activate the scramblase **Xkr8**, which moves lipids in both directions and so collapses the asymmetry. Stopping the flippase alone would leave only spontaneous flip-flop, which takes hours. So the "eat me" signal is actively switched on as well as no longer being actively suppressed. Xkr8's counterpart in the worm *C. elegans* is CED-8.

Q+: How do laboratories use exposed phosphatidylserine to count apoptotic cells?
A: With **annexin V**, a Ca²⁺-dependent phospholipid-binding protein with high affinity for phosphatidylserine. Fluorescently labelled annexin V is added to living cells in a buffer containing calcium and measured by flow cytometry: healthy cells keep their phosphatidylserine on the inside and do not bind it, while apoptotic cells do. Propidium iodide, a DNA dye that cannot cross an intact plasma membrane, is usually added alongside, so that cells in early apoptosis (annexin-positive, still excluding the dye) can be told apart from cells whose membranes have already broken down.

Q: Glycolipids have the most extreme asymmetry of any membrane lipid: they are found only in the monolayer facing away from the cytosol, in the plasma membrane and in intracellular membranes alike. Why?
A: Because of where their sugars are added. In animal cells glycolipids are built on sphingosine, like sphingomyelin, and the sugar groups are attached in the lumen of the Golgi apparatus. The Golgi lumen is topologically equivalent to the outside of the cell, so the sugars start out facing away from the cytosol and stay that way, since the lipids do not flip-flop. When the lipids are delivered to the plasma membrane, the sugars end up exposed on the cell surface, where they have important roles in the cell's interactions with its surroundings.

Q+: How abundant are glycolipids, and what makes them tend to cluster?
A: They probably occur in the plasma membrane of every eukaryotic cell, where they generally make up about 5% of the lipid molecules in the outer monolayer, and they are also found in some intracellular membranes. They self-associate, partly through hydrogen bonds between their sugars and partly through van der Waals forces between their long, straight hydrocarbon chains. That is why they partition preferentially into lipid raft phases.

Q: What distinguishes a **ganglioside** from other glycolipids, and where are gangliosides most abundant?
![Structures of the neutral glycolipid galactocerebroside, the GM1 ganglioside with its branched sugar chain including a charged sialic acid, and the sialic acid N-acetylneuraminic acid](assets/img/fig-10-16.png)
A: Gangliosides are the most complex glycolipids. Their oligosaccharides contain one or more sialic acid residues, which give them a net negative charge. In human cells the sialic acid is mostly N-acetylneuraminic acid, or NANA. A glycolipid whose sugar head is uncharged, such as galactocerebroside, is called neutral. More than 40 different gangliosides have been identified, and they are most abundant in the plasma membrane of nerve cells, where they make up 5 to 10% of the total lipid mass.

Q+: Animal glycolipids are built on sphingosine. What about bacteria and plants?
A: In bacteria and plants almost all glycolipids are derived from glycerol, as most of their phospholipids are. Animal cells are the ones that build their glycolipids on sphingosine, the same backbone as sphingomyelin.

Q: What are glycolipids thought to do?
A: The book is cautious here, since hints come mainly from where they are found. In epithelial cells they are confined to the exposed apical surface, where they may protect the membrane against harsh conditions such as low pH and high concentrations of degradative enzymes. Charged glycolipids such as gangliosides alter the electrical field across the membrane and the concentrations of ions, especially Ca²⁺, at its surface. Glycolipids also take part in cell recognition: membrane-bound carbohydrate-binding proteins called **lectins** bind the sugar groups of glycolipids and glycoproteins during cell-cell adhesion. Mutant mice lacking all their complex gangliosides show nervous system abnormalities, including axonal degeneration and reduced myelination.

Q: The ubiquitous glycolipids of the cell surface are exploited by pathogens as a way in. Give the examples, and trace how cholera toxin causes diarrhoea.
A: Influenza virus interacts with the sialic acid sugars of gangliosides during entry into cells, and polyomaviruses bind gangliosides first too. Q: How much of a membrane's mass is protein, and why does that vary so much?
A: Because membrane proteins perform most of a membrane's specific tasks, the amount of protein tracks what the membrane is for. In myelin, which serves mainly as electrical insulation around nerve-cell axons, protein is less than 25% of the mass. In membranes that make ATP, such as the internal membranes of mitochondria and chloroplasts, it is about 75%. A typical plasma membrane sits in between, at about half.

Q+: In a membrane that is 50% protein by mass, are there equal numbers of lipid and protein molecules?
A: No, there are about 50 lipid molecules for each protein molecule. Equal masses do not mean equal numbers because a lipid molecule is small, under a thousand daltons, while a protein molecule is typically many tens of thousands, so it takes dozens of lipids to match the mass of one protein.

Q: What are the ways a protein can associate with a lipid bilayer?
![Ten ways proteins associate with the lipid bilayer: single-pass and multipass alpha helices, proteins exposed on one side only, a beta barrel, an amphiphilic helix, a lipid-anchored protein, a GPI-anchored protein, and proteins bound only to other membrane proteins](assets/img/fig-10-17.png)
A: Membrane proteins, like lipids, are amphiphilic, and the variety comes from how their hydrophobic parts meet the bilayer.
1. Crossing once as a single α helix, sometimes with a fatty acid chain inserted into the cytosolic monolayer as well.
2. Crossing several times as multiple α helices.
3. and 4. Inserted in the bilayer with the bulk of their mass on only one side. Glycosyltransferases in the Golgi and SNARE proteins, which catalyse membrane fusion, are examples.
5. Crossing as a rolled-up β sheet, a β barrel.
6. Lying entirely in the cytosol, anchored by an amphiphilic α helix whose hydrophobic face partitions into the cytosolic monolayer.
7. Lying in the cytosol, attached solely by a covalently bound lipid chain, a fatty acid or a prenyl group.
8. Lying entirely outside, attached through an oligosaccharide linker to phosphatidylinositol in the noncytosolic monolayer: a GPI anchor.
9. and 10. Not entering the bilayer at all, but bound to either face by noncovalent interactions with other membrane proteins.

Types 1, 2 and 5 are **transmembrane proteins**, with mass extruding on both sides.

Q+: Which of these proteins could be released from the membrane by washing with a very high-salt solution, leaving the bilayer intact?
- [x] A protein bound only to the cytosolic domain of a transmembrane protein
- [ ] A single-pass transmembrane protein
- [ ] A protein anchored by a prenyl group
- [ ] A GPI-anchored protein
A: The one held only by protein-protein contacts. Solutions of very high or low ionic strength, or of extreme pH, interfere with protein-protein interactions without disrupting the bilayer, so they release membrane-associated proteins bound noncovalently to other membrane proteins. These are often called **peripheral membrane proteins**. Transmembrane proteins, and proteins held in the bilayer by lipid groups or hydrophobic polypeptide regions that insert into its core, cannot be released this way.

Q: A GPI-anchored protein ends up entirely outside the cell, attached only by a lipid. How does it get there?
A: It is first made and inserted into the ER membrane with a single transmembrane segment at its C-terminus, much like a protein exposed on one side only. While still in the ER, that transmembrane segment is cleaved off and a glycosylphosphatidylinositol anchor is added in its place, leaving the protein bound to the noncytosolic, luminal surface of the ER membrane solely by the anchor. Transport vesicles then deliver it to the plasma membrane, where the luminal face has become the outside of the cell.

Q: Only one class of membrane protein can work on both sides of the bilayer or carry molecules across it. Which, and what does that say about how attachment relates to function?
A: Transmembrane proteins. A cell-surface receptor has to bind a signal molecule outside and generate a signal inside, so it is usually transmembrane. A transport protein has to provide a path for small hydrophilic molecules through the hydrophobic barrier, and the architecture of multipass proteins, whether a bundle of helices or a β barrel, suits that job. Proteins that work on only one side, by contrast, are often attached exclusively to the lipid monolayer or to a protein domain on that side.

Q: Compare the three ways a lipid chain can be covalently attached to a protein to anchor it to the cytosolic face of a membrane.

Q+: Src-family kinases carry both a myristoyl and a palmitoyl group. Why two, and why does it matter which one is added second?
A: A single lipid anchor is not a very strong attachment, so a second is often added to hold a protein more firmly. All Src-family kinases are myristoylated on their N-terminal amino group during synthesis. For most, the second modification, palmitic acid on a cysteine, is added in response to an extracellular signal and helps recruit the kinase to the plasma membrane. When the signalling pathway is turned off the palmitic acid is removed, and the kinase returns to the cytosol. The reversible anchor is the one that makes membrane localization a regulated switch. Ras-family GTPases use the same logic with a prenyl group plus palmitic acid.

Q+: A Src-family kinase is engineered with its N-terminal glycine replaced by alanine. What would you predict?

- [ ] It is myristoylated on the alanine instead, with no change
- [ ] It acquires a GPI anchor and ends up on the cell surface
- [ ] It becomes a transmembrane protein

The protein is made in the cytosol and has no other built-in way to reach the membrane, so it should largely stay there. Q: Some proteins are membrane proteins at one moment and soluble the next. How do Rab GTPases manage it?
A: Their lipid anchor is concealed or exposed by a conformational change that depends on the bound nucleotide. In the GDP-bound state a Rab protein is soluble in the cytosol, often stabilized by binding to a GDP dissociation inhibitor (GDI). In the GTP-bound state the lipid anchor is exposed and tethers the protein to a membrane. Rabs regulate intracellular membrane traffic, and this kind of dynamic, switchable attachment, along with regulated protein-protein binding of peripheral proteins, greatly expands what membranes can do.

Q: The segment of a polypeptide chain that crosses a lipid bilayer almost always adopts one of two regular structures. Why can it not simply be a random coil of hydrophobic amino acids?
![A segment of glycophorin crossing the hydrophobic core of the lipid bilayer as an alpha helix, with hydrophobic side chains in green and hydrophilic ones in yellow at either end](assets/img/fig-10-19.png)
A: Because of the backbone, not the side chains. Membrane-spanning segments are composed largely of amino acids with nonpolar side chains, but the peptide bonds themselves are polar, and inside the bilayer there is no water for them to hydrogen-bond with. They are therefore driven to form hydrogen bonds with one another, and there are two ways to maximize that. The commonest is a regular α helix, which satisfies the bonds along a single stretch of chain. The alternative is for several strands to form a β sheet rolled up into a cylinder, a β barrel. A single-pass protein crosses once, a multipass protein several times.
Tags: membrane-proteins, structure

---

Q+: A transmembrane protein always has a unique orientation in the membrane. What sets it?
A: Two things. The protein is inserted into the ER bilayer asymmetrically during its biosynthesis, and its cytosolic and noncytosolic domains have different functions. Those domains are separated by the membrane-spanning segments, so once inserted there is no way to swap sides, since flipping would drag a hydrophilic domain through the hydrophobic core.
Tags: membrane-proteins

Q: How does a **hydropathy plot** find the membrane-spanning helices in a protein sequence?
![Hydropathy plots of glycophorin with a single positive peak, and of bacteriorhodopsin with seven positive peaks, each corresponding to a membrane-spanning helix](assets/img/fig-10-20.png)
A: For a window of fixed size, usually around 10 to 20 amino acids, the free energy needed to transfer that segment from a nonpolar solvent to water is calculated from its amino acid composition, using data from model compounds. The calculation is repeated starting at each successive amino acid, and the resulting hydropathy index is plotted along the chain. Positive values mean energy is needed to move the segment into water, so the segment is hydrophobic. A stretch of about 20 to 30 highly hydrophobic amino acids is long enough to span the bilayer as an α helix and shows up as a peak. Glycophorin, with one membrane-spanning helix, gives one peak; bacteriorhodopsin, with seven, gives seven. From such plots about 30% of an organism's proteins are estimated to be transmembrane proteins.
Tags: membrane-proteins

---

Q+: The best-known hydropathy scale is Kyte and Doolittle's, from 1982. What window and cutoff is it commonly used with, and why does that window make sense?
A: The commonly quoted recommendation is a window of 19 residues, with a segment whose average hydropathy score exceeds 1.6 flagged as a likely transmembrane helix. The window length is not arbitrary. An α helix rises about 0.15 nm per residue, so 19 residues make a helix about 2.9 nm long, close to the thickness of the hydrophobic core of a bilayer, which is about 3 nm.
Tags: membrane-proteins

---

Q+: Why can a hydropathy plot not find the membrane-spanning strands of a β barrel?
A: Because they do not look hydrophobic when averaged. As an extended β strand, 10 or fewer amino acids are enough to cross the bilayer, which is shorter than the window. And in a strand only every other side chain points outward into the lipid, so only alternate residues need to be hydrophobic, while the others face the inside of the barrel and can be polar. Averaged over a window, such a sequence produces no peak.
Tags: membrane-proteins

---

Q+: Multipass proteins can contain regions that the plot will also miss, even though they are made of helices. What are they?
![The structure of an aquaporin monomer with two short helices, coloured red and blue, each reaching only halfway through the bilayer from opposite sides](assets/img/fig-10-21.png)
A: Regions that fold into the membrane from one side and squeeze between the transmembrane helices without ever contacting the hydrophobic core of the bilayer. Since they touch only other polypeptide, they do not need to satisfy all their hydrogen bonds on their own and can take various secondary structures, including helices that go only partway across. The aquaporin water channel has two such short helices, buried at a protein-protein interface. Regions like these line the pores of water and ion channels and confer their substrate specificity, and they are found only by solving the three-dimensional structure or by aligning the sequence with a homologue whose structure is known. The strong drive to satisfy hydrogen bonds in the absence of water is why most transmembrane helices, by contrast, cross the membrane completely.

Q: In many single-pass membrane proteins, the transmembrane helix contributes nothing to the folding of the domains on either side. What experimental door does that open?
A: The domains can be studied on their own. Cells can be engineered to produce just the cytosolic or just the extracellular domain as a water-soluble molecule, which fold correctly without the membrane anchor. That has been invaluable for determining the structure and function of these domains, especially those of transmembrane receptor proteins.

Q+: So is the transmembrane helix of a single-pass protein just an anchor?
A: Often not. Many single-pass proteins form homodimers or heterodimers held together by noncovalent but strong and highly specific interactions between their two transmembrane helices. The information directing that pairing is in the amino acid sequence of the helices themselves, which means a stretch of hydrophobic residues is not interchangeable with any other stretch of the same length.

Q: A multipass membrane protein can carry polar and even charged amino acids in its transmembrane helices, though these would ordinarily be disfavoured in the bilayer. How?
![A newly synthesized multipass protein with hydrophilic amino acids in its transmembrane helices folding into a bundle that buries them at the interfaces between helices, forming a hydrophilic path across the bilayer](assets/img/fig-10-22.png)
A: By burying them between helices. The helices occupy specific positions set by interactions with their neighbours, and each shields parts of the others from the lipids. In the folded bundle, the outer surface facing the lipid is composed mainly of hydrophobic amino acids, while the interior, which never contacts lipid directly, can hold polar and charged residues. In multi-subunit complexes such as many ion channels, the buried contacts can be between helices of different subunits. This is how a multipass protein builds binding sites and channels for hydrophilic molecules across the membrane, and it probably explains why multipass proteins are the majority of membrane proteins.

Q: A bundle of α helices can be arranged in many ways, but β-barrel membrane proteins are always cylinders. Why, and what varies between them?
![Four beta barrels of increasing size: the 8-stranded OmpA, 12-stranded OMPLA, a 16-stranded porin with loops narrowing its channel, and the 22-stranded FepA filled by a globular domain](assets/img/fig-10-23.png)
A: Every hydrogen bond in the sheet has to be satisfied, and a sheet with an exposed edge would leave the edge strand's bonds unpaired inside the membrane, which is energetically disfavoured. Closing the sheet into a cylinder removes the edges. So all β barrels share the architecture of a cylinder whose outward-facing amino acids are hydrophobic. What varies is the size of the barrel, from as few as 8 strands to as many as 22, and what is inside it, and both are matched to the protein's function.

---

Q+: Where are β-barrel proteins found, and what do they do?
A: They are abundant in the outer membranes of bacteria, mitochondria and chloroplasts. Many are pore-forming: the **porins** are commonly 16-stranded antiparallel barrels with polar side chains lining a water-filled channel and loops protruding in to narrow it, which can make them selective, as maltoporin is for maltose and maltose oligomers in *E. coli*. **FepA**, with 22 strands, transports iron ions across the bacterial outer membrane; a globular domain fills the barrel completely, binds iron, and changes conformation to move it across. Not all are transporters. Smaller barrels such as OmpA (8 strands, a receptor for a bacterial virus) and OMPLA (12 strands, a lipase whose catalytic residues project from the outside of the barrel) are filled with side chains and act as rigid anchors for receptor or enzyme activity.

Q+: A newly identified protein of the outer mitochondrial membrane behaves biochemically as an integral membrane protein, yet its hydropathy plot shows no hydrophobic peak long enough for a transmembrane helix. What is its most likely architecture?
- [x] A β barrel
- [ ] A single-pass α helix
- [ ] A bundle of seven α helices
- [ ] A peripheral protein bound to a transmembrane partner
A: A β barrel. Two clues point the same way: β barrels are abundant in the outer membranes of mitochondria, which descend from bacteria, and their membrane-spanning strands are invisible to hydropathy plots because they are short and only alternately hydrophobic. Any helical transmembrane protein, single-pass or multipass, would have produced peaks. A peripheral protein would not behave as integral, since it could be stripped off with salt or pH changes.

Q: Most multipass proteins in eukaryotic cells are built from α helices rather than β barrels. What does each architecture do well?
A: Helices can slide against one another, which allows the conformational changes needed to open and close ion channels, move solutes across, or transduce signals. In a β barrel, hydrogen bonds tie each strand rigidly to its neighbours, making conformational changes within the barrel wall unlikely. That rigidity is a weakness for moving parts but makes β barrels remarkably stable, and so easier to purify and crystallize, which is one reason they were among the first multipass proteins whose structures were solved.

Q: On a transmembrane protein, where do you find the sugars and the disulfide bonds, and why only there?
![A single-pass transmembrane protein crossing the bilayer as a right-handed alpha helix, with oligosaccharide chains and intrachain and interchain disulfide bonds all on the noncytosolic side, and free sulfhydryl groups in the reducing cytosol](assets/img/fig-10-24.png)
A: Both are on the noncytosolic side, for different reasons. The sugars are added in the lumen of the ER and Golgi apparatus, so oligosaccharide chains are always on the side that faced the lumen, which becomes the outside of the cell. The disulfide bonds reflect chemistry: the cytosol is a reducing environment that keeps cysteine sulfhydryl groups in their reduced –SH form, so S–S bonds rarely form there. On the noncytosolic side they do form, within one chain or between chains, and help stabilize the fold or hold subunits together. Most transmembrane proteins in animal cells are glycosylated.

Q: What is the **glycocalyx**, and what is it made of?
![An electron micrograph of a lymphocyte stained with ruthenium red showing a thick dark carbohydrate layer on its surface, and a diagram of that layer built from glycoproteins, glycolipids, a transmembrane proteoglycan and an adsorbed glycoprotein](assets/img/fig-10-25.png)
A: The carbohydrate-rich zone coating the surface of all eukaryotic cells, also called the cell coat. It consists of oligosaccharide chains attached to membrane proteins (glycoproteins) and lipids (glycolipids), and the long polysaccharide chains of proteoglycans, whose protein core either crosses the bilayer or is held by a GPI anchor. Q: Why are oligosaccharides especially well suited to cell recognition?
A: Because of their diversity and their position. The chains usually have fewer than 15 sugars, but they are often branched and the sugars can be joined by various kinds of covalent linkage, whereas the amino acids of a polypeptide are all joined by identical peptide bonds. Even three sugars can be assembled into hundreds of different trisaccharides. Sitting exposed on the cell surface, these structures are read by plasma-membrane-bound lectins that recognize specific oligosaccharides on glycolipids and glycoproteins and mediate transient cell-cell adhesion, including in lymphocyte recirculation and inflammatory responses.

Q: What does it take to get a membrane protein out of a membrane in soluble form?
A: Only agents that disrupt hydrophobic associations and take the lipid bilayer apart will do it, and the most useful are **detergents**: small amphiphilic molecules of variable structure that are much more soluble in water than in lipids. Their hydrophobic ends bind the hydrophobic regions of a membrane protein, displacing the lipid molecules with a collar of detergent, while their polar ends face the water. The protein comes into solution as a detergent-protein complex, usually with some lipid molecules still attached, and the membrane's own phospholipids are solubilized alongside as lipid-detergent micelles.

Q: What is the **critical micelle concentration** of a detergent?
![Structures of SDS, Triton X-100 and beta-octylglucoside with hydrophobic parts in yellow and hydrophilic parts in orange; a graph of monomer and micelle concentration against total detergent; and an irregular micelle drawn schematically and as a space-filling snapshot](assets/img/fig-10-26.png)
A: The threshold concentration above which detergent molecules aggregate into micelles. Below it the detergent is monomeric in solution. Above it, molecules diffuse rapidly in and out of micelles, and the monomer concentration stays constant however many micelles form. Because of that constant exchange, a micelle's structure is always changing: at any moment most, but not all, of the head groups face the water and most, but not all, of the tails are inside, so hydrophobic regions are transiently exposed. The CMC and the average number of molecules per micelle are characteristic of each detergent but also depend on temperature, pH and salt concentration, which makes detergent solutions complex and hard to study.

Q+: A detergent solution is at twice its CMC. You add more detergent until the total concentration is four times the CMC. What happens?

Q+: Distinguish ionic from nonionic detergents, with an example of each.

Q: SDS will solubilize even the most hydrophobic membrane proteins. Why is it not the detergent of choice for studying what those proteins do?
A: Because strong ionic detergents such as SDS unfold proteins. They disrupt the protein's own internal hydrophobic core as well as its contacts with the bilayer, leaving it denatured and inactive. That is fine for analysis by SDS polyacrylamide-gel electrophoresis, and denatured proteins can still be separated and purified; sometimes removing the SDS even lets the protein renature. For functional work, mild detergents are used instead. They cover the membrane-spanning segments exposed when lipid is removed without unfolding the protein, and with multipass proteins it often helps to keep a thin layer of lipid around the protein during extraction to preserve its activity.

Q: How is a functional membrane protein system **reconstituted** from purified parts, and what can that prove?
![Na+-K+ pumps solubilized with mild detergent, purified, mixed with added phospholipids and detergent, then incorporated into a phospholipid vesicle as the detergent is removed, where they pump sodium and potassium using ATP](assets/img/fig-10-28.png)

Reconstitution shows which proteins are both necessary and sufficient for a particular function, since nothing else is in the vesicle. Q: What is a **nanodisc**, and why is it useful?
![A nanodisc: a small patch of phospholipid bilayer about 5 nm thick wrapped by a belt of high-density lipoprotein protein, shown empty and with a multipass membrane protein embedded](assets/img/fig-10-29.png)
A: A small, uniformly sized patch of lipid bilayer surrounded by a belt of specially designed protein that covers the exposed hydrophobic edge and keeps the patch in solution. The belt protein is derived from high-density lipoproteins (HDLs), whose normal job is keeping lipids soluble for transport in the blood. In the Sligar lab's version the belt is a membrane scaffold protein built from apolipoprotein A-I, and the discs are around 10 nm across, with a very narrow spread of sizes set by the length of the scaffold protein. A membrane protein reconstituted into a nanodisc sits in a native-like lipid environment yet is accessible from both sides of the bilayer, which suits ligand-binding experiments. Nanodiscs can also be analysed by single-particle cryo-electron microscopy, which determines a structure to high resolution without the protein having to crystallize into a regular lattice, often very hard to achieve for membrane proteins. Q: What is the "purple membrane" of *Halobacterium salinarum*, and why did it make bacteriorhodopsin such a good first target for structure determination?

Q+: The first bacteriorhodopsin structure, in 1975, used no three-dimensional crystals at all. What did Henderson and Unwin do, and what did they see?
A: They used electron microscopy of the purple membrane's natural two-dimensional crystals, unstained and imaged at a range of tilts, and combined the views into a three-dimensional map at 7 Å resolution. Q: How does bacteriorhodopsin turn a photon into a pumped proton?
![The seven-helix structure of bacteriorhodopsin crossing a 3 nm hydrophobic core, with retinal linked to a lysine and numbered steps of proton transfer from the cytosolic side to the extracellular side, beside a structure showing many lipids bound to its surface](assets/img/fig-10-31.png)
A: The protein folds into seven closely packed transmembrane α helices and carries a single light-absorbing chromophore, **retinal**, covalently linked to a lysine side chain, which gives it its purple colour. When a photon is absorbed, the retinal changes shape, and the first and key step is the transfer of an H⁺ from the chromophore to the side chain of aspartic acid 85 beside it. That triggers a series of small conformational changes and further H⁺ transfers, in a fixed order, along hydrophilic side chains lining a path through the membrane, until one H⁺ has moved from inside to outside and the protein is back in its starting state. In bright light each molecule pumps several hundred protons per second.

Q+: What does the archaeal cell get out of the proton gradient?
A: Energy. The light-driven transfer builds an H⁺ gradient across the plasma membrane, and a second protein in the same membrane uses that gradient to make ATP. The gradient also drives other energy-requiring processes, so bacteriorhodopsin effectively converts solar energy into a form the whole cell can spend.

Q+: How did bacteriorhodopsin provide some of the most direct evidence that a proton gradient alone can drive ATP synthesis?
A: In 1974 Racker and Stoeckenius reconstituted purple membrane into phospholipid vesicles. In the light the vesicles took up protons, and in the dark they released them. When the oligomycin-sensitive ATPase from mitochondria was incorporated as well, the vesicles made ATP, and did so only when illuminated. The two proteins came from an archaeon and a mitochondrion and had no way of interacting except through the membrane they shared, so the only thing that could link light absorption by one to ATP synthesis by the other was the H⁺ gradient across the vesicle. It is the reconstitution strategy described earlier in the chapter at its most persuasive.

Q+: Retinal turns up in a very different place in our own bodies. Where?
A: In the eye. Retinal is vitamin A in its aldehyde form, and it is identical to the chromophore of rhodopsin in the photoreceptor cells of the vertebrate retina. Rhodopsin is also built from seven transmembrane helices, though it works as a signal transducer rather than a pump.

Q: The high-resolution structure of bacteriorhodopsin shows many lipid molecules bound at specific places on its surface. What does that suggest about the role of lipids around membrane proteins generally?
A: That lipids do more than fill the space around a protein. Interactions with specific lipids are thought to stabilize many membrane proteins, which work best, and sometimes crystallize more readily, when some lipids stay bound during detergent extraction or are added back. The bound layer fills gaps between the jagged hydrophobic surface of the protein and the core of the bilayer, which helps maintain the permeability barrier for ions and other solutes. The lipids bind and dissociate on a millisecond time scale. The book's analogy is that membrane lipids are a two-dimensional solvent: some membrane proteins need particular head groups, just as many enzymes in solution need a particular ion. This specificity may help explain why eukaryotic membranes contain such a variety of lipids.

Q: Bacteriorhodopsin and the G-protein-coupled receptors both consist of seven transmembrane α helices, yet their sequences show no similarity. How is that interpreted?
A: As two evolutionarily distant branches of an ancient protein family, which have diverged so far that the sequence similarity has been erased while the fold survived. Structure is conserved much longer than sequence, so a shared architecture can outlast any detectable relationship at the sequence level. The honest caveat is that without sequence evidence, independent arrival at the same seven-helix solution cannot be ruled out, which is presumably why the book says "probably". Functionally they have gone separate ways: GPCRs, including rhodopsin and many receptors for extracellular signal molecules, respond to a signal by activating a GTP-binding protein inside the cell rather than moving ions.

Q: Membrane proteins, like lipids, do not flip-flop across the bilayer. What kinds of movement are open to them?
A: They can rotate rapidly about an axis perpendicular to the plane of the bilayer, which is **rotational diffusion**, and many can move sideways within the membrane, which is **lateral diffusion**. Q: What was the first direct evidence that plasma membrane proteins move in the plane of the membrane?
![A newly fused mouse-human hybrid cell with green-labelled mouse proteins on one half and red-labelled human proteins on the other, which after time are both spread over the entire surface](assets/img/fig-10-32.png)
A: Frye and Edidin's 1970 experiment. They fused mouse cells with human cells to make hybrid cells, called heterokaryons, and stained them with two antibodies: one labelled green, recognizing mouse plasma membrane proteins, and one labelled red, recognizing human ones. Immediately after fusion the two sets of proteins were confined to the halves of the membrane that had come from each cell. Within about half an hour they had diffused and mixed over the entire cell surface.

Q+: How could Frye and Edidin be sure the mixing was diffusion of existing proteins, and not new proteins being made and inserted everywhere?
A: They tried to block it. The only treatment that worked was lowering the temperature, which reduced the proportion of mixed cells along a sigmoidal curve, as you would expect of a process that depends on the physical state of the membrane. The paper itself reports total mixing in over 90% of the heterokaryons within 40 minutes of fusion, a little longer than the book's half hour.

Q+: Why does it matter to a cell that its membrane proteins can move laterally?

Q: How does **fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP)** measure lateral diffusion?
![Fluorescent membrane proteins bleached by a laser in a small area, with unbleached molecules diffusing back in over time, and a graph of fluorescence in the bleached area dropping sharply then recovering](assets/img/fig-10-33.png)
A: The protein of interest is marked with a fluorescent group, either by binding a fluorescent ligand such as a labelled antibody, or by expressing it fused to a fluorescent protein such as GFP. A laser beam bleaches the fluorescence in a small area of membrane. Unbleached molecules then diffuse into the area and bleached ones diffuse out, and the time course of the recovering fluorescence gives the diffusion coefficient: the greater the coefficient, the faster the recovery. Values vary widely between proteins and cells, because interactions with other proteins impede diffusion to different degrees. Proteins that are least impeded indicate that cell membranes have a viscosity comparable to that of olive oil.
Tags: diffusion, methods

---

Q+: How comparable is "comparable to olive oil"?
A: Only loosely. Olive oil has a viscosity of about 0.06 Pa·s at 25°C, roughly 70 times that of water. A membrane's viscosity is measured as a two-dimensional surface viscosity, and recent measurements on vesicles of simple phosphatidylcholines at 25°C give about 4 to 10 nPa·s·m. Dividing by a bilayer thickness of 4 to 5 nm converts that to an effective three-dimensional viscosity of roughly 1 to 2 Pa·s, which is some 15 to 40 times that of olive oil. Published values for a single lipid also scatter by more than an order of magnitude. The fair reading of the book's comparison is "a viscous oil, not a solid or a gel", rather than a measured match.
Tags: diffusion

Q+: A FRAP experiment shows that fluorescence never returns to a bleached spot. Why can you not conclude that the protein is immobile?
A: Because FRAP follows a large population of molecules over a relatively large area. If nothing migrates into the spot, the proteins might be truly fixed, or they might be moving freely but confined to regions smaller than the bleached area, perhaps by cytoskeletal proteins. The measurement cannot distinguish the two. **Single-particle tracking** gets around this by labelling individual molecules with antibodies coupled to fluorescent dyes or tiny gold particles and following each one by video microscopy, which records the actual path of a single protein over time.

Q: Why is the picture of a membrane as a lipid sea with proteins floating freely in it an oversimplification?
A: Because most cells confine membrane proteins to particular regions of a continuous bilayer. Bacteriorhodopsin in the purple membrane is locked into a two-dimensional crystal, and ATP synthase complexes in the inner mitochondrial membrane associate into long double rows, and such large aggregates diffuse very slowly. Epithelial cells keep different enzymes and transport proteins in their apical and basolateral membranes, and a sperm cell divides a single continuous membrane into several domains. Recognizing that membranes are two-dimensional fluids was a major advance, but what goes on in them is heavily organized.

Q: How does an epithelial cell keep the proteins and lipids of its apical membrane apart from those of its basal and lateral membranes?

Q: What gives a red blood cell its biconcave shape, and why is its cytoskeleton so important to it?
![A scanning electron micrograph of human red blood cells showing their flattened biconcave disc shape](assets/img/fig-10-37.png)
A: Interactions between its plasma membrane proteins and an underlying cytoskeleton made mainly of **spectrin**, a long, thin, flexible rod about 100 nm long. The red blood cell has no nucleus or other organelles, so its plasma membrane is its only membrane, and the spectrin meshwork covering the whole cytosolic face maintains its structural integrity and shape. The result is deformable, which lets the cell withstand the stress on its membrane as it is forced through narrow capillaries. Mice and humans with genetic abnormalities in spectrin are anaemic, with red cells that are spherical instead of concave and fragile, and the anaemia is more severe the greater the spectrin deficiency.

Q+: How is the spectrin meshwork put together and attached to the membrane?

Q: Is the spectrin cytoskeleton a peculiarity of red blood cells?
A: No, but theirs is the simplest version. Most other cells in the body have an analogous, much more elaborate and highly dynamic network beneath the plasma membrane, forming the **cortex** of the cell. Q: The cortical cytoskeleton restricts the diffusion even of membrane proteins that are not attached to it. How?
![Cortical cytoskeletal filaments dividing the plasma membrane into small domains, and a single-particle tracking trace of one protein diffusing within one domain before hopping into neighbouring ones, shown by changes of colour](assets/img/fig-10-39.png)

Q+: When a cell-surface receptor binds its signal molecule it becomes more confined to its corral. Why, and why might that be useful?

Q: Cell membranes take many shapes: flat sheets, narrow tubules, round vesicles, fenestrated sheets and pita-bread-shaped cisternae, often in different regions of one continuous bilayer. What are the three principal ways membrane-bending proteins produce curvature?
![Membrane bending by proteins: a flat bilayer; a protein inserting hydrophobic wedges into one monolayer; a curved protein scaffold binding head groups; and a protein clustering lipids with large head groups, each bending the membrane](assets/img/fig-10-40.png)
A: They bind to specific membrane regions as needed and act in one or more of three ways.
1. **Insertion**: hydrophobic protein domains, such as amphiphilic helices or hydrophobic hairpins, or attached lipid anchors, wedge into one leaflet. Increasing the area of one leaflet only forces the membrane to bend. The proteins shaping the narrow tubules of the ER work like this.
2. **Scaffolding**: a rigid, curved protein surface binds lipid head groups and either deforms the membrane or stabilizes curvature already there. The coat proteins that shape budding transport vesicles are in this class.
3. **Lipid clustering**: a protein gathers lipids of a particular shape in one leaflet. Phosphoinositides, with their large head groups, are wedge-shaped, so accumulating them in one leaflet induces positive curvature, while phospholipases that remove head groups produce inversely shaped lipids that induce negative curvature.

Different bending proteins often collaborate, and they frequently work together with pushing and pulling forces from the cytoskeleton.
Tags: curvature

---

Q+: Why does a lipid's shape decide which way it bends a membrane?
A: The shape is set by the relative cross-sectional areas of the head group and the hydrocarbon tails. A cylinder, with head and tails of similar width, packs flat. A lipid with a large head and narrow tails is a cone, and many of them side by side splay the heads apart and curve the leaflet so that the heads are on the outside of the bend. A lipid with a small head relative to its tails is an inverted cone and curves the leaflet the other way. Clustering either kind in one leaflet only is what produces a net bend in the bilayer.
Tags: curvature

Q: True or false: it is estimated that about 30% of the proteins encoded in an animal's genome are membrane proteins required for a cell to function and interact with its environment.

Q: True or false: all of the common phospholipids (phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylserine and sphingomyelin) carry a positively charged group on their head, but none carries a net positive charge.

Q: True or false: although phospholipid molecules are free to diffuse in the plane of the bilayer, they cannot flip-flop across it unless enzymes called phospholipid translocators are present.

Q: When a lipid bilayer is torn, why does it not seal the edges with a "hemi-micelle" cap instead of closing up?
![A torn lipid bilayer whose two free edges are each sealed with a curved cap of lipids, like half a micelle](assets/img/fig-q10-01.png)
A: Because phospholipids are the wrong shape for it. A hemi-micelle cap requires the lipids at the rim to pack around a very tight curve, with their heads fanned out and their tails converging, which is the packing of cone-shaped molecules in a micelle. Phospholipids, with two tails, are cylinders. Forced around such a tight curve they leave gaps between the head groups through which the hydrophobic tails are exposed to water, so the rim remains energetically costly. Joining the two torn edges back together, or closing the sheet into a sealed compartment, removes the edge altogether, and that is always the lower free-energy state. Transient holes do form, but they are unstable and the lipids rapidly flow back to patch them.

Q: Which one of these changes is energetically favourable and occurs spontaneously in an aqueous solution?

- [ ] Formation of a long tear in a phospholipid bilayer

Q: Hydrophobic solutes are said to "force the adjacent water molecules to reorganize into icelike cages". Why would such a cage be energetically unfavourable relative to pure water?
![A 2-methylpropane molecule surrounded by water molecules hydrogen-bonded to one another in an icelike cage](assets/img/fig-q10-02.png)
- [ ] An icelike cage gives water a higher temperature
- [ ] An icelike cage is less organized than pure water
- [ ] An icelike cage is unstable and easily breaks down
- [x] An icelike cage reduces the entropy of the system
A: It reduces the entropy. The apparent paradox, that water "knows" about a solute it does not interact with, dissolves once you see that the water is not reacting to the solute so much as to the loss of its own options. Water next to a nonpolar surface cannot point hydrogen bonds at it, so to keep as many hydrogen bonds as possible with other water molecules, it has to adopt a more restricted set of orientations. That more ordered arrangement has lower entropy, which raises the free energy. The option that the cage is less organized has it backwards, and whether it is stable is beside the point.

Q: Margarine is made from vegetable oil by a chemical process. Does the process convert saturated fatty acids to unsaturated ones or the reverse?
A: Unsaturated to saturated. Vegetable oils are liquid at room temperature because their fatty acid chains contain *cis*-double bonds, whose kinks stop the chains packing together, the same reason unsaturated lipids keep a bilayer fluid. Margarine has to be solid, so hydrogen is added across the double bonds (hydrogenation), making the chains straight and saturated so that they pack more tightly and melt at a higher temperature. When hydrogenation is only partial, some remaining double bonds can be converted to the *trans* configuration, which is also straight; those are the trans fats.

Q: You want pure inside-out vesicles from the plasma membrane of cultured neuroblastoma cells, but your preparations are contaminated with variable amounts of right-side-out vesicles. A friend suggests passing them over an affinity column of lectin coupled to beads. What is the point?

Q: Glycophorin in the red blood cell membrane normally exists as a homodimer held together entirely by interactions between its transmembrane helices. Transmembrane domains are hydrophobic, so how can they associate so specifically?
A: Hydrophobicity is not what makes the pairing specific, since inside the bilayer the helices are surrounded by hydrocarbon anyway and there is no water to drive them together. Specificity comes from shape complementarity: the side chains on one face of each helix fit against the other so closely that many van der Waals contacts form at once, which only the right partner can achieve. Engelman's group mapped this for glycophorin A by mutating every position: sensitive residues fell on one face of the helix, about every 3.9 residues, and even conservative swaps of a valine broke the dimer. The interface boils down to the motif LIxxGVxxGVxxT, which can make other transmembrane helices dimerize when introduced into them. The two glycines, four residues apart (GxxxG), let the helices approach very closely, and in a later screen of random transmembrane sequences selected for self-association, GxxxG turned up in over 80% of the winners.

Q: Three mechanisms by which cytosolic proteins bend a membrane are shown, each inducing an invagination of the plasma membrane. Could similar cytosolic proteins induce a protrusion instead? Which ones, and how?
![Three cytosolic protein mechanisms bending the plasma membrane toward an invagination: a protein finger inserted into the cytosolic leaflet, lipids bound to the curved surface of a protein, and a protein clustering lipids with large head groups; and a plasma membrane protrusion](assets/img/fig-q10-03.png)
A: The scaffold and the lipid-clustering mechanisms could; the inserted finger could not. In an invagination the cytosolic face is on the outside of the bend, and in a protrusion it is on the inside.
- **Insertion (A)**: pushing a hydrophobic finger into the cytosolic leaflet increases the area of that leaflet only, which can bend the membrane only so that the cytosolic side is convex. To make a protrusion the protein would have to expand the outer leaflet, which a cytosolic protein cannot reach.
- **Scaffold (B)**: the direction of bending is set by the curvature of the protein surface, so a protein whose lipid-binding face curves the other way, convex rather than concave, could bind the inner surface of a protrusion and impose or stabilize it.
- **Lipid clustering (C)**: clustering lipids with large head groups in the cytosolic leaflet bends it with the cytosolic side convex, but clustering inverted-cone lipids with small head groups would bend it the other way. The chapter's own example fits: phospholipases that remove head groups produce inversely shaped lipids that induce negative curvature.
