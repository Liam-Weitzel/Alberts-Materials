Q: What are the three features shared by *all* living cells?
A: 1. They store hereditary information in **DNA**.
2. They transcribe portions of it into **RNA**, and translate some RNA into **protein**.
3. They are bounded by a **plasma membrane** across which nutrients and waste must pass.
Tags: universals, core

---

Q: State the central dogma in its standard arrow form.
A: $$\text{DNA} \xrightarrow{\text{transcription}} \text{RNA} \xrightarrow{\text{translation}} \text{protein}$$
Replication copies DNA to DNA. Reverse transcriptase, in some viruses, runs RNA to DNA.

---

Q: Which of the following are true of **prokaryotes**?
- [x] They lack a membrane-enclosed nucleus
- [x] Their genome is typically a single circular DNA molecule
- [ ] They always lack a cell wall
- [ ] They cannot perform photosynthesis
A: Cyanobacteria are photosynthetic prokaryotes, and most bacteria have a cell wall outside the plasma membrane. The defining negative is the absence of a nucleus.
Tags: prokaryotes

---

Q: The three domains of life are Bacteria, {{Archaea}} and {{Eukaryotes}}.
A: Archaea were split off from Bacteria on the basis of ribosomal RNA sequence comparisons. Their information-processing machinery is closer to that of eukaryotes.

---

Q: Why is **ribosomal RNA** the molecule of choice for building the tree of life?
A: rRNA is present in every organism and does the same job everywhere, so homologous sequences stay alignable across billions of years of divergence. It also changes slowly enough to remain comparable while still accumulating enough substitutions to resolve relationships.

---

Q: Which organism is the workhorse for studying the eukaryotic **cell cycle**?
- [ ] *Escherichia coli*
- [x] *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* (budding yeast)
- [ ] *Arabidopsis thaliana*
- [ ] *Mus musculus*
A: Budding and fission yeast are single-celled eukaryotes that grow fast and are easy to manipulate genetically, and their cell-cycle machinery is conserved in humans. The *cdc* mutants in yeast named most of the cycle's control genes.

---

Q: Roughly how many protein-coding genes does the human genome contain, and how much of the genome do they occupy?
A: About **20,000** protein-coding genes. Coding sequence is only ~1.5% of the 3.2 × 10⁹ base pairs. The rest is regulatory sequence, introns, and repetitive DNA.

---

Q: Define a **homolog**, and distinguish orthologs from paralogs.
A: **Homologs** are genes descended from a common ancestral gene.
- **Orthologs**: homologs in different species, separated by a *speciation* event.
- **Paralogs**: homologs within one genome, separated by a *gene duplication*.

---

Q: What is horizontal gene transfer, and why does it complicate the tree of life?
A: The movement of genetic material between organisms other than by parent-to-offspring inheritance, by conjugation, transduction or transformation. Because different genes in one genome can have different ancestries, the tree is better described as a network for prokaryotes.
Tags: evolution

---

Q: Why do mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own genomes?
A: The **endosymbiont theory**. Both descend from free-living bacteria engulfed by an ancestral eukaryotic cell. The evidence:
- both are bounded by a double membrane
- both divide by fission
- both carry circular DNA read by bacteria-like ribosomes

Most of their original genes have since migrated to the nuclear genome.
Tags: organelles, evolution
