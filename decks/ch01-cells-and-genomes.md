Q: E. B. Wilson wrote that "the key to every biological problem must finally be sought in the cell; for every living organism is, or at some time has been, a cell." What claim about biology is he making?
A: That there is no level of biological explanation below the cell that you can stop at, and no organism that escapes the cell as its starting point. A tissue, an organ or a behaviour is built out of cells and their interactions, so an explanation that never reaches the cell has not reached bottom. The second half of the sentence closes the loophole: even organisms that are not obviously cellular in their adult form, ourselves included, began as a single cell.

---

Q: Heredity is often called the property that separates life from non-living processes. What does that rule out, and why?
A: It rules out processes that generate structure without passing on the peculiarities of the parent to the offspring. A crystal grows, a candle flame consumes free energy and sustains itself, and waves on water organize themselves into repeating forms, but none of them hands down a specification that makes the next one a copy of the one before. A living organism also consumes free energy, as a candle does, but it spends that free energy running a system of chemical reactions specified by hereditary information it received and will pass on.

---

Q: How many species have been described on Earth, and roughly how many cells make up a human body?
A: More than 2 million species described, with many more still undiscovered. A human body is an aggregate of more than $10^{13}$ cells, all of them produced by cell division from a single fertilized egg.

---

Q: The egg cells of a sea urchin, a mouse and the seaweed *Fucus* look superficially alike under a microscope, yet each gives rise to a completely different organism. What does that tell you about where the difference lies?
![Egg cells of a sea urchin, a mouse and the seaweed Fucus shown beside the adult organisms that develop from them](assets/img/fig-1-01.png)
A: The difference cannot lie in the gross appearance of the cell, because that is much the same in all three. It lies in the hereditary information the egg carries. A single cell holds the complete specification for the organism, along with the machinery needed to gather raw materials and build a new cell in its own image, so a difference invisible under the microscope is enough to produce a sea urchin rather than a seaweed.

---

Q: Chapter 1 Page 3 states: "**All** cells on Earth today store their hereditary information in the form of double strand molecules of DNA". Is this true?
A: No. Most living cells contain DNA, but some specialized or mature cells lose it:
- Mature red blood cells push out their nucleus and other internal parts as they mature, leaving more room to carry oxygen.
- Platelets are cell fragments that help stop bleeding; they never had a nucleus or DNA.
- Dead outer skin, hair and nail cells finish their life cycle, lose their internal parts, and end up filled with a hard protein called keratin.

---

Q: A piece of human DNA can be inserted into a bacterium, and a piece of bacterial DNA into a human cell, and in both cases the information is read, interpreted and copied. Why is that such strong evidence for a single common ancestor?
A: Because there was no need for it to work. Cells have been diversifying for more than 3.5 billion years, and information can be stored in many physical forms, as our own dead storage formats show. If the storage medium and the machinery for reading it had arisen more than once, the encoding conventions would almost certainly differ enough that one lineage could not read another's. That every lineage still uses the same medium, the same four letters and compatible reading machinery points to all of them having inherited the arrangement, rather than each inventing it.

---

Q: When is the last universal common ancestral cell thought to have existed?
A: Roughly {{3.5 to 3.8 billion}} years ago.

---

Q: What exactly does the word **genome** refer to?
A: The totality of an organism's hereditary information, embodied in the linear sequence of nucleotides in its DNA. Complete genome sequences are now known for tens of thousands of species, from the smallest bacteria to the largest plants and animals.

---

Q: What is a nucleotide? What does it consist of?
A: A nucleotide is a monomer of a DNA (or RNA) strand. It consists of a sugar (deoxyribose in DNA) with a phosphate group attached, and a **base**: adenine (A), guanine (G), cytosine (C) or thymine (T).
![A nucleotide built from phosphate, sugar and base, and a stretch of double-strand DNA showing A-T and G-C base pairing](assets/img/fig-1-02.png)
Each nucleotide is a specific arrangement of about 35 covalently linked atoms.

---

Q: What is the difference between a monomer and a nucleotide?
A: A monomer is any small building block that links into a chain. A nucleotide is a specific kind of monomer, the one that builds DNA and RNA. Every nucleotide is a monomer, but not every monomer is a nucleotide.

---

Q: The sugar-phosphate units along a DNA strand are asymmetric, which gives the backbone a definite **directionality**, or polarity. Why does that matter?
A: Because it gives the molecular machinery a consistent direction to work in. Replication and transcription always read the sequence in the same order along the strand, in the way written English is always read left to right rather than in whichever direction the reader happens to start. Without an asymmetric backbone there would be no way to specify which end of a sequence is the beginning, and the same stretch of DNA could be read two contradictory ways.

---

Q: What are the base pairing rules?
A: Adenine (A) pairs with thymine (T), and cytosine (C) pairs with guanine (G) in DNA. In RNA, uracil (U) replaces thymine, so adenine pairs with uracil.
Hint: **A**pples go under the tree, **c**ars in the **g**arage.

---

Q: What does **templated polymerization** mean, and what problem does it solve?
A: A new polymer strand is built on a preexisting strand that dictates the order of the subunits. In isolation the four nucleotides could be added in any order, since each links to the next through the part of the molecule they all share, so nothing about the chemistry of the backbone selects a sequence. Base pairing against a template solves that: A binds T and C binds G, so the template holds each incoming monomer in place and determines which of the four goes on next. The same strategy copies DNA and transcribes it into RNA.

---

Q: Within a DNA strand the nucleotides are held together by covalent bonds, while the two strands are held to each other by hydrogen bonds. Why is that difference in bond strength so useful?
A: It lets the two strands come apart without the molecule falling to pieces. The hydrogen bonds between paired bases are individually weak, so the double helix can be unzipped, but the covalent sugar-phosphate backbone of each strand survives intact. Each separated strand can then act as a template for a fresh complementary strand. A structure held together by one kind of bond throughout could not both stay stable and open on demand.

---

Q: What happens during DNA replication, and what is the end result?
![The two strands of a DNA double helix pulled apart, each serving as a template for a new complementary strand, producing two identical daughter double helices](assets/img/fig-1-03.png)
A: The two strands of the double helix are pulled apart and each serves as a template for the synthesis of a new complementary strand. The result is two daughter double helices identical in sequence to the parent. Rates, controls and helper molecules differ between cell types, but the underlying mechanism is the same throughout the living world.

---

Q: State the central dogma in its standard arrow form.
A: $$\text{DNA} \xrightarrow{\text{transcription}} \text{RNA} \xrightarrow{\text{translation}} \text{protein}$$
Replication copies DNA to DNA. Reverse transcriptase, used by some viruses, runs RNA back to DNA.
![From DNA to protein: DNA replicates itself, is transcribed into RNA, and RNA is translated into protein](assets/img/fig-1-04.png)

---

Q: What is transcription?
A: The process by which a segment of DNA sequence is used as a template for synthesizing a shorter, closely related molecule: **ribonucleic acid, or RNA**.

---

Q: Chemically, how does RNA differ from DNA?
A: Two changes. The backbone sugar is ribose rather than deoxyribose, and uracil (U) takes the place of thymine (T). The other three bases are identical, and all four RNA bases pair with their complementary counterparts in DNA, which is what allows RNA monomers to be selected on a DNA template during transcription.

---

Q: The cell's DNA archive is described as fixed and sacrosanct, while RNA transcripts are mass-produced and disposable. What is the point of that asymmetry?
A: The same segment of DNA can be transcribed over and over to yield many identical RNA molecules, so the cell can adjust how much of a given product it makes without ever touching the master copy. Damage or error in a transcript costs the cell one disposable molecule; damage to the DNA is heritable and affects every future transcript. Keeping one protected archive and many expendable working copies separates the job of storing information from the job of using it.

---

Q: Do all RNA transcripts serve as messengers for protein synthesis?
A: No. Most function as messenger RNA (mRNA), guiding protein synthesis according to the instructions stored in DNA. Others are never translated and act directly in the cell, ribosomal RNA and transfer RNA among them.

---

Q: What is translation?
A: The process by which an RNA molecule directs the synthesis of a protein, stringing amino acids together into a polypeptide chain in the order specified by the RNA sequence.

---

Q: How is an amino acid built, and what makes one different from another?
A: Each is built around a core structure that lets it be covalently linked in a standard way to any other amino acid in the set. Attached to that core is a side group of atoms, and that side group is the only thing that differs between the 20 types. The shared core is what makes them interchangeable as chain links; the side group is what gives each one its distinctive chemical character.

---

Q: What determines a protein's three-dimensional structure, and why does the structure matter?
A: The sequence of amino acids in the polypeptide chain determines how it folds. The folded shape is what lets the protein bind other molecules with high specificity, and that binding is the basis of every job proteins do: forming structures, generating movement, sensing signals, and catalysing reactions at reactive sites on their surface. Several billion years of selection have tuned these sequences so that each protein folds into a shape with a useful function.

---

Q: What is a catalyst?
A: A molecule that speeds up a specific chemical reaction without itself being consumed or permanently changed. Everyday speech uses the word for any agent that provokes change; in chemistry it is narrower than that.
![Life as an autocatalytic process: DNA and RNA provide sequence information used to build proteins, and proteins provide the catalytic activity needed to build DNA, RNA and more protein, closing the loop](assets/img/fig-1-05.png)
A cell is this loop made physical: DNA and RNA supply sequence information, proteins supply catalytic activity, and each is needed to make the other.

---

Q+: Are all of a cell's catalysts proteins?
A: No. The great majority are proteins, which we call enzymes, but a few RNA molecules are catalysts too. Those are known as **ribozymes**.

---

Q: A living cell has been described as a self-replicating collection of catalysts. What does that description capture?
A: That the cell's central activity is reproducing its own catalytic machinery. It takes in food, uses it for both building blocks and free energy, makes more catalysts, and discards what is left over as waste. The collection has to collaborate to reproduce the whole collection before the cell divides, which is what makes the behaviour autocatalytic rather than merely chemical.

---

Q: The genetic code is described as neat and rational in some respects but strangely arbitrary in others. What does the phrase **frozen accident** mean here?
A: It refers to features of the code that have no evident reason to be the way they are, and could have been otherwise, but are now effectively unchangeable. They are thought to stem from chance properties of the earliest organisms, passed on by heredity and since built so deeply into every cell that altering them now would be disastrous. The arbitrariness is evidence of history rather than of design, and the near-universality of these arbitrary choices across all life is what makes the accident look frozen.

---

Q: What is a codon?
A: A triplet of nucleotides that codes for a single amino acid.

---

Q: How many naturally occurring amino acids are there, and how many codons are possible?
A: **20** naturally occurring amino acids. With triplet codons there are $4^3 = 64$ possible codons, and all 64 occur in nature. Since there are far more codons than amino acids, several codons necessarily correspond to the same amino acid.

---

Q: What is a **transfer RNA**, and what is an **anticodon**?
A: A tRNA is a small RNA molecule that reads the genetic code. Each type attaches at one end to a specific amino acid, and displays at its other end a sequence of three nucleotides called the anticodon. The anticodon recognizes a particular codon, or a small subset of codons, in mRNA by base-pairing with it. That pairing is what physically connects a nucleotide triplet to the amino acid it specifies.

---

Q: What are ribosomes, and what are they made of?
A: Molecular machines that translate mRNA into protein, built from ribosomal RNA and protein. They can float free in the cytosol or sit docked on the endoplasmic reticulum, depending on whether the protein being made stays in the cytosol or is destined for the membrane system or export.

---

Q: What is a gene?
A: A segment of DNA that corresponds to either a single protein (sometimes to a set of closely related protein variants) or to a single catalytic, regulatory or structural RNA molecule. Special sequences in the DNA act as punctuation, marking where the information for each product begins and ends.

---

Q: What is regulatory DNA?
A: Stretches of DNA interspersed among the protein-coding segments that do not themselves code for anything. Instead they bind proteins that control the rate at which nearby genes are transcribed.

---

Q+: Why does a cell need regulatory DNA at all, rather than simply transcribing everything it can?
A: Because making the full repertoire of proteins and RNAs at full tilt all the time would be wasteful and useless. The cell adjusts transcription and translation of each gene independently, according to need, so the genome dictates not only which proteins the cell can make but when and where they are made. The organization of regulatory DNA varies widely between classes of organisms, while the strategy itself is universal.

---

Q: What does it mean to say a living cell operates far from chemical equilibrium?
A: That its composition is held away from the state its chemistry would settle into if left alone, and holding it there costs a continual supply of free energy from the environment. Equilibrium is not a resting state for a cell but the end of it: when the free energy stops arriving, the cell decays toward equilibrium and dies. Growth and reproduction are on top of that, requiring free energy and raw materials to drive synthetic reactions.

---

Q: Copying genetic information requires free energy. What is the free energy actually paying for?
A: The creation of order against thermal disorder. The nucleotides needed are part of a crowd of molecules moving violently at random, and copying means capturing them, arranging them in an order set by a template, and locking them in a fixed relationship. The bonds that hold them must be strong enough to resist thermal motion, and driving their formation robustly costs free energy, which is released and dissipated as heat as each monomer snaps into its lower-energy bonded state.

---

Q: ATP is needed by every cell for two quite different reasons. What are they?
A: It is a building block for the synthesis of DNA and RNA, since it is itself a phosphorylated nucleotide. It is also the carrier of free energy that drives a very large number of the cell's chemical reactions.

---

Q: What are the three features shared by basically *all* living cells?
A: 1. They store hereditary information in **DNA**.
2. They transcribe portions of it into **RNA**, and translate some of that RNA into **protein**.
3. They are bounded by a **plasma membrane** across which nutrients and waste must pass.
![A phospholipid has a hydrophilic head and hydrophobic tails, and in water these molecules spontaneously form a closed bilayer vesicle with heads facing the water on both sides](assets/img/fig-1-06.png)

---

Q: What does **amphiphilic** mean, and why does it make phospholipids form membranes on their own?
A: An amphiphilic molecule has one part that is hydrophilic and one that is hydrophobic. In water such molecules aggregate spontaneously, packing their hydrophobic portions together to hide them from the water while leaving the hydrophilic portions exposed. For a phospholipid, whose shape suits this arrangement, the result is a bilayer that closes on itself into a sealed vesicle. No machinery is needed to assemble it.

---

Q+: At an interface between oil and water rather than in bulk water, what do phospholipids form instead?
- [ ] A bilayer, as they do in water
- [x] A single sheet, or monolayer, with heads to the water and tails to the oil
- [ ] Sealed vesicles with oil trapped inside
- [ ] Nothing ordered, since neither phase is fully aqueous
A: With oil available, the tails have somewhere hydrophobic to go without needing each other, so a single sheet at the interface satisfies both ends. A bilayer only becomes the answer when water is on both sides.

---

Q: The spontaneous assembly of a lipid bilayer illustrates a general principle about how cells build things. What is that principle?
A: That cells produce molecules whose chemical properties cause them to self-assemble into the structures the cell needs. The information for the structure is carried in the chemistry of the parts rather than applied from outside by an assembler, so a great deal of cellular architecture builds itself once the right molecules are present.

---

Q: If the plasma membrane keeps the cell's contents in, how does anything get across it?
A: Through specialized transport proteins embedded in the membrane, each moving specific molecules from one side to the other. The cell boundary cannot be totally impermeable, since raw materials have to come in and waste has to go out. Some of these transport proteins are so well conserved that family resemblances are recognizable between the most distantly related organisms.

---

Q+: Between the transport proteins in the membrane and the enzymes inside the cell, what does each determine?
A: The transport proteins largely determine which molecules enter the cell at all, while the enzymes inside determine what reactions those molecules undergo once in. Since the DNA specifies both sets of proteins, the genetic information ends up dictating the entire chemistry of the cell, and with it the cell's form and behaviour.

---

Q: What are motor proteins?
A: Enzymes that use the free energy of ATP hydrolysis to produce movement. They pump ions across the plasma membrane, move large assemblies from one site in the cell to another, and propel the cell through its environment. They account for the most obvious of the movements that keep cell contents in perpetual motion.

---

Q: What is Brownian motion?
A: The random thermal motion of molecules, which is especially prominent at cellular scales, since cells can be as small as a micrometer ($10^{-6}$ m) across. It was first observed by Robert Brown in 1827, looking through a microscope at pollen grains immersed in water.

---

Q: Why is Brownian motion important in a cell?
A: Random molecular collisions drive **diffusion**, and diffusion sets the rate at which molecules find each other and react. This is what determines the rate of most biochemical reactions inside a cell.

---

Q: A cell can put random thermal motion to work rather than merely suffering it. How does a **Brownian ratchet** do that?
![A single actin filament abutting the plasma membrane, which fluctuates back and forth; when it fluctuates outward a new actin subunit fills the gap, preventing the membrane from returning](assets/img/fig-1-07.png)
A: By allowing motion in one direction and blocking the return. At the leading edge of a crawling cell, the plasma membrane fluctuates back and forth from thermal motion while an actin filament sits against it. When the membrane happens to fluctuate outward, the gap is wide enough for another actin subunit to add on, and the slightly longer filament then stops the membrane coming back. The fluctuations themselves are undirected; what makes the net movement directional is that only outward excursions get locked in.

---

Q+: Which of these does the Brownian ratchet at the leading edge depend on?
- [x] Random thermal fluctuation of the membrane
- [x] Rapid addition of actin subunits into the gap that opens
- [ ] A motor protein pulling the membrane forward
- [ ] A motor protein pushing the filament into the membrane
A: No motor protein is involved. The energy comes from thermal motion, which is free and undirected, and the directionality comes from actin polymerization making the outward excursions irreversible.

---

Q: Why is there no "gliding" inside a cell?
A: Because at the micrometer scale viscous drag dominates and momentum counts for nothing. An object that size is constantly buffeted by water molecules, and moving it in a direction means continuously overcoming that drag. The moment a motor protein disengages, directed movement stops dead and the complex is left to be knocked about by thermal motion. Nothing coasts.

---

Q: What is the minimum number of genes required for a living cell?
A: Roughly 500. The bacterium *Mycoplasma genitalium*, a parasite of mammals with one of the smallest known genomes, has 525 genes, most of them essential.
![Electron micrograph of a cross section through the small bacterium Mycoplasma genitalium](assets/img/fig-1-08.png)
Its host supplies many small molecules ready-made, but it still has to make all its own DNA, RNA and protein.

---

Q+: *Mycoplasma genitalium* has a genome of 580,070 nucleotide pairs. Roughly how much information is that in computer terms?
A: About 145,018 bytes, which is roughly the text of one chapter of this book. Cell biology is complicated, but the size of the specification is not beyond imagining.

---

Q+: Of the 525 genes in *Mycoplasma genitalium*, how are they divided up?
A: 43 code for transfer RNAs, ribosomal RNAs and other noncoding RNAs, leaving 482 protein-coding genes. Of those, 154 handle replication, transcription, translation and related work on DNA, RNA and protein; 98 build the membrane and surface structures; 46 transport nutrients and other molecules across the plasma membrane; and 71 run energy conversion and the synthesis and breakdown of small molecules.

Q: Why is ribosomal RNA the molecule of choice for building the tree of life?
A: rRNA is present in every organism and does the same job everywhere, so homologous sequences stay alignable across billions of years of divergence. It also changes slowly enough to stay comparable, while still accumulating enough substitutions to resolve relationships. The more similar the rRNA sequences of two species, the more recently they diverged.
![A stretch of ribosomal RNA gene sequence aligned across a human, an archaeon and a bacterium, with most positions identical, showing how little this gene has changed since the last universal common ancestor](assets/img/fig-1-17.png)

---

Q+: Once a rough tree has been built from rRNA, why not stop there?
A: Because rRNA is too conserved to separate close relatives. Its value is that it can be recognized in every genome, which is exactly what is needed to place the deepest branches, but species that diverged recently will have nearly identical rRNA. For those, many additional sequences that cannot be identified across all of life, and so are useless for the global tree, resolve relationships accurately.

---

Q: The three domains of life are Bacteria, {{Archaea}} and {{Eukaryotes}}.
A: Archaea were split off from Bacteria on the basis of ribosomal RNA sequence comparisons. Their information-processing machinery is closer to that of eukaryotes.
![A global tree of life based on genome comparisons, with branch lengths proportional to genome differences, showing bacteria, archaea and eukaryotes](assets/img/fig-1-09.png)

---

Q+: On this tree, which domain shows the greatest evolutionary diversity, and why?
- [x] Bacteria
- [ ] Archaea
- [ ] Eukaryotes
- [ ] All three are roughly equal
A: Bacteria, by a wide margin. Their diversity reflects how much earlier they appeared in the planet's history, and it matches their ability to colonize nearly every ecological niche. So many new bacterial species are now being found by sequencing environmental samples that simply naming them has become a problem. Eukaryotes, the focus of most of this book, are only a small slice of global diversity.

---

Q: Where does the word **eukaryote** come from, and what did the distinction between eukaryotes and prokaryotes originally rest on?
A: From the Greek *eu*, meaning "well" or "truly", and *karyon*, meaning "kernel" or "nucleus", so "truly nucleated". The split was made in the early twentieth century on what could be seen with a simple light microscope: organisms whose DNA sat inside a membrane-bound nucleus were eukaryotes, and those without were prokaryotes. We now know that the prokaryotes are not one group but two of the three domains, the bacteria and the archaea.

---

Q: Besides having a nucleus, how do eukaryotic cells typically differ from bacteria and archaea?
A: They are usually much larger, and they contain a variety of membrane-bound organelles that prokaryotes lack. Their genomes also tend to be much bigger: more than 20,000 genes for humans and for corals, against 4000 to 6000 genes for a typical bacterium or archaeon.

---

Q: Which of the following types of cells can be found in the human body?
- [x] Eukaryotes
- [ ] Prokaryotes
- [ ] Archaea
A: All cells in the human body are eukaryotic: they contain a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. Prokaryotes and Archaea are single-celled organisms, like bacteria, and are not part of the human body's own cellular makeup.

---

Q: What is the typical size and shape of a bacterium, and what is the most extreme exception?
![Shapes and sizes of some bacteria, including spherical, rod-shaped, spiral and the smallest cells](assets/img/fig-1-10.png)
A: Most are a few micrometers in linear dimension, and typically spherical or rod-shaped. The extreme exception is the cigar-shaped *Epulopiscium fishelsoni*, which lives in the gut of a surgeonfish and can reach 600 μm in length, big enough to see without a microscope.

---

Q: What lies under a bacterium's cell wall, and how is the interior organized?
![Drawing of Vibrio cholerae showing its internal organization and flagellum, beside an electron micrograph of a longitudinal section through E. coli](assets/img/fig-1-11.png)
A: Beneath the tough protective cell wall, a plasma membrane encloses a single cytoplasmic compartment, the cytoplasm, holding DNA, RNA, proteins and the small molecules needed for life. There are no membrane-bound compartments inside, but the interior is nevertheless highly organized, even though that organization is hard to make out in a light microscope.

---

Q+: What distinguishes a **Gram negative** bacterium from a **Gram positive** one?
A: Whether it has an outer membrane in addition to the inner plasma membrane. *Vibrio cholerae* and *E. coli* are both Gram negative and have both membranes. Many bacterial species lack the outer membrane, and those are classified as Gram positive.

---

Q+: What does the flagellum of *Vibrio cholerae* do, and what disease does the organism cause?
A: The flagellum is a helical appendage at one end that rotates like a propeller to drive the cell forward. *V. cholerae* infects the human small intestine and causes cholera, whose severe diarrhoea kills more than 100,000 people a year worldwide.

---

Q: How varied are the biochemical capabilities of bacteria?
A: Extraordinarily varied, matching the diversity of their genomes. Some species can use virtually any organic molecule as food, from sugars and amino acids to hydrocarbons, including methane. Others harvest light energy, and some of those carry out photosynthesis and release oxygen as a by-product, as plants do. Still others live on a purely inorganic diet, taking carbon from CO₂ and drawing energy from environmental chemicals such as H₂, Fe²⁺, H₂S and elemental sulfur.

---

Q: In a filament of the photosynthetic bacterium *Anabaena cylindrica*, not every cell does the same job. What division of labour appears?
![Light micrograph of Anabaena cylindrica showing chains of cells, with vegetative, heterocyst and spore cells labelled](assets/img/fig-1-12.png)
A: The cells form long chains in which most carry out photosynthesis, capturing CO₂ and building carbon into organic compounds. Others become specialized for fixing nitrogen from N₂, and still others develop into spores that survive unfavourable conditions. Some prokaryotes also have intracellular membranes, so colonies like this resemble simple multicellular organisms rather more than the usual picture of bacteria suggests.

---

Q: How does the bacterium *Beggiatoa* make a living, and what visible trace does that leave?
![Light micrograph of Beggiatoa showing yellow sulfur deposits inside the cells](assets/img/fig-1-13.png)
A: It lives in sulfurous environments and gets its energy by oxidizing H₂S, which lets it fix carbon even in complete darkness. The sulfur left over is visible as yellow deposits inside the cells.

---

Q: Living biomass on Earth comes to roughly 550 gigatons of carbon. How is it distributed?
![Area chart of global biomass by taxon: plants 450 Gt C, bacteria 70, archaea 7, protists 4, fungi 12, animals 2, viruses 0.2](assets/img/fig-1-14.png)
A: Plants dominate at 450 Gt C, about 80% of the total. Bacteria account for 70 Gt C and archaea for 7 Gt C, both mainly in soil and Earth's crust. Animals come to only 2 Gt C, about 0.4% of the total, and viruses to 0.2 Gt C. Terrestrial biomass is 100 times greater than that in the oceans, even though most of the animal mass is marine.

---

Q+: How does human biomass compare with that of wild animals?
A: Human biomass is 10 times greater than that of all measurable wild animals put together. Human biomass continues to rise while that of wild animals falls, largely because of human activity.

---

Q: Distinguish **organotrophic**, **phototrophic** and **lithotrophic** organisms.
A: They differ in where the free energy comes from. Organotrophs feed on other living things or the organic chemicals they produce, which covers animals, fungi and many gut bacteria. Phototrophs harvest the energy of sunlight. Lithotrophs, whose name means "rock feeding", capture energy from energy-rich systems of inorganic chemicals that are far from chemical equilibrium. Phototrophs and lithotrophs are the primary energy converters, and organotrophs like us could not exist without them.

---

Q+: In what sense is an aerobic lithotroph feeding on the products of past life?
A: Because it uses molecular oxygen from the environment, and atmospheric O₂ is itself a by-product of the biosynthetic activity of phototrophic organisms. Many other lithotrophs live anaerobically, in places with little or no oxygen, which is closer to the conditions of early Earth before oxygen accumulated.

---

Q: What drives a hot hydrothermal vent, and what chemistry does it deliver?
![Cross section of a hydrothermal vent showing seawater percolating down to hot basalt and being driven back up through a mineral chimney](assets/img/fig-1-15.png)
A: They sit where the ocean floor is spreading and new crust forms from material upwelling from Earth's interior. Seawater percolates down, is heated by the molten rock, and is driven back up as a submarine geyser carrying chemicals leached from the hot rock. A typical cocktail includes H₂S, H₂, CO, Mn²⁺, Fe²⁺, Ni²⁺, CH₄, NH₄⁺ and phosphorus compounds. Minerals precipitate as the water cools and build a chimney a few meters tall, with a temperature gradient from more than 350°C at the core down to 2 to 3°C in the surrounding ocean.

---

Q+: Different organisms live at different heights on the chimney. Why?
A: Because the temperature gradient sorts them. Close to the vent, at temperatures up to about 120°C, lithotrophic bacteria and archaea live directly on geochemical energy. Further out, where it is cooler, invertebrate animals live by feeding on those microorganisms. The whole ecosystem is analogous to the world of plants and animals, but powered by geochemical energy rather than light.

---

Q: The giant tube worm *Riftia pachyptila* grows to two meters near hydrothermal vents and has no mouth, no gut and no anus. How does it feed?
![Photograph of giant tube worms Riftia pachyptila living near a vent 2500 meters deep](assets/img/fig-1-16.png)
A: It does not feed in the ordinary sense. Specialized organs in the worm house enormous numbers of symbiotic sulfur-oxidizing bacteria, which harvest geochemical energy and supply nourishment to the host directly. The worms are thought to have evolved from more conventional animals and to have become secondarily adapted to vent life, which is why they have lost a digestive tract their ancestors had.

---

Q: DNA, RNA and protein are built from only six elements. Which are they, and why is their abundance not the whole story?
A: Hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, sulfur and phosphorus. All six are plentiful in rocks, water and the atmosphere, but abundance is not the constraint. They are largely present in chemical forms that cannot be incorporated into biological molecules directly, and atmospheric N₂ and CO₂ in particular are extremely unreactive, so a large amount of free energy is needed to convert them into usable organic compounds.

---

Q: What does it mean to **fix** nitrogen or carbon dioxide, and who does it?
A: Fixing means driving the energetically expensive reactions that convert unreactive inorganic N₂ or CO₂ into organic compounds that living things can build with. Many cells lack the machinery entirely and depend on others. Animals depend on plants, directly or indirectly, for carbon-containing and nitrogen-containing compounds. Plants can fix carbon dioxide but cannot fix atmospheric nitrogen, so they in turn depend on nitrogen-fixing bacteria. Plants of the pea family house symbiotic nitrogen-fixing bacteria in root nodules.

---

Q: What is the difference between an **endosymbiotic** and an **ectosymbiotic** association?
A: In an ectosymbiotic association the partners remain separate organisms living in close contact. In an endosymbiotic one they have gone further and lost their separate identities, joining into a single composite cell. Cells differ so widely in basic biochemistry that partners with complementary needs and capabilities have repeatedly formed close associations, and some of those have gone all the way to endosymbiosis.

---

Q: What is a mutation?
A: A permanent change in the DNA sequence of a gene, arising from random accidents and errors during the storage and copying of genetic information. Because of them, the genomes of a cell's two daughters are often not quite identical to each other or to the parent's.

---

Q+: A mutation can turn out three ways. What happens in each case?
A: A change for the better is rare, and tends to be perpetuated because the altered cell is more likely to survive and reproduce. A neutral change causes no significant difference in the cell's prospects, and whether it spreads is a matter of chance in the competition for limited resources. A change that causes serious damage, by disrupting the coding sequence of a key protein or RNA for instance, leads nowhere, because the cell that suffers it dies without progeny. Endless repetition of this cycle of mutation and natural selection is what makes organisms evolve.

---

Q: Why are some genes 'highly conserved' across species?
A: A stretch of DNA with no coding or regulatory role is free to drift, changing at whatever rate random errors accumulate. A gene coding for a highly optimized, essential protein or RNA cannot drift the same way: when a mutation breaks it, the cell carrying that mutation is usually disabled and eliminated.
Genes of this second kind are highly conserved. Over 3.5 billion years of evolution many DNA sequences have changed beyond recognition, but the most highly conserved genes are still recognizable across all living species.

---

Q+: In the aligned rRNA sequences from an archaeon, a bacterium and a human, what do the red lines and the single black dot mark?
A: The red vertical lines mark sites where the nucleotide is identical between the species being compared. The black dot halfway along the *E. coli* sequence marks a site where a nucleotide was either deleted in the bacterial lineage or inserted in the other two. The segment shown is part of the gene for the smaller of the two main rRNA molecules, which runs to about 1500 to 1900 nucleotides in full depending on the species.

---

Q: How are new genes generated from preexisting genes?
A: Four main routes: intragenic mutation, gene duplication, DNA segment shuffling, and horizontal (intercellular) DNA transfer.
![Four modes of genetic innovation: intragenic mutation, gene duplication, DNA segment shuffling, and horizontal DNA transfer](assets/img/fig-1-18.png)
The raw material is always sequence that already exists, since there is no natural mechanism for making long stretches of new random DNA. In that sense no gene is ever entirely new.

---

Q: What is an intragenic mutation?
A: A random change to an existing gene's DNA sequence, arising from errors made during DNA replication or DNA repair.

---

Q: What is gene duplication?
A: The accidental duplication of an existing gene, producing a pair of initially identical genes within a single cell. The two copies can then diverge over the course of evolution.

---

Q: What is DNA segment shuffling?
A: Two or more existing genes break and rejoin, producing a hybrid gene made of segments that originally belonged to separate genes.

---

Q: What is horizontal (intercellular) DNA transfer?
A: A piece of DNA moves from the genome of one cell into that of another, including across species. This contrasts with the usual vertical transfer of genetic information from parent to offspring.

---

Q+: What special form does horizontal transfer take when two species enter a permanent symbiosis?
A: Genes from one partner cell can be transferred into the genome of the other. This is the route by which mitochondria and chloroplasts lost most of their original genes to the nuclear genome of their host.

---

Q: How does gene duplication give rise to 'gene families'?
A: Once a gene has been duplicated, the two copies can independently pick up mutations and specialize for different functions, while remaining in the same cell and its descendants. Repeated rounds of duplication and divergence over many millions of years turn one gene into a family of related genes within a single genome.

---

Q+: In the bacterium *Bacillus subtilis*, how much of the genome consists of such families, and what is the largest one?
![Pie chart of gene families in the Bacillus subtilis genome, showing how many genes fall into families of various sizes](assets/img/fig-1-19.png)
A: 47% of its genes have one or more obvious relatives. The largest family has 77 members, all coding for varieties of a class of membrane transport proteins called ABC transporters, which are found in all three domains of life.

---

Q: Define a **homolog**, and distinguish orthologs from paralogs.
A: **Homologs** are genes descended from a common ancestral gene.
- **Orthologs**: homologs in different species, separated by a *speciation* event. They are likely to have kept corresponding functions in the two sister species.
- **Paralogs**: homologs within one genome, separated by a *gene duplication*. They are likely to have diverged in function.
![Orthologs arise from speciation, paralogs arise from gene duplication within one genome](assets/img/fig-1-20.png)

---

Q: Why does finding a gene's relatives make it easier to work out what the gene does?
A: Because gene sequence determines gene function, so a newly sequenced gene is likely to do something similar to its homologs. Searching sequence databases for relatives will often turn up one whose function has already been pinned down experimentally, usually in a model organism, and that gives a good first guess at the new gene's function. A great deal about an organism's biology can be worked out this way from genome sequence alone.

---

Q: Roughly how many gene families are shared across all three domains of life, and what do most of them do?
A: More than 200. One systematic analysis found 264 ancient conserved families, each assignable a function from its best-characterized member. The largest groups are involved in translation and in amino acid metabolism and transport, which fits their presence in the last universal common ancestor.
Tags: genome-evolution

---

Q+: For that analysis, what counted as a "universal" gene family?
A: One represented in the genomes of at least two diverse archaea (*Archaeoglobus fulgidus* and *Aeropyrum pernix*), two evolutionarily distant bacteria (*Escherichia coli* and *Bacillus subtilis*), and one eukaryote (the yeast *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*). The bar is deliberately not "present in every genome", because that would be too strict to be useful.

---

Q+: Why is the ancestral gene set so hard to pin down exactly?
A: Three things blur it. Individual species have lost ancestral genes, so absence proves little. Other genes were acquired by horizontal transfer and are not truly ancestral despite being widespread. And over two or three billion years some genes that really were shared have mutated beyond recognition. Any tally of conserved families is therefore a rough sketch of the common inheritance rather than a reconstruction of it.
Tags: genome-evolution

---

Q: Roughly how much bigger is a eukaryotic cell than a prokaryotic one?
A: Typically 10 to 30 times larger in linear dimension, which works out to roughly 1000 to 10,000 times larger in volume.

---

Q: Label the major features of a eukaryotic cell.
A: ![Simplified map of a eukaryotic cell showing the nucleus, rough and smooth ER, Golgi apparatus, mitochondria, lysosome, peroxisome and free ribosomes](assets/img/fig-1-21.png)
The nucleus holds the DNA; the nucleolus inside it is where ribosome assembly starts. Rough ER (ribosome-studded) and smooth ER handle protein and lipid synthesis. The Golgi apparatus modifies and sorts what comes from the ER. Mitochondria supply ATP. Lysosomes digest waste, peroxisomes handle oxidative reactions, and free ribosomes in the cytosol translate proteins that stay in the cytosol.
![Membrane-enclosed organelles distributed through a eukaryotic cell: nucleus, mitochondrion, lysosome, peroxisome, Golgi apparatus, endoplasmic reticulum, endosome and transport vesicles, all sitting in the cytosol](assets/img/fig-1-24.png)
An endosome is where material taken in by endocytosis first arrives, before it's sorted onward to a lysosome or elsewhere.
Tags: organelles

---

Q+: The diagram is tidy in one respect that the real cell is not. Which?
A: Crowding. The interior of a cell is far more densely packed than any simplified drawing shows, and the empty-looking space between organelles is in reality a concentrated solution of macromolecules.

---

Q: What is the **nuclear envelope**, and how does anything cross it?
A: A double layer of membrane surrounding the nucleus, separating the nuclear DNA from the cytoplasm. It is perforated by **nuclear pores**, channels formed by protein complexes that handle two-way traffic of large molecules between nucleus and cytoplasm.

---

Q: What are chromosomes, and why are they usually invisible?
A: The long DNA polymers of the nucleus packaged together with proteins. In a light microscope they only become visible when they condense in preparation for cell division; the rest of the time they are too diffuse to resolve.

---

Q: What is the cytoskeleton made of, and what does it do?
A: Three types of protein filament: actin filaments, microtubules and intermediate filaments. Together with the many proteins that attach to them they form a network of girders, ropes and motors that gives the cell mechanical strength. When the cell divides, the cytoskeleton reorganizes, pulls the replicated chromosomes apart and distributes them equally to the two daughter cells. In animal cells and free-living single-cell eukaryotes it also controls cell shape and drives movement.

---

Q+: What do plant cells have that the standard animal-cell diagram lacks, and what does it cost them?
A: Chloroplasts, and a rigid external cell wall containing cellulose outside the plasma membrane. The wall is what makes plant cells largely immobile, since a cell encased in a rigid box cannot crawl or change shape the way an animal cell does.

---

Q: What is phagocytosis?
A: A specialized, large-scale form of endocytosis in which a cell engulfs whole particles or other cells by extending its plasma membrane around them. In animals it is mostly carried out by dedicated cells such as macrophages and neutrophils.
![Electron micrograph of a mammalian neutrophil extending surface protrusions to engulf a dividing bacterium](assets/img/fig-1-22.png)
Eukaryotic cells can do this because they lack the tough cell wall of bacteria and archaea, which leaves them free to change shape rapidly.

---

Q: What does the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) do, and how do rough and smooth ER differ?
A: The ER is a network of membrane-bound tubes and sacs continuous with the nuclear envelope, and the most extensive organelle in the cell. It is where most cell membrane components are made, along with material destined for secretion.
- **Rough ER** is studded with ribosomes; it synthesizes and folds proteins destined for secretion, the membrane system, or organelles.
- **Smooth ER** has no ribosomes; it synthesizes lipids, detoxifies certain molecules, and stores calcium.

---

Q: What does the Golgi apparatus do?
A: It receives proteins and lipids from the ER and modifies, sorts, and packages them into vesicles, directed either to secretion at the plasma membrane or to other organelles.

---

Q: What are lysosomes?
A: Small, irregularly shaped membrane-bound organelles filled with hydrolytic (digestive) enzymes and kept at low pH. They are where intracellular digestion happens: breaking down waste material, worn-out organelles, and anything taken in by endocytosis.

---

Q: What are peroxisomes?
A: Small membrane-bound vesicles that run oxidative reactions, including fatty acid breakdown, and use hydrogen peroxide to inactivate toxic molecules. The peroxisome's own catalase then disposes of the hydrogen peroxide.

---

Q: How does material move between the single-membrane organelles, and between the cell and its surroundings?
A: By small transport vesicles that pinch off the membrane of one organelle and fuse with the membrane of another, so the exchange is continual. The same vesicle traffic operates at the cell surface: portions of the plasma membrane pinch inward to carry external material in by **endocytosis**, and vesicles from inside fuse with the plasma membrane to release their contents outward by **exocytosis**.
![Endocytosis pinches material into the cell as a vesicle, exocytosis fuses a vesicle with the membrane to release material outside](assets/img/fig-1-23.png)

---

Q: What is endocytosis?
A: The general process by which a cell takes in material from outside by wrapping plasma membrane around it and pinching off an internal vesicle. Phagocytosis (engulfing large particles) and pinocytosis (taking in fluid and dissolved molecules) are both forms of it. Endocytosed material is delivered first to endosomes.

---

Q: What is exocytosis?
A: The process by which a cell exports material: a membrane-bound vesicle travels to the plasma membrane and fuses with it, releasing its contents outside the cell.

---

Q: Which organelles are enclosed by **two** membranes rather than one?
A: The nucleus, mitochondria, and in plant cells and algae, chloroplasts. Most other organelles are enclosed by a single membrane.

---

Q: What do mitochondria do, and what does their internal structure look like?
A: They take up oxygen and harness energy from the oxidation of food molecules such as sugars and fats to produce most of the cell's ATP.
![Electron micrograph of a mitochondrion in cross section beside a cut-away drawing showing the smooth outer membrane and convoluted inner membrane](assets/img/fig-1-25.png)
The outer membrane is smooth while the inner one is heavily convoluted, and it is that inner membrane which houses the proteins that generate ATP.

---

Q: What do chloroplasts do?
A: Found in plant cells and algae, they carry out photosynthesis, using the energy of sunlight to synthesize carbohydrates from atmospheric CO₂ and water, and delivering those energy-rich products to the host cell as food.
![Light micrograph of a leaf cell showing green chloroplasts, beside a drawing of one chloroplast with its highly folded internal membranes](assets/img/fig-1-28.png)
The internal membranes are folded into an elaborate system that holds the chlorophyll molecules which absorb the light.

---

Q: Roughly how much of the cytoplasm do membrane-enclosed organelles take up, and what is the rest?
A: In many eukaryotic cells they occupy roughly half of it. The surrounding fluid is the **cytosol**, which holds the ribosomes and is where most of the cell's other metabolic reactions take place.

---

Q: What is the difference between the **cytoplasm** and the **cytosol**?
A: The cytoplasm is everything inside the plasma membrane and outside the nucleus: the cytosol plus all the organelles suspended in it. The cytosol is just the aqueous, gel-like fluid itself.

---

Q: Not every organelle has a membrane. How are the membraneless ones held together?
A: As **biomolecular condensates**, in which multiple components that work together are held in close proximity without any membrane separating them from the cytosol. The nucleolus, where ribosome assembly takes place, is an example.

---

Q: When did the first eukaryotic cell arise, and out of what?
A: About 2 billion years ago, in a world that had held only prokaryotes for more than 1.5 billion years, when an archaeal cell and a bacterial cell merged.

---

Q: What is the evidence that mitochondria descend from a captured bacterium?
A: They are similar in size to small bacteria and reproduce by dividing, as bacteria do. They carry their own DNA, with genes resembling bacterial genes, and their own ribosomes and translation factors, which also resemble the bacterial versions. The captured cell is thought to have been an aerobic bacterium, one that harvested energy by combining electrons from food with oxygen gas, taken up by an anaerobic host, after which the two evolved an endosymbiotic relationship inside a common cytoplasm.
Tags: organelles

---

Q+: Why is the capturing cell thought to have been an archaeon rather than a bacterium?
A: Because the genomes of present-day archaea encode many proteins characteristic of eukaryotic cells. The archaea with the most eukaryote-like genes belong to the **Asgard** lineage, which was first identified purely from DNA fragments recovered from the seabed.

---

Q: The first Asgard archaeon to be grown in culture took a heroic effort to isolate. What made it so difficult, and what did it turn out to look like?
![Scanning electron micrograph of an Asgard archaeon in culture showing long branching membranous protrusions and associated ectosymbionts](assets/img/fig-1-26.png)
A: It grows extraordinarily slowly, doubling only about every 20 days, against roughly half an hour for *E. coli*. The team kept deep marine sediment under anaerobic conditions in a bioreactor for more than 2000 days to mimic the seabed, then tried to culture samples under a range of conditions, and only after 12 years and repeated subculturing did they succeed. The organism looked like no other prokaryote, extending long branched and unbranched membranous protrusions and blebs, and it lived in an ectosymbiotic relationship with a bacterium and another archaeon that came out with it.

---

Q+: How does that organism suggest a route to the first eukaryotic cell?
![Model of early eukaryotic evolution in which archaeal surface protrusions surround an ectosymbiotic bacterium, fuse, and trap it as an endosymbiont](assets/img/fig-1-27.png)
A: In the model, the surface protrusions of an ancient Asgard archaeon expanded around an ectosymbiotic aerobic bacterium and then fused with one another, trapping it as an endosymbiont enclosed by a membrane derived from the archaeon. The endosymbiont later escaped that enclosing membrane into the cytosol, where it evolved into the mitochondrion, keeping its own DNA and membranes. The internal archaeal membranes produced by all that protrusion and fusion are postulated to have gone on to form the nucleus and the single-membrane organelles such as the ER.

---

Q: How did chloroplasts arise, and how does that differ from the origin of mitochondria?
A: A photosynthetic bacterium was captured by an ancient eukaryotic cell that **already had mitochondria**, possibly by phagocytosis. So the chloroplast is a later, second acquisition rather than part of the original merger. Like mitochondria, chloroplasts are enclosed in double membranes, carry their own circular genomes, and reproduce by dividing.

---

Q: Animal cells, plant cells and fungal cells each make a living in a different way. How does the book frame the contrast?
A: Animal cells and many single-celled eukaryotes are hunters, capturing other cells and eating them by phagocytosis. A plant cell equipped with chloroplasts has no need to chase prey, since it is fed by its captive chloroplasts, and it has wrapped itself in a tough cell wall and given up rapid shape change, so it has traded hunting for farming. Fungal cells have mitochondria but no chloroplasts, and also have a tough wall that stops them engulfing anything, so they scavenge instead: they feed on nutrients other cells secrete or release on death, often digesting the material outside themselves by secreting enzymes into their surroundings.

---

Q: In what sense do eukaryotic genomes have a hybrid origin?
A: The information-processing genes (DNA replication, transcription, translation) trace back to an archaeal-like host lineage, while many metabolic genes trace back to the bacterial endosymbiont that became the mitochondrion. A eukaryotic genome is a mosaic built from both lineages rather than a clean descendant of either one alone.
![Eukaryotic cells descend from an archaeal host that acquired a bacterial endosymbiont which became the mitochondrion, and later, in the plant lineage, a photosynthetic bacterium that became the chloroplast](assets/img/fig-1-29.png)
Tags: genome-evolution

---

Q+: Mitochondria are essentially the same in plants, animals and fungi. What does that tell you about when they were acquired?
A: That the acquisition happened before those three lineages diverged, about 1.5 billion years ago. If mitochondria had been picked up separately by each lineage they would differ far more between them.
Tags: genome-evolution

---

Q: How big is the human mitochondrial genome, and what does it still encode?
A: Only 16,569 nucleotide pairs, coding for 13 proteins plus a set of 24 RNAs involved in protein synthesis. Both the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes are cut-down versions of the bacterial genomes they came from.

---

Q+: Where did the missing genes go?
A: Most were not lost but moved into the nucleus of the host cell. Animal nuclear DNA carries many genes for proteins that work inside mitochondria, and plant and algal nuclear DNA carries many for proteins required in chloroplasts. In both cases the sequences of those nuclear genes still show clear evidence of their bacterial origin.
Tags: genome-evolution

---

Q: Are eukaryotic genomes generally bigger than prokaryotic ones? What accounts for the difference?
A: Yes, typically by hundreds of times. Natural selection has favoured small genomes in mitochondria, but nuclear genomes seem to have been free to enlarge, perhaps because the eukaryotic way of life rewards large cells, since a predatory cell generally needs to be bigger than its prey and cell size rises with genome size. Most of the extra is not extra genes but noncoding DNA.
![Genome sizes compared on a log scale](assets/img/fig-1-30.png)
Tags: genome-evolution

---

Q+: Put numbers on that. How does the human genome compare with *E. coli*?
A: The human genome has about 700 times as many nucleotide pairs, but only about 4.5 times as many protein-coding genes. The difference is how much of it codes for anything: roughly 98.5% of the human genome is noncoding, against 11% in *E. coli*. Across eukaryotes generally the pattern is 3 to 8 times as many protein-coding genes as prokaryotes and often a thousand times more noncoding DNA.
Tags: genome-evolution

---

Q+: Closely related organisms can differ hugely in genome size while carrying similar numbers of protein-coding genes. What does that imply about genome size as a measure?
A: That genome size is not a measure of gene content, and by extension not a measure of organismal complexity. The variation between close relatives is in how much noncoding and highly repeated DNA has accumulated, which selection has evidently not been pruning hard. This mismatch between genome size and complexity is sometimes called the C-value paradox.
Tags: genome-evolution

---

Q: Much noncoding DNA is described as dispensable junk, retained like a mass of old papers. What does the puffer fish contribute to that argument?
A: It shows the junk really is largely dispensable. Puffer fish have somehow shed large quantities of noncoding DNA, and yet they are similar in structure, behaviour and fitness to relatives carrying vastly more of it. The retention elsewhere looks like the path of least resistance: when there is little pressure to keep an archive small, keeping everything is easier than sorting out what is worth having. Even so, the puffer fish genome still has more noncoding DNA than coding DNA, and some noncoding DNA certainly matters, above all the part that regulates gene expression.
Tags: genome-evolution

---

Q: Why are eukaryotic genomes rich in regulatory DNA?
A: A eukaryotic cell needs to turn the same set of genes on and off differently in different cell types and at different times. That requires far more regulatory sequence than a prokaryote managing a single cell type ever needs, and elaborate gene regulation is especially critical for building complex multicellular organisms with many cell types.
Tags: genome-evolution

---

Q: Cells within one organism can look and behave completely differently, a neuron versus a red blood cell versus a skin cell, despite (almost) every cell carrying an identical copy of the genome. How is that possible?
![Cell types can vary enormously in size and shape: a human neuron with a large branching dendritic tree is compared to a small round neutrophil white blood cell, drawn to the same scale](assets/img/fig-1-31.png)
A: The genome isn't a shopping list every cell fully acts on; it's more like a reference library big enough for the whole organism, and each cell type only calls up part of it. The cell behaves as a multipurpose machine with sensors for environmental signals and the ability to call different sets of genes into action according to its developmental history and what it detects around it. Those expression differences, not any difference in the DNA sequence itself, are what make cell types look and behave so differently.
Tags: genome-evolution, regulatory-dna

---

Q+: What kinds of gene products do the regulating?
A: Mostly **transcription regulators**, proteins that bind directly or indirectly to the DNA sequences controlling which genes are expressed and at what level. Eukaryotic genomes also produce many noncoding RNA molecules which are never translated but control gene expression in a variety of ways. In this sense the expanded genome specifies both the hardware of the cell and the software that decides how the hardware gets used.
Tags: regulatory-dna

---

Q: In the snapdragon *Antirrhinum*, a mutation in a single gene coding for a regulatory protein makes leafy shoots grow where flowers should be. Why is that a striking demonstration rather than just an odd defect?
![A mutant snapdragon with leafy shoots in place of flowers shown beside a normal flowering plant](assets/img/fig-1-32.png)
A: Because nothing is wrong with the cells' ability to build a structure; they build a perfectly good leafy shoot. What changed is which structure they were told to build. Altering one regulatory protein made the cells adopt characters appropriate to a different location in the plant, which shows that position and identity in a developing organism are set by regulatory genes rather than by the structural genes doing the building.
Tags: regulatory-dna

---

Q: How elaborate can a single-celled eukaryote be?
![An assortment of single-cell eukaryotes drawn to different scales: ciliates, a heliozoan, an amoeba, a dinoflagellate and a euglenoid](assets/img/fig-1-33.png)
A: Remarkably elaborate. Their anatomy can include sensory bristles, photoreceptors, sinuously beating cilia, leg-like appendages, mouth parts, stinging darts and muscle-like contractile bundles. Although they are single cells, they can be as intricate and as complex in their behaviour as many multicellular organisms.

---

Q: What is a **protist**, and how does *Didinium* hunt?
![Didinium, a carnivorous ciliate, shown swimming and then engulfing another protist almost as large as itself](assets/img/fig-1-34.png)
A: A protist is a free-living, single-celled, mobile eukaryote. *Didinium* is a carnivorous ciliate with a globular body about 150 μm across, encircled by two fringes of beating cilia, with a flattened front end bearing a single snout-like protrusion. It swims at high speed by beating its cilia in synchrony, and on meeting suitable prey, usually another protist, it fires numerous small paralyzing darts from the snout, then attaches and devours the victim by phagocytosis, inverting like a hollow ball to engulf prey that can be almost as large as itself.

---

Q: Are all eukaryotic cells part of multicellular organisms?
A: No. Many eukaryotic cells live as solitary, free-living cells: amoebae, yeast, and other single-celled protists. Multicellularity is one thing eukaryotic cells can do, not a defining feature of the cell type.
![A eukaryotic tree of life based on genome comparisons, showing eight major supergroups: Archaeplastida, Haptophytes, Alveolates, Stramenopiles, Rhizaria, Amoebozoa, Excavates and Opisthokonts (animals and fungi), with animals and plants separated by many single-celled lineages](assets/img/fig-1-35.png)
Animals and plants sit on opposite sides of the eukaryotic tree, separated by several solitary-celled lineages, which is why multicellularity is thought to have evolved independently more than once.

---

Q+: What does the diversity of single-celled eukaryotes say about when animals and plants appeared?
A: That they appeared relatively late. The genomes of single-celled eukaryotes are far more evolutionarily diverse than those of multicellular animals and plants, so animals and plants occupy late branches of a long and varied eukaryotic pedigree rather than sitting at its centre.

---

Q: Why does biology lean so heavily on 'model' organisms?
A: Studying one organism deeply pays off far beyond that organism: because the core machinery of life is conserved, a mechanism worked out in yeast, a worm, or a fly usually turns out to work the same way in humans. Concentrating effort on a small set of well-characterized species lets the whole field pool its knowledge and tools instead of every lab starting from scratch.
Tags: model-organisms

---

Q: The two complementary approaches for working out what a gene does are biochemistry and genetics. What does each contribute?
A: Biochemistry examines the functions of purified molecules directly: get the protein or RNA out of the organism and study its chemical activities in detail. Genetics starts from mutants instead: find or make an organism in which the gene is altered, then look at the effects on its structure and performance. Neither alone connects a sequence to a role in a living cell, but the two together, with cell biology, tie genes and molecules to what the cell and the organism actually do.

---

Q: A single gene mutation turns the fission yeast *Schizosaccharomyces pombe* from a cigar shape into a T shape. What does that experiment establish, and what does it leave open?
![Scanning electron micrographs of a normal cigar-shaped Schizosaccharomyces pombe cell beside a mutant T-shaped cell](assets/img/fig-1-36.png)
A: It establishes that the mutated gene has a function in controlling cell shape, since altering it and nothing else changes the shape. What it leaves open is how the gene product does that in molecular terms, which genetics alone cannot answer and which needs biochemical analysis.

---

Q: Is there a general recipe for discovering what a gene does?
A: No. Sequence comparison can point to conserved subregions that are likely to matter most, and those can be tested by mutating specific sites or building hybrid genes from parts of two others. Structural work can give the position of every atom in the product, and cell biology can find its interaction partners and location. But you can learn that a gene product catalyses a particular reaction and still have no idea why that reaction matters to the organism. Function is not fully understood until the gene's role in the life of the whole organism is known, which means studying organisms and not only isolated molecules or cells.

---

Q: What is a virus?
A: A small packet of genetic material, DNA or RNA, wrapped in a protective protein coat. Viruses are not strictly alive: they have no metabolism of their own and depend on the reproductive and biosynthetic machinery of the host cells they infect. In sheer numbers they are the most abundant biological entities on the planet.
Tags: viruses

---

Q+: Viruses were missed entirely until the end of the nineteenth century. How were they first detected, and what finally made them visible?
A: By filtration. They were identified as infectious agents that pass through filters fine enough to trap bacteria, yet are held back by finer filters that let large molecules through, which put a size on them long before anyone could see one. Only the invention of the electron microscope allowed them to be visualized as particles with definite shapes and sizes.
Tags: viruses

---

Q: What is a bacteriophage?
A: A virus that infects bacteria. Phages such as T4 and lambda were the classic tools used to work out fundamental mechanisms of molecular genetics, since their genomes are far smaller than a bacterium's and correspondingly easier to analyse.
Tags: viruses

---

Q: How is the T4 bacteriophage built, and how does it infect?
![Electron micrograph of T4 bacteriophage particles, a cross section of an infected E. coli, and cryo-electron microscopy images of DNA injection](assets/img/fig-1-37.png)
A: It has a hexagonal head containing the viral DNA and a tail carrying the apparatus for injecting that DNA into a host bacterium. The particle attaches to the surface of an *E. coli*, injects its DNA, and new heads assemble inside the bacterium until the cell bursts and releases them.
Tags: viruses

---

Q+: What did genetic work on bacteriophages T4 and lambda deliver?
A: Geneticists made and characterized more than a hundred different mutant genes in T4 alone. Sets of genes encoding the head and the tail were identified, and studying them revealed important principles of biological assembly. The T4 genes essential for DNA replication let those proteins be purified so that biochemists could reconstruct DNA replication in a test tube. Work on bacteriophage lambda gave the early understanding of transcription regulators and gene regulatory networks.
Tags: viruses

---

Q: How numerous are viruses in the ocean, and what do they do to it?
A: Ocean viruses are estimated at $10^{30}$ particles. Lined up end to end they would extend beyond our nearest galaxies. They kill roughly 20% of the total ocean microbial biomass per day, and because that puts so much material back into circulation, their role in nutrient recycling profoundly affects Earth's ecology.

---

Q: What are the main stages of a viral infection?
A: Attachment to the host cell, entry of the genome, replication of the viral genome and synthesis of viral components, assembly of new virus particles, and release from the host cell by lysis or, for enveloped viruses, budding.
Tags: viruses

---

Q: What is the difference between the **lytic** and **lysogenic** cycles of a bacteriophage?
A: In the **lytic** cycle the phage replicates immediately and bursts the host cell open to release new virus particles. In the **lysogenic** cycle the phage genome instead integrates into the host chromosome as a **prophage**, is copied passively along with the host's own DNA for generations, and can later be induced to switch to the lytic cycle.
Tags: viruses

---

Q: Viral DNA that does not immediately make new virus particles can persist in a host in two forms. What are they?
A: Either as a separate intracellular fragment of DNA, known as a **plasmid**, or as a sequence inserted into the cell's own genome. In both cases it can ride along as a relatively harmless passenger for many cell generations.
Tags: viruses

---

Q: How do viruses act as vectors for gene transfer between cells?
A: A virus replicates in one cell, leaves with a protective wrapping, then enters another cell that may be of the same or a different species. On these travels it can accidentally pick up fragments of DNA from one host genome and carry them into another. Many bacteria and archaea can also take up nonviral DNA straight from their surroundings, so by either route cells in the wild acquire genes from their neighbours fairly easily.
Tags: genome-evolution

---

Q+: What makes that medically dangerous?
A: Genes conferring antibiotic resistance or the ability to produce a toxin can move from species to species and give the recipient a strong selective advantage. New and sometimes dangerous antibiotic-resistant strains have been observed evolving this way in the bacterial ecosystems of hospitals and of various niches in the human body.
Tags: genome-evolution

Q: Roughly what fraction of present-day *E. coli* genes were acquired by horizontal gene transfer within the last 100 million years?
A: An estimated 18%.

Q: How does viral DNA integrating into a host genome connect to CRISPR?
A: When a bacteriophage infects a bacterium, its DNA can end up integrated into the host chromosome. CRISPR is a bacterial immune system built on exactly this kind of event: short fragments of past invaders' DNA are stored as "spacers" in a CRISPR array in the genome, and are used to recognize and destroy matching DNA the next time that phage attacks. It is effectively a genetic memory of past infections, and it is the mechanism CRISPR-based gene editing tools are built on.

Q: Why is *E. coli* such a heavily used model organism?
A: It lives in the gut of humans and other vertebrates but grows easily in a simple nutrient broth, reproducing every 20 minutes or so under favourable conditions, and it adapts to changing chemical conditions and evolves by mutation and selection at remarkable speed. Most of our understanding of how cells replicate DNA and decode it into RNA and protein came first from *E. coli* and its viruses, and holds up because the basic genetic mechanisms are essentially the same in our own cells.

Q: Why is the yeast *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* used as the minimal model eukaryote?
A: It lets you study the workings of a eukaryotic cell without the additional problem of multicellular development. It is a small single-celled fungus, closer to animals than to plants by genome sequence, robust and easy to grow in simple medium, dividing about every hundred minutes when nutrients are plentiful.
![Scanning electron micrograph of budding yeast cells beside an electron micrograph of a cross section showing the thick cell wall and intracellular organelles](assets/img/fig-1-39.png)
Like other fungi it has a tough cell wall, is relatively immobile, and has mitochondria but no chloroplasts.

Q: How does yeast reproduce?
A: Either vegetatively, by ordinary cell division as a haploid or diploid cell, or sexually: two haploid cells ($n = 1$) fuse to form a diploid ($n = 2$), which under starvation can undergo meiosis to produce haploid spores.
![The yeast life cycle: diploid cells proliferate by budding, or under starvation undergo meiosis and sporulation into haploid spores, which hatch into haploid cells that proliferate by budding or mate to restore the diploid state](assets/img/fig-1-40.png)
Spores are dormant haploid cells resistant to harsh conditions. Unlike most animals, yeast can be pushed down either route simply by changing the growth conditions, which is a considerable convenience for an experimenter.
Tags: model-organisms

---

Q+: What else makes yeast convenient for genetics, and what has come out of it?
A: Its genome is exceptionally small by eukaryotic standards, about 12,500,000 nucleotide pairs including roughly 78,500 from mitochondrial DNA, yet it suffices for every basic task a eukaryotic cell must perform. That is only about 2.7 times as much DNA as *E. coli* and codes for only about 1.5 times as many proteins, since selection for rapid proliferation has kept it compact. Mutants exist for every gene, so the consequence of losing each one in turn can be observed. Work on yeast produced the keys to the cell-division cycle and meiosis, and to chromosome structure, nuclear organization, gene expression, organelle formation and protein secretion.

Q+: How conserved are these processes between yeast and us?
A: Conserved enough that a human homolog of a yeast protein will often carry out the yeast protein's job faithfully when artificially expressed in yeast cells, across more than a billion years of eukaryotic evolution.

Q: Why is *Arabidopsis thaliana* the model plant of choice?

Q: Why is *C. elegans* particularly useful for studying **apoptosis** (programmed cell death)?
![Photograph of the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, about 1 mm long](assets/img/fig-1-42.png)
A: Its development is essentially invariant: every cell division from fertilized egg to adult follows the same fixed pattern, producing an adult with exactly 959 body cells plus a variable number of egg and sperm cells. That regularity makes it possible to trace the fate of every single cell, which is what let researchers work out the genetic pathway controlling programmed cell death. The same mechanisms control cell numbers in the human body, in normal development and in cancer.

Q+: What else about *C. elegans* makes it convenient, and what else came out of it?
A: It is about 1 mm long, lives in soil, is harmless, and most individuals are hermaphrodites producing both eggs and sperm. Its life cycle takes only a few days and it survives indefinitely in a freezer in suspended animation. Its genome of about 100 million nucleotide pairs codes for about 20,000 proteins. Beyond apoptosis, studies in the worm first revealed many features of RNA interference and gave key insights into how neurons make their proper connections.

Q: How did studies in *Drosophila* provide a key to understanding vertebrate development?
![A normal adult Drosophila beside a mutant in which legs have developed where the antennae should be](assets/img/fig-1-43.png)
A: Screens for mutants with body parts misplaced or mispatterned uncovered the genes required to build a properly structured body, with gut, limbs, eyes and everything else in its correct place. In the mutant shown, a mutation in a regulatory DNA sequence switched on leg-forming genes in the position reserved for antennae, so legs grew where antennae belong. Once those genes were identified, homologous genes were found in vertebrates and tested in mice, revealing an astonishing degree of similarity in the mechanisms governing insect and vertebrate development.

Q+: Nearly 100 years ago *Drosophila* also settled a more basic question. Which one, and how?
![Giant polytene chromosomes from Drosophila salivary gland cells showing a reproducible pattern of bands](assets/img/fig-1-44.png)
A: Whether genes, at the time purely abstract units of hereditary information, are carried on chromosomes. The proof used the giant chromosomes of salivary gland cells, in which many rounds of DNA replication without cell division leave more than 1000 identical DNA molecules aligned in register, making a reproducible banded pattern visible in a light microscope. Specific changes in the hereditary information of mutant flies correlated exactly with the loss or alteration of specific bands.

Q+: On such a chromosome, what does a "puffed" band indicate?
A: A gene being transcribed at a high rate. Bands can also be stained to show where a particular regulatory protein is bound, by using an antibody that binds that protein.

Q: How uniform a group are the mammals, genetically and anatomically?
A: Highly uniform. Anatomically the differences are chiefly of size and proportion, and it is hard to name a human body part with no counterpart in an elephant or a mouse. Evolution plays freely with quantitative features but does not readily change the logic of the basic structure. Mammals have roughly 1.5 times as many protein-coding genes as *Drosophila* and a genome about 16 times larger, with adult bodies made of millions or billions of times as many cells.

Q+: If you line up a human protein against its ortholog in an elephant, how similar are they? And against a bird?

Q: Divergence times between vertebrates can be estimated from how much orthologous proteins have drifted apart. What complicates reading the hemoglobin data as a clock?
![Geological timescale of divergence times for pairs of vertebrates, with percent amino acid identity in the hemoglobin alpha chain shown alongside](assets/img/fig-1-47.png)

Q: Where are viruses thought to have come from?
A: From parasitic genetic elements in the RNA world, before there were proteins or DNA molecules: small RNA molecules that exploited more advanced replicating entities in order to proliferate. Those are believed to be the ancestors of today's smallest viruses, whose single-strand RNA genomes run to as few as 3000 nucleotides, which would make virus-like entities a feature of life on Earth for more than 3 billion years.

Q+: What is the minimum a virus genome has to encode?
A: Two core functions. One is a nucleic acid replication process that makes multiple copies of its genome once inside the host cell. The other is a genome-packaging process that wraps those new genomes in a protective protein coat and lets the virus leave the host and enter others.

Q+: Why then are real virus genomes much larger than that minimum?
A: Because of a constant war with their hosts. Host cells have evolved multiple antiviral defenses and viruses have evolved ways around them, so through cycles of mutation and selection most virus genomes have grown well past what the two core functions require, with the additional genes encoding proteins that help the virus circumvent host defenses.

Q: How is a coronavirus built, and how many proteins does SARS-CoV-2 make?
![Electron micrograph of SARS-CoV-2 particles on a monkey cell, a cut-away drawing showing spike proteins, and a chart of the 29 viral proteins in three categories](assets/img/fig-1-49.png)

Q+: What do those three categories of protein do?
A: The structural proteins (S, M, E and N) package the RNA genome into the particle. The nonstructural proteins replicate the genome and manage translation of the viral genes: Nsp1 binds ribosomes to block host protein synthesis, Nsp3, Nsp4 and Nsp6 build a double-membrane "replication organelle" out of host-cell membranes, and Nsp7, Nsp8 and Nsp12 form the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase. Each accessory protein has a role in protecting the virus from host antiviral responses.

Q+: Why is the spike protein the main target for vaccines?
A: Because it does the two things that begin an infection: it attaches the virus to the outside of a host cell, and it then catalyses transfer of the viral genome into the cell interior. Blocking it blocks infection at the entry step.

Q: Coronaviruses are not new to us. Where do they normally circulate, and what makes SARS-CoV-2 different?
A: Many strains circulate in animals including pigs, birds and bats, and several endemic strains circulate among humans causing only mild symptoms, responsible for about one in four common colds. On rare occasions a bat coronavirus mutates so that it can infect humans, where it can cause severe or fatal disease, and the COVID-19 pandemic of 2020 is thought to have started that way. SARS-CoV-2 is closely related both to the cold-causing coronaviruses and to SARS-CoV, which emerged from bats in 2002 and killed nearly 1 in 10 of the people it infected. Q: How did the COVID-19 pandemic turn a virus into a model organism?
A: The scale of the pandemic focused an enormous amount of scientific effort onto SARS-CoV-2 itself, driving rapid progress in virology, structural biology of the spike protein, and vaccine platforms such as mRNA vaccines. Thousands of laboratories working on one eukaryotic virus made it a model system in the same sense that *E. coli* phages once were.

Q: In what sense is the human population itself a kind of model organism?
A: Through medical examination and self-reporting we catalog our own genetic disorders, which no other species does. With roughly 8 billion people alive and genome sequences determined for hundreds of thousands of them, there is an enormous database of human mutations and their effects, making it easier than ever to identify the precise genetic change behind a given mutant phenotype.

Q+: Whose genome is "the" human genome?
A: There is no single answer, which is the point. Any two people taken at random differ at roughly 4 million sites in their DNA, so the human genome really means the whole pool of variant genes in the population. That variation is what tells us why some people are prone to one disease and others to another, and why some respond well to a drug and others badly. It also carries traces of our history: population movements, interbreeding among our ancestors, the infections they suffered and the diets they ate.

Q: Why does understanding cells increasingly require mathematics and computation, not just biochemistry?
A: Cell behavior emerges from large networks of interacting genes and proteins wired together with feedback loops, and small changes in parameters can cause radical changes in outcome, which intuition handles badly.
![A very simple gene regulatory circuit: a gene's regulatory DNA and protein-coding region are transcribed into mRNA and translated into a transcription regulator protein, which feeds back to bind the gene's own regulatory DNA](assets/img/fig-1-50.png)
Even this single self-regulating gene raises questions a diagram cannot answer: does the bound protein inhibit or stimulate transcription, how steeply does the rate depend on protein concentration, how long does a molecule stay bound, how fast is each molecule made and degraded. Depending on those values the circuit may settle to a steady state, behave as a switch between alternative states, oscillate, or show large random fluctuations.
Tags: genome-evolution

---

Q+: If we knew every interaction in a cell, could we then calculate its behaviour the way an astronomer calculates orbits?
A: Not yet, and the obstacle is the quality of the information rather than the idea. What we have is plentiful but full of gaps and uncertainties, and largely qualitative: cell biologists usually sum up their knowledge in schematic diagrams rather than in numbers, graphs and differential equations. The move from qualitative description to quantitative deduction has so far succeeded only for small subsystems involving a handful of proteins or two or three genes regulating one another, where theory and experiment go closely together.

Q: The human hemoglobin gene family has seven genes arranged in two clusters on different chromosomes. Is every member an ortholog of every other member?

Q: "Without additional information, no amount of gazing at genome sequences will reveal the functions of genes." Is that true?

Q: "Life" is easy to recognize but hard to define. The dictionary defines it by metabolism, growth, reproduction and response to stimuli. Score a car, a cactus and yourself against those four criteria. What does the exercise reveal?

Q: The natural genetic code sits at the extreme low end of a mutation-susceptibility distribution built from millions of randomly generated alternative codes: only about one in a million random codes resists mutation better than the real one. Does that argue for the code being a frozen accident, or a product of natural selection?
![Histogram of susceptibility to mutation for millions of randomly generated genetic codes, forming a bell curve, with the natural genetic code marked as a rare outlier far to the low-susceptibility side](assets/img/fig-q1-01.png)
A: It argues for natural selection having shaped the code. A pure frozen accident predicts the natural code should land somewhere unremarkable within the distribution, wherever chance first happened to fix it. Instead it sits far out in the tail, more error-resistant than 999,999 out of a million alternatives. That's the signature of codes with poor mutation tolerance being selected against over time. The honest complication: selection could have acted early and then the improved code got frozen in place, so "frozen accident" and "shaped by selection" aren't fully exclusive.

Q+: What structural feature of the code is doing most of that work?
A: Redundancy in the third position of a codon. A change there often specifies the same amino acid, or one with similar chemical properties, so a large fraction of point mutations either say the same thing or say something close to it. Susceptibility in this analysis measures the average change in amino acid properties caused by random mutation, so a code that mostly swaps like for like scores low.

Q: You sample the ocean beneath the ice of Europa and find a cellular life-form that grows in rich broth and contains DNA, RNA and protein. A colleague suspects contamination from Earth. How would you try to tell contamination apart from a genuinely independent, DNA-RNA-protein-based life-form?
A: Check whether it shares the specific, arbitrary choices terrestrial life made, not just the general chemistry. Does it use the same genetic code, the same codon-to-amino-acid assignments? The same handedness of sugars and amino acids? Sequence its genome and see whether its rRNA or core genes are homologous to, and alignable with, anything on Earth's tree of life. Shared ancestry with terrestrial contamination predicts recognizable homology; a truly independent origin does not, even though both would be built from DNA, RNA and protein.

Q: What does it actually mean for phototrophs to "feed" on sunlight, or for lithotrophs to "feed" on the mix of H₂S, H₂, CO, Mn²⁺, Fe²⁺, Ni²⁺, CH₄ and NH₄⁺ spewing from a hydrothermal vent? Where is the "food"?

The "food" is the redox potential difference in the vent chemistry, not any single molecule in it.

Q: Assuming Bacteria, Archaea and Eukaryotes all arose from a single common ancestor, how many distinct rooted branching patterns (trees) are possible for the three of them?

With three taxa descending from one ancestor, the only choice is which two are each other's closest relatives: (Bacteria, Archaea) with Eukaryotes outside, (Bacteria, Eukaryotes) with Archaea outside, or (Archaea, Eukaryotes) with Bacteria outside. Q: Ribosomal RNA genes are highly conserved and have evolved very slowly across all of life. Were they "born" perfect?
A: No. Slow evolution today doesn't mean rRNA started out optimal; it means any mutation to it now is so likely to break ribosome function that selection removes it almost immediately, freezing the sequence in place. Early on, while translation machinery was still being worked out, rRNA plausibly tolerated, and needed, much more change. The slow rate is a property of the present-day constraint, not of the gene's origin.

Q: The rat lineage evolves faster than the human lineage, in both coding and noncoding sequence. What could explain a rate difference that shows up regardless of whether the DNA is under selection?
A: Generation time is the standard explanation. Most mutations arise from errors made during DNA replication, and rats pack far more generations into a given stretch of time than humans do. More generations means more rounds of replication and more chances for a copying error, and that effect hits coding and noncoding DNA equally, which fits the observation that the gap doesn't depend on whether the sequence is under selective constraint.

Q: Genes for informational processes (replication, transcription, translation) undergo horizontal transfer between species far less often than genes for metabolism, possibly because informational processes involve large aggregates of gene products while metabolic reactions are often catalyzed by a single protein. Why would that difference in complexity affect the rate of horizontal transfer?
A: A single-protein metabolic enzyme can be dropped into a new genome and function immediately; it only has to interact with its small-molecule substrate. A component of an information-processing machine like a ribosome or a replisome has to physically and functionally fit with many other components already present in the recipient cell, all of which must be compatible. A transferred copy that lacks its usual partners is far more likely to be useless or actively harmful, so genes with many interaction partners face a much steeper barrier to successful horizontal transfer than genes that act alone.

Q: Animal cells have no cell wall and no chloroplasts. Plant cells have both. Fungal cells have a wall but no chloroplasts. Are fungi more likely to be animal cells that gained a wall, or plant cells that lost their chloroplasts? How was this actually settled?
A: Neither. Molecular phylogenies built from rRNA and other conserved genes, not from cell-surface traits, showed fungi form their own branch, and that branch sits closer to animals than to plants. The wall itself is a red herring: fungal walls are made of chitin and plant walls of cellulose, so the two evolved independently rather than one being inherited or lost from the other. This is a clean example of why classification moved from morphology to genome comparison.

Q: *Giardia lamblia* is a eukaryote with a nucleus but no mitochondria, and no obvious ER or Golgi. Is that because *Giardia* is an ancient lineage that split off before those features evolved, or because it's a more typical eukaryote that lost them? How could nucleotide sequence comparison distinguish the two hypotheses?
A: Build a phylogenetic tree from genes *Giardia* does have, and see where it roots relative to other eukaryotes. An ancient, pre-mitochondrial lineage should branch off near the base of the eukaryotic tree, before the split shared by every other eukaryote. Separately, check *Giardia*'s nuclear genome for the mitochondria-derived genes that migrated to the nucleus in essentially every other eukaryote; finding them despite the absence of a visible mitochondrion points to secondary loss rather than an ancestral lineage that never had one. (This is in fact what later work found: *Giardia* retains a vestigial, highly reduced mitochondrion-derived organelle.)

Given a phylogenetic tree built from hemoglobin sequences across vertebrates, invertebrates, plants and protozoa, does the tree support or refute that hypothesis, and what would the tree look like instead if the transfer had come from a parasitic nematode?
![Unrooted phylogenetic tree of hemoglobin genes: vertebrates, invertebrates, plants including legumes, and protozoa each form their own separate branch from a shared center, rather than plant hemoglobins nesting inside the animal branch](assets/img/fig-q1-02.png)
A: The tree refutes it. If the legume genes had been transferred from an animal, they should nest inside the animal branch, closest to whichever animal was the donor. Instead the plant hemoglobins, legumes included, form their own coherent branch, as far from any single animal sequence as the animal sequences are from each other. That's the pattern expected from vertical inheritance of an ancient gene present before plants and animals diverged, not from a recent transfer.
If the source had instead been a parasitic nematode, the legume genes would be expected to branch from within, or as the closest sister to, the nematode's own sequence among the invertebrates, rather than forming an independent plant branch.
