Four pages that were disbelieved for roughly fifteen years and then won a Nobel Prize. I
read it because the textbook presents the proton gradient as settled fact, and I wanted to
see what it looked like as a proposal.

## The problem it solves

Everyone was hunting for a chemical intermediate, some high-energy compound that carried
the energy from electron transport to ATP synthesis, by analogy with substrate-level
phosphorylation. Nobody could isolate it, for years, which in hindsight is the whole story.

Mitchell's answer is that there is no such molecule. The intermediate is a **gradient**:
electron transport pumps protons across a membrane, and the resulting electrochemical
potential drives ATP synthesis. The energy sits in the geometry rather than in a bond.

## Why it was hard to accept

It requires the membrane to be a *component* of the mechanism rather than a container for
it. A closed compartment isn't a convenience of the experiment; it is load-bearing. Break
the vesicle and the reaction cannot work at all, even with every enzyme present and
functional. That was an unfamiliar shape of explanation in 1961.

The prediction that settled it is right there in the paper, and it is cleanly falsifiable:
an artificially imposed pH gradient, with no electron transport at all, should make ATP.
It does.

## What I took from it

Worth holding onto as a general lesson about failed searches. Fifteen years of not finding
the intermediate was itself the strongest available evidence about what kind of thing the
intermediate was. A negative result that stubborn is itself data.
