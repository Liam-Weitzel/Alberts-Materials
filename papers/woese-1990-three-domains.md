The paper that put the three domains on the map. Alberts states the conclusion in a
paragraph; this is the four pages of argument underneath it, and it is worth reading
because the reasoning is more interesting than the result.

## The argument

Classification had been built on what cells look like, which put Archaea with Bacteria:
both are small, both lack a nucleus. Woese's move is to insist that the only defensible
basis for a *natural* system is descent, and that descent is legible in sequence rather
than in morphology. Small-subunit rRNA is the molecule to read it in, because every cell
has one and it changes slowly enough to still carry signal across billions of years.

Once you do that, Archaea and Bacteria turn out to be about as distant from each other as
either is from us. The prokaryote/eukaryote split isn't the deepest division in life; it
just happens to be the one visible in a microscope.

## What I took from it

The methodological point generalises well beyond this result: **a shared trait is only
evidence of relatedness if it is unlikely to have arisen twice**. "No nucleus" fails that
test, because it is the absence of a feature and absence is easy to arrive at
independently. A specific rRNA sequence passes it.

Also worth noticing that they are explicit about the tree being rooted by paralogous gene
duplications rather than by the rRNA data itself. The rRNA tree alone is unrooted, which
is a limitation the textbook diagram quietly drops.
