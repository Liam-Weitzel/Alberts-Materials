## Atoms and chemical bonds

All cellular chemistry ultimately depends on atoms and the bonds and interactions between them.

### Covalent bonds

A **covalent bond** forms when atoms share pairs of electrons.

Covalent bonds are strong and are used to construct stable biological molecules such as proteins, nucleic acids, carbohydrates, and lipids.

Breaking a covalent bond requires considerably more energy than disrupting the weak noncovalent interactions that organize many cellular structures.

### Ionic interactions

An **ionic bond** or ionic interaction occurs between oppositely charged atoms or groups.

Ions are produced when electrons are transferred or when chemical groups gain or lose charge.

For example:

$$
Na \rightarrow Na^+ + e^-
$$

$$
Cl + e^- \rightarrow Cl^-
$$

The resulting positive and negative ions attract each other.

Ionic interactions can be strong in the absence of water, but they are much weaker in an aqueous environment because water molecules surround and stabilize the individual charges.

### Electronegativity

**Electronegativity** describes how strongly an atom attracts electrons.

When two atoms have similar electronegativities, electrons tend to be shared relatively evenly.

When their electronegativities differ, the bond becomes polar because one atom attracts the shared electrons more strongly.

A sufficiently large difference in electron affinity can favor electron transfer and the formation of ions.

## Polar covalent bonds and water

Water contains polar covalent bonds.

Oxygen is more electronegative than hydrogen and therefore attracts the shared electrons toward itself.

This produces:

* a partial negative charge on oxygen
* partial positive charges on the hydrogens

These partial charges allow water molecules to interact strongly with one another and with other polar molecules.

## Hydrogen bonds

A **hydrogen bond** forms when a partially positive hydrogen atom interacts with an electronegative atom carrying a partial negative charge.

In water, the hydrogen of one water molecule can form a hydrogen bond with the oxygen of another water molecule.

Hydrogen bonds can also involve nitrogen and other sufficiently electronegative atoms.

Hydrogen bonding is important in many biological structures, including:

* DNA base pairing
* protein folding
* interactions between water and polar molecules

Polar and charged molecules generally dissolve well in water because they can form favorable interactions with water molecules.

## Weak noncovalent interactions

Many biological structures depend on large numbers of individually weak interactions.

### Van der Waals interactions

**Van der Waals interactions** are weak attractions caused by transient fluctuations in electron distribution.

These fluctuations create temporary partial charges that can attract nearby atoms.

Each interaction is weak, but many van der Waals interactions acting together can contribute significantly to molecular stability.

### Hydrophobic interactions

Nonpolar molecules cannot form favorable interactions with water.

Water molecules surrounding a nonpolar surface become more ordered.

When nonpolar molecules cluster together, less nonpolar surface is exposed to water.

This reduces the amount of ordered water surrounding them.

The tendency of nonpolar molecules to aggregate in water is called the **hydrophobic effect**.

The hydrophobic effect is important in:

* formation of cell membranes
* protein folding
* assembly of many molecular complexes

## Effect of water on chemical interactions

Covalent bonds are relatively unaffected by whether they are present in water or in a vacuum.

Noncovalent interactions can behave very differently.

Ionic interactions are much weaker in water because polar water molecules surround the ions and partially shield their charges from one another.

This is why many ionic compounds dissolve readily in water.

# Small organic molecules

Four major families of small organic molecules are particularly important in cells:

* sugars
* amino acids
* fatty acids
* nucleotides

These molecules can serve as building blocks for larger biological molecules.

Examples include:

$$
\text{amino acids} \rightarrow \text{proteins}
$$

$$
\text{nucleotides} \rightarrow \text{DNA and RNA}
$$

$$
\text{sugars} \rightarrow \text{polysaccharides}
$$

Fatty acids are important components of lipids, although lipids are not generally polymers in the same way that proteins and nucleic acids are.

## Condensation and hydrolysis

Many biological macromolecules are assembled through **condensation reactions**.

During a condensation reaction, two molecules are joined through the formation of a covalent bond.

These biosynthetic reactions generally require an input of energy.

The reverse process is **hydrolysis**.

During hydrolysis, water is used to break a covalent bond.

Hydrolysis reactions are often energetically favorable, although whether a particular reaction proceeds spontaneously depends on its overall free-energy change.

# Thermodynamics and cellular order

## First law of thermodynamics

The **first law of thermodynamics** states that energy cannot be created or destroyed.

Energy can only be transferred or converted from one form into another.

Cells therefore do not create energy.

They convert energy from food or other sources into forms that can be used to perform cellular work.

## Second law of thermodynamics

The **second law of thermodynamics** states that the total disorder of the universe tends to increase.

More precisely, spontaneous processes increase the total entropy of the system and its surroundings.

Cells are highly ordered structures, but they do not violate the second law.

Cells maintain and create local order by consuming energy and releasing heat and waste into their surroundings.

The increase in disorder outside the cell exceeds the decrease in disorder produced inside the cell.

## Metabolism

Cells obtain energy by breaking down food molecules.

The complete set of chemical reactions occurring in a cell is called **metabolism**.

### Catabolism

**Catabolic pathways** break molecules down.

They release energy and generate smaller molecules that can be used elsewhere in the cell.

For example:

$$
\text{food molecules} \rightarrow \text{smaller molecules + usable energy + heat}
$$

### Anabolism

**Anabolic pathways** use energy to synthesize more complex molecules.

They increase local order within the cell.

Catabolic reactions can therefore provide both the energy and the building blocks required for anabolic reactions.

These pathways depend on enzymes to occur at biologically useful rates.

# Oxidation and reduction

Many energy-transfer reactions in cells involve the movement of electrons.

## Oxidation

**Oxidation** is the loss of electrons.

In biological molecules, oxidation often corresponds to:

* loss of hydrogen atoms
* gain of oxygen atoms
* a decrease in the number of C-H bonds

## Reduction

**Reduction** is the gain of electrons.

Reduction often corresponds to gaining hydrogen atoms or losing oxygen atoms.

Oxidation and reduction always occur together.

If one molecule loses electrons, another molecule must accept them.

These coupled reactions are called **redox reactions**.

A useful mnemonic is:

**OIL RIG**

Oxidation Is Loss
Reduction Is Gain

# Gibbs free energy

**Gibbs free energy**, represented by \(G\), describes the amount of energy available to perform useful work under a particular set of conditions.

The change in free energy during a reaction is:

$$
\Delta G = G_{\text{products}} - G_{\text{reactants}}
$$

## Negative ΔG

If:

$$
\Delta G < 0
$$

the reaction is energetically favorable and can occur spontaneously.

The products contain less free energy than the reactants.

Free energy is released during the reaction.

This does not necessarily mean that the reaction occurs rapidly.

## Positive ΔG

If:

$$
\Delta G > 0
$$

the reaction is energetically unfavorable.

The products contain more free energy than the reactants.

An input of energy is required for the reaction to proceed.

## Equilibrium

If:

$$
\Delta G = 0
$$

the system is at equilibrium.

There is no net tendency for the reaction to proceed in either direction.

---

# Activation energy and enzymes

Even a reaction with a negative ΔG may occur extremely slowly.

Before the reaction can proceed, reactants must pass through a high-energy transition state.

The energy required to reach this state is called the **activation energy**.

On a graph of free energy against reaction progress, this appears as an energy barrier.

## Enzymes

Enzymes lower the activation energy required for a reaction.

This allows the reaction to proceed more rapidly.

Enzymes do **not** change the overall ΔG of the reaction.

They only provide a lower-energy pathway between reactants and products.

---

# Reaction coupling

Cells can drive energetically unfavorable reactions by coupling them to favorable reactions.

Suppose one reaction has:

$$
\Delta G > 0
$$

and another has:

$$
\Delta G < 0
$$

If they are chemically coupled so that:

$$
\Delta G_{\text{total}} < 0
$$

the combined process becomes energetically favorable.

This principle is used throughout cellular metabolism.

# Activated carrier molecules

Cells use specialized molecules to transfer energy or chemical groups between reactions.

## ATP

**ATP**, adenosine triphosphate, is the most widely used activated carrier of chemical energy.

Hydrolysis of ATP is energetically favorable:

$$
ATP + H_2O \rightarrow ADP + P_i
$$

ATP hydrolysis can be coupled to an unfavorable reaction.

Cells often use ATP by transferring a phosphate group to another molecule.

This produces a higher-energy intermediate that can participate in a reaction that would otherwise be unfavorable.

ATP is commonly used during biosynthetic reactions in which molecules need to be joined together.

## NADH and NADPH

**NADH** and **NADPH** are activated electron carriers.

They carry high-energy electrons in the form of a hydride ion, equivalent to:

$$
2e^- + H^+
$$

NADH is used primarily in reactions involved in energy production.

NADPH is used primarily in biosynthetic reactions and other reductive processes.

Although they are chemically similar, cells maintain separate pools of NADH and NADPH for different purposes.

## FAD and FADH₂

**FAD** is another electron carrier.

When reduced, it becomes:

$$
FADH_2
$$

FAD and FADH₂ participate in redox reactions, including reactions involved in energy metabolism.

## Acetyl-CoA

**Acetyl-CoA** carries an activated two-carbon acetyl group.

It is important both in energy metabolism and in biosynthesis.

The acetyl group can be transferred into other metabolic pathways or oxidized to obtain energy.

# Energy from food

Cells break down food molecules in a series of controlled reactions.

The energy released is captured in molecules such as ATP, NADH, and FADH₂ rather than being released entirely as heat.

## Glycolysis

**Glycolysis** occurs in the cytosol and does not require oxygen directly.

One glucose molecule is converted into two pyruvate molecules.

The overall yield is:

$$
\text{glucose} + 2 NAD^+ + 2 ADP + 2P_i
$$

$$
\rightarrow 2\text{ pyruvate} + 2 NADH + 2 ATP + 2H_2O
$$

Four ATP molecules are produced during glycolysis, but two ATP molecules are consumed earlier in the pathway.

The **net gain is therefore 2 ATP**.

## Pyruvate without oxygen

When oxidative metabolism cannot proceed, pyruvate can be reduced to lactate.

$$
\text{pyruvate} \rightarrow \text{lactate}
$$

This reaction regenerates \(NAD^+\), allowing glycolysis to continue.

This process is called **lactate fermentation**.

## Pyruvate with oxygen

Under aerobic conditions, pyruvate enters the mitochondrion.

It is converted into **acetyl-CoA**.

Acetyl-CoA then enters the citric acid cycle.

---

# Citric acid cycle

The **citric acid cycle**, also called the Krebs cycle or TCA cycle, takes place in the mitochondrial matrix in eukaryotic cells.

For each acetyl-CoA molecule that enters the cycle, the main products are:

$$
3 NADH
$$

$$
1 FADH_2
$$

$$
1 GTP
$$

$$
2 CO_2
$$

The carbon atoms of the acetyl group are ultimately oxidized.

Most of the usable energy is captured in NADH and FADH₂.

These electron carriers can then supply electrons to the electron transport chain, where much larger amounts of ATP can be generated.

---

# Fatty acid breakdown

Fatty acids can also be used as a major source of energy.

They are transported into mitochondria and broken down through **beta-oxidation**.

Beta-oxidation repeatedly removes two-carbon units from fatty acids.

These two-carbon units are released as acetyl-CoA.

The pathway also produces NADH and FADH₂.

The resulting acetyl-CoA can enter the citric acid cycle.

---

# Energy storage

Cells and organisms store energy in different chemical forms.

## Glycogen

Glucose can be stored in the form of **glycogen**.

When energy is required, glycogen can be broken down and the resulting glucose derivatives can enter glycolysis.

## Fat

Fat is an especially concentrated form of energy storage.

Fatty acids contain many reduced carbon atoms and therefore release a large amount of energy when oxidized.

---

# Metabolic intermediates

Metabolism is not simply a linear process whose only purpose is to produce ATP.

Many intermediates generated during glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and related pathways can be diverted into biosynthetic pathways.

These molecules can be used to make:

* amino acids
* nucleotides
* lipids
* other cellular components

Metabolic pathways therefore form an interconnected network.

Catabolism provides both energy and molecular building blocks.

Anabolism uses those resources to construct the molecules required by the cell.
