## Cells

All cells contain the information and machinery required for their own growth, reproduction, and interpretation of genetic information.

The human body contains approximately:

$$
3 \times 10^{13}
$$

cells.

A cell is the minimal unit that carries both halves of the problem: the **information** and the **machinery to interpret and reproduce it**. The lecture opens by asking for two numbers, and gives them on the next click: about **3 × 10¹³ human cells**, of about **325 cell types**.

The exact number of cell types depends on how finely subtypes are classified.

![Cell types can vary enormously in size and shape: a human neuron with a large branching dendritic tree is compared to a small round neutrophil white blood cell, drawn to the same scale](assets/img/fig-1-31.png)

By number, red blood cells dominate: the slide's breakdown of the human cell count puts **erythrocytes at 84%** — 25 × 10¹² of a 30 × 10¹² total — with platelets 4.9%, bone marrow cells 2.5%, vascular endothelial cells 2.1% and lymphocytes 1.5%. Everything people usually think of as "the body" is in the remainder: hepatocytes 0.8%, neurons and glia 0.6%, epidermal cells 0.5%, muscle cells 0.001%.

The lesson of the chart is that **cell number and cell mass are very different measures**, and that blood accounts for most of the count.

All cells are surrounded by a **plasma membrane**, which consists primarily of a **phospholipid bilayer**.

![A phospholipid has a hydrophilic head and hydrophobic tails, and in water these molecules spontaneously form a closed bilayer vesicle with heads facing the water on both sides](assets/img/fig-1-06.png)

Nutrients and waste have to cross that membrane, and the bilayer forms spontaneously: phospholipids at an oil–water interface line up as a **monolayer**, and in bulk water they close into a **bilayer vesicle**, tails inward, heads facing the water on both sides.

Despite the diversity of life, the fundamental biochemistry of living cells is remarkably similar.

## The universal features of life on Earth

The lecture states four of them, one per slide, and they are worth reciting in this order because each one sets up the next:

1. **All cells store their hereditary information as double-stranded DNA** — two sugar–phosphate backbones, hydrogen-bonded base pairs, and the four bases **adenine, guanine, cytosine and thymine**.
2. **All cells duplicate that information by templated polymerization.** The parent helix is opened and each strand acts as a template for a new one, giving two identical daughter helices. This is **replication**.
3. **All cells transcribe portions of their DNA into RNA**, and translate RNA into protein: **DNA synthesis → replication, RNA synthesis → transcription, protein synthesis → translation**.
4. **All cells use proteins as catalysts.** A protein's specific shape is dictated by its sequence, and the shape makes the catalytic site. The lecture's example is **lysozyme**, which binds a polysaccharide chain, cuts it, and releases the two products.

## Genetic information

DNA is replicated by **template-directed polymerization**.

Each DNA strand can serve as a template for the synthesis of a complementary strand.

![The two strands of a DNA double helix pulled apart, each serving as a template for a new complementary strand, producing two identical daughter double helices](assets/img/fig-1-03.png)

The basic flow of genetic information is:

$$
DNA \rightarrow RNA \rightarrow Protein
$$

DNA is **transcribed** into RNA.

Protein-coding RNA is **translated** into protein.

![From DNA to protein: DNA replicates itself, is transcribed into RNA, and RNA is translated into protein](assets/img/fig-1-04.png)

Not all RNA is translated. Many RNAs have functions of their own.

Proteins perform many of the catalytic reactions within cells, but proteins are not the only biological catalysts. Some RNA molecules also have catalytic activity.

![Life as an autocatalytic process: DNA and RNA provide sequence information used to build proteins, and proteins provide the catalytic activity needed to build DNA, RNA and more protein, closing the loop](assets/img/fig-1-05.png)

A major example is the **ribosome**. The ribosome contains both proteins and ribosomal RNA, and the catalytic center responsible for peptide bond formation is formed primarily by rRNA.

### Life as an autocatalytic process

The slide closes the loop: **DNA and RNA supply the sequence information** used to build proteins, and **proteins supply the catalytic activity** needed to make more DNA, RNA and protein. Neither half works without the other, and the loop is what makes a cell a **self-replicating collection of catalysts** — food in, waste out, and the whole collection reproduced before the cell divides.

## Mycoplasma

**Mycoplasma** species are notable for lacking a cell wall. They are surrounded only by a plasma membrane.

Mycoplasma contamination is a common problem in cultured cells because the bacteria can grow alongside the culture without necessarily causing obvious changes at first.

![Electron micrograph of a cross section through the small bacterium Mycoplasma genitalium](assets/img/fig-1-08.png)

Examples include:

* *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*
* *Mycoplasma genitalium*

A synthetic minimal cell called **JCVI-syn3.0** was constructed with only **473 genes**. It was derived from *Mycoplasma mycoides*.

This experiment was important because it investigated the minimum genetic information required for a cell capable of autonomous growth and division under laboratory conditions.

The lecture's own summary of that number: **the chemistry of DNA, RNA and protein — that is, of life — takes roughly 500 genes.**

## Summary of the first half

Straight from the lecture's "Summary so far…" slide:

* An **individual cell is the minimal self-reproducing unit** of living matter.
* …and is a **self-replicating collection of catalysts**.
* **Transmission of information to progeny: DNA.**
* **Proteins are the catalysts** for most chemical reactions.
* **The function of a protein is determined by its amino acid sequence**, which is specified by the DNA sequence.
* So the **genome determines the biochemistry of a cell**.
* **The biochemistry of every living cell is fundamentally similar.**
* The chemistry of DNA/RNA/proteins requires **~500 genes**.

## Two trees of life

The lecture draws the tree twice and asks what each one is based on.

**The classical tree** groups animals by **evolutionary relationships inferred from development and morphology**: Deuterostomia (chordates, echinoderms) on one side, Protostomia (arthropods, nematodes, molluscs, annelids, flatworms) on the other, the two splitting about **700 million years ago**, with sponges and cnidarians below the split.

**The molecular tree** is built from **genome and rRNA sequence comparison**, and it partitions all of life into **three domains**: **Bacteria**, **Archaea** and **Eukaryotes**.

![A global tree of life based on genome comparisons, with branch lengths proportional to genome differences, showing bacteria, archaea and eukaryotes](assets/img/fig-1-09.png)

The point the lecturer makes about the archaea: they are **'intermediates' between bacteria and eukaryotes** — they **resemble eukaryotes in their genetics** (replication, transcription and translation machinery) and **bacteria in their metabolism**. The Asgard archaea sit next to the eukaryote branch, which is where the eukaryotic host lineage came from.

### Humans and the great apes

A worked example of how little separates closely related genomes. Orangutan, gorilla, chimpanzee and bonobo all have **48 chromosomes (24 pairs)**; humans have **46 (23 pairs)**. The missing pair is not missing at all:

* **Human chromosome 2** is a **fusion of two ancestral chromosomes**, matching chimpanzee chromosomes **12 and 13**.
* The banding of the **p arm** matches chimp 12 and the **q arm** matches chimp 13.
* The fusion left **telomeric DNA in the middle** of human chromosome 2, and the position of its centromere matches that of chimp 12.

Divergence times on the slide: orangutan ~13 Mya, gorilla ~8 Mya, chimpanzee and bonobo ~6 Mya from the human line, with chimp and bonobo splitting ~3 Mya.

## Evolution of genomes

The genomes of organisms change over evolutionary time.

These changes generate genetic diversity and can eventually produce organisms with new structures and biological functions.

![Four modes of genetic innovation: intragenic mutation, gene duplication, DNA segment shuffling, and horizontal DNA transfer](assets/img/fig-1-18.png)

Despite this divergence, many genes are highly conserved across evolution.

### Ribosomal RNA

Ribosomal RNA genes are found in all cellular organisms and are highly conserved.

Comparing rRNA sequences between organisms such as humans, *E. coli*, and archaea such as *Methanococcus* reveals their evolutionary relationships.

The sequences are not identical. Instead, important regions have changed slowly enough that their common ancestry can still be recognized.

![A stretch of ribosomal RNA gene sequence aligned across a human, an archaeon and a bacterium, with most positions identical, showing how little this gene has changed since the last universal common ancestor](assets/img/fig-1-17.png)

Because rRNA is both universal and highly conserved, rRNA sequence comparison has been extremely useful for reconstructing the evolutionary relationships between organisms.

The slide aligns the same stretch of rRNA from **human**, ***Methanococcus*** (an archaeon) and ***E. coli***. Most positions still match **after 3.5 billion years of evolution**, which is exactly why this gene is the one used to build the tree — the three-domain tree above.

## Human genes

The lecture asks it as a question: bacteria carry **1,000–6,000 genes**. We?

Humans have approximately **20,000 to 21,000 protein-coding genes** — fewer than *Arabidopsis*, and about the same as a nematode worm.

The number of genes does not directly determine the biological complexity of an organism.

Which leads to the lecture's next question: **where do 'new' genes come from?**

## How new genes arise

### Mutation within an existing gene

Mutations can change the sequence and function of an existing gene.

This does not normally increase the number of genes, but it can produce a gene with an altered function.

### Gene duplication

A gene can be duplicated so that the genome now contains two copies.

The copies can then accumulate mutations independently.

One copy can continue performing the original function while the other is free to evolve a modified or entirely new function.

Gene duplication is therefore an important source of new genes.

### DNA segment shuffling

Existing pieces of DNA can be rearranged into new combinations.

For example, exons or protein domains can be recombined to produce proteins with new combinations of functional regions.

DNA segment shuffling can therefore produce novel genes or novel gene structures without requiring an entirely new sequence to evolve from scratch.

### Horizontal gene transfer

DNA can sometimes move between organisms rather than being inherited from parent to offspring.

This is particularly important in microorganisms.

Horizontal gene transfer can introduce an entirely new gene into a genome.

## Globin gene family

The globin family is an example of evolution through gene duplication.

An ancestral globin gene underwent duplication events, producing related genes that subsequently evolved different functions.

Modern globin proteins include:

* myoglobin
* alpha-globin
* beta-globin
* embryonic and fetal globins

Adult hemoglobin is normally composed of two alpha-globin chains and two beta-globin chains.

Myoglobin is related to the hemoglobin globins but performs a different function, particularly in muscle.

Different globin genes are expressed at different stages of human development.

For example, fetal hemoglobin differs from the predominant form of adult hemoglobin. These differences are advantageous because fetal and adult blood have different physiological requirements.

### The gene clusters and their timeline

The slide's evolutionary tree of the globins puts dates on the duplications:

* the **myoglobin** line and the haemoglobin line split from a single ancestral globin gene about **1,100 million years ago**;
* the **α-cluster** (α1, α2, ζ on **chromosome 16**) and the **β-cluster** (ε, Gγ, Aγ, δ, β on **chromosome 11**) separated about **500 Mya**;
* duplications *within* the β-cluster are much more recent — roughly **200**, **100**, **40** and **30 Mya** — which is why β, δ and the two γ genes are so similar to each other.

Adult haemoglobin is a **tetramer of two α and two β chains**, each carrying a **heme** group, and the slide shows it as α2β2 with the four hemes in green.

![Hemoglobin as a symmetrical assembly of two alpha-globin and two beta-globin subunits, each carrying a heme group](assets/img/fig-3-20.png)

### The fetal-to-adult switch

The lecture goes one step further than the chapter and shows **how** the switch is made, from a 2025 *NEJM* review (Orkin, "The Fetal-to-Adult Hemoglobin Switch — Mechanism and Therapy").

The β-cluster carries a **locus control region (LCR)** upstream of the genes. Which globin is made depends on **which gene the LCR loops onto**:

* **Fetal stage:** the LCR contacts **Gγ and Aγ**; the adult genes are repressed, and the haemoglobin made is **α2γ2**.
* **Adult stage:** the LCR contacts **δ and β**; the fetal genes are repressed, and the haemoglobin made is **α2β2**.

On the expression curves, **ε** falls away in the embryonic stage, **γ** runs at nearly 100% through the fetal stage, and **β** crosses over it **at around birth**, with δ staying minor throughout.

That mechanism is also a drug target: reactivating γ in an adult is a way to treat sickle cell disease and β-thalassaemia, which is why the review is called "Mechanism *and Therapy*".

The similarity between the different globin genes provides evidence that they arose through duplication and divergence from an ancestral globin gene.

## Homologous genes

Genes that share an evolutionary origin are called **homologs**.

Homologs can be divided into paralogs and orthologs.

![Orthologs arise from speciation, paralogs arise from gene duplication within one genome](assets/img/fig-1-20.png)

### Paralogs

**Paralogs** are homologous genes produced by a gene duplication event.

For example, alpha-globin and beta-globin are paralogs.

Because the duplicated genes can evolve independently, paralogs may eventually acquire different functions.

### Orthologs

**Orthologs** are homologous genes found in different species that originated from the same ancestral gene during speciation.

For example, the human version of a gene and the mouse version of the corresponding ancestral gene may be orthologs.

Orthologs often retain similar functions between species.

## Gene families

Related genes produced through duplication and divergence form **gene families**.

Many genes can be assigned to one of hundreds of known gene families.

![Pie chart of gene families in the Bacillus subtilis genome, showing how many genes fall into families of various sizes](assets/img/fig-1-19.png)

If the function of a newly identified gene is unknown, comparison with known members of the same gene family can provide clues about its likely function.

Sequence similarity can therefore be used to infer gene function.

**More than 200 gene families are common to all three domains of life**, and Table 1–1 breaks them down by what they do. The shape of that table is the point: the universal core is mostly **information processing and metabolism**, and almost nothing else.

Cell-cycle control gets two families and signal transduction gets one, because those are the parts each domain built separately.

## Genome size and organism complexity

There is a general tendency for more complex organisms to have larger genomes, but the relationship is weak and contains many exceptions.

Genome size is therefore not a reliable measure of biological complexity.

![Genome sizes compared on a log scale](assets/img/fig-1-30.png)

Much of the difference in genome size between organisms comes from noncoding DNA, repetitive DNA, transposable elements, and differences in genome organization rather than simply the number of protein-coding genes.

**Eukaryotic genomes are big, but the variation within a group is larger than the difference between groups.** On the slide's log scale, bacteria and archaea sit around 10⁶, while protozoans run from the malarial parasite (~10⁷) to amoebae (>10¹¹), and amphibians include both the frog and the newt, two orders of magnitude apart. Mammals, birds and reptiles are a narrow band around 10⁹.

Table 1–2, the numbers worth carrying:

| Organism | Genome size (nucleotide pairs) | Protein-coding genes |
|---|---|---|
| *Escherichia coli* | 4.6 × 10⁶ | 4,300 |
| *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* (yeast) | 12.5 × 10⁶ | 6,600 |
| *Caenorhabditis elegans* | 100 × 10⁶ | 20,000 |
| *Arabidopsis thaliana* | 135 × 10⁶ | 27,000 |
| *Drosophila melanogaster* | 180 × 10⁶ | 14,000 |
| *Danio rerio* (zebrafish) | 1,400 × 10⁶ | 26,000 |
| *Mus musculus* (mouse) | 2,800 × 10⁶ | 20,000 |
| *Homo sapiens* | 3,100 × 10⁶ | 20,000 |

A human genome is **31 times** the size of a fly's and carries **1.4 times** as many genes; *Arabidopsis* has more genes than either. Genome size is not complexity, and gene count is not complexity either.

## Model organisms

A small number of organisms are studied extensively because particular biological processes are easier to investigate in them.

Many fundamental cellular mechanisms are conserved across evolution, so discoveries in model organisms can often be applied to humans.

### *Escherichia coli*

A bacterium used to study fundamental mechanisms such as DNA replication, transcription, gene regulation, and basic molecular biology.

The slide shows the **K-12** genome as a single circular chromosome of **4,639,221 nucleotide pairs**, with its **origin** and **terminus of replication** marked on opposite sides of the circle.

![Scanning electron micrograph of a cluster of E. coli cells beside a circular map of the E. coli K-12 genome](assets/img/fig-1-38.png)

### Yeast

*Saccharomyces cerevisiae* is a single-celled eukaryote, and the electron micrograph on the slide is the argument for using it: in one small round cell you can see a **cell wall**, a **Golgi stack**, a **nucleus**, a **mitochondrion**, **ribosomes** and the **plasma membrane**. It is a eukaryotic cell with everything present and nothing extra.

It has been important for studying the eukaryotic cell cycle, chromosome biology, intracellular organization, and many conserved cellular processes.

![The yeast life cycle: diploid cells proliferate by budding, or under starvation undergo meiosis and sporulation into haploid spores, which hatch into haploid cells that proliferate by budding or mate to restore the diploid state](assets/img/fig-1-40.png)

### *Caenorhabditis elegans*

A small nematode worm with a highly reproducible pattern of development.

It has been particularly important for studying development, cell lineage, programmed cell death, and the nervous system.

![Photograph of the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, about 1 mm long](assets/img/fig-1-42.png)

### *Drosophila melanogaster*

The fruit fly has been central to genetics and developmental biology.

Studies in *Drosophila* revealed many of the genes controlling body organization and development. Related genes were later found to perform similar functions in vertebrates.

![A normal adult Drosophila beside a mutant in which legs have developed where the antennae should be](assets/img/fig-1-43.png)

### *Xenopus*

Frogs of the genus *Xenopus* produce large eggs that are easy to manipulate experimentally. Two species are used, and the slide shows them side by side: the small ***X. tropicalis*** and the larger ***X. laevis***.

They have been useful for studying embryonic development, the cell cycle, and early vertebrate development.

![Drawings of the early stages of Rana pipiens development from fertilized egg through blastula and gastrula to tadpole](assets/img/fig-1-45.png)

### Zebrafish

Zebrafish embryos develop externally and are **transparent** during early development, so organ formation can be watched in a living animal under a microscope — the slide pairs the adults with a two-day embryo about 150 µm across.

They are useful for studying vertebrate development, genetics, organ formation, and disease.

![Zebrafish adults, and a fluorescence image of a two-day-old embryo with blood vessels marked in red and lymphatic vessels in green](assets/img/fig-1-46.png)

### Mouse

*Not in session 1a — the deck stops at zebrafish — but kept here from the chapter, since the mouse is the model organism the rest of the course keeps returning to.*

The mouse is one of the most important mammalian model organisms.

Its close evolutionary relationship to humans makes it useful for studying mammalian development, physiology, genetics, and human disease.

![A human baby and a mouse, each with a similar abnormal white patch on the forehead caused by a mutation in the Kit gene](assets/img/fig-1-48.png)

## Central idea

The diversity of life is built on a common cellular and molecular foundation.

Many genes and cellular mechanisms have been conserved over enormous evolutionary distances.

![Geological timescale of divergence times for pairs of vertebrates, with percent amino acid identity in the hemoglobin alpha chain shown alongside](assets/img/fig-1-47.png)

Studying simpler organisms can therefore reveal principles that also apply to human cells.
