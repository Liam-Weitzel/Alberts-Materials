# The plasma membrane

The lecture opens on an immunofluorescence image: the plasma membrane stained green as a sharp outline around every cell, the nucleus blue in the middle. That outline is the object of the whole chapter, and it is about **5 nm** thick.

Five properties, from the second slide:

* It **separates and protects** the living cell from its surroundings.
* It is **selectively permeable**: some substances cross it far more easily than others.
* It is a **lipid bilayer with proteins embedded in it**.
* It is **fluid**.
* It is where a list of processes happens: **cell-cell and cell-substrate adhesion**, **ion conductivity**, **cell signalling**, and **endocytosis and exocytosis**.

Those last two properties are the ones the rest of the lecture is about. Fluidity is not incidental to a boundary, it is what lets the boundary do anything: a rigid shell could not deform, could not fuse a vesicle with itself, and could not bring two receptors together.

# Membrane composition

## Phospholipids, and why they are amphipathic

**Phospholipids are the most abundant lipid in the plasma membrane.** One molecule has a **polar hydrophilic head group** (on the slide's example, choline on a phosphate on a glycerol) and **two nonpolar hydrocarbon tails**, one of which is kinked by a ***cis*-double bond**.

![Phosphatidylcholine drawn as a chemical formula, a space-filling model, a schematic with a polar head of choline, phosphate and glycerol above two hydrocarbon tails, one kinked by a double bond, and as a simple symbol](assets/img/fig-10-02.png)

A molecule with both a water-loving and a water-hating end is **amphipathic**, and that is the single fact the rest of the structure follows from. Be careful with the generalisation, though: the **membrane** lipids are amphipathic, not lipids in general. A triacylglycerol has no polar head at all and forms a fat droplet rather than a bilayer.

## Why a bilayer assembles by itself

Put amphipathic molecules in water and they arrange themselves, with no machinery and no energy input, so that the hydrocarbon tails are kept away from water and the polar heads face it. Which arrangement you get depends on the shape of the molecule:

| Arrangement | Which lipids | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| **Micelle** | Single-tailed, cone-shaped | A sphere with the tails all pointing to a common centre |
| **Bilayer** | Two-tailed, cylindrical | Two sheets tail to tail, heads out on both faces |

Phospholipids have two tails, so they give **bilayers**, spontaneously, in aqueous environments, with the hydrophilic polar heads to the outside.

![Cone-shaped amphiphilic molecules packing into a spherical micelle and cylinder-shaped ones packing into a bilayer, with cross sections of a micelle and a lipid bilayer in water](assets/img/fig-10-07.png)

A sheet with free edges would expose tails to water along those edges, so a bilayer closes on itself into a sealed compartment. That is why a membrane has no ends, and why a punctured membrane reseals.

## The bilayer is a two-dimensional fluid

Nothing in the bilayer is bonded to its neighbour. Each lipid is free to move, and the slide names four motions:

| Motion | What it is | How often |
|---|---|---|
| **Lateral diffusion** | Swapping places with a neighbour in the same monolayer | Constantly |
| **Rotation** | Spinning about its long axis | Constantly |
| **Flexion** | The tails bending and flopping | Constantly |
| **Flip-flop** | Crossing from one monolayer to the other | **Rarely** |

The odd one out is flip-flop, and the reason it is rare is the reason for everything else on this slide. Moving sideways costs a lipid nothing, because its head stays in water the whole way. Flipping across means dragging a **polar head group through the hydrophobic core**, which is energetically expensive, so it essentially does not happen without an enzyme.

![A computer simulation of a phosphatidylcholine bilayer showing disordered head groups and tangled tails with water on both sides, and a diagram of lipid motions: lateral diffusion, flexion, rotation, and the rare flip-flop](assets/img/fig-10-10.png)

That asymmetry between fast lateral motion and forbidden transverse motion is what makes the bilayer a **two-dimensional fluid**: freely mobile within each sheet, and sealed between them.

## Glycolipids and sterols

The bilayer is not phospholipid alone. Two other classes are on the slide.

**Glycolipids** carry **sugars attached in place of the phosphate-linked group**. The slide's examples are galactocerebroside, with a single galactose, and the GM1 ganglioside, with a branched chain of sugars including a negatively charged one.

![Structures of the neutral glycolipid galactocerebroside, the GM1 ganglioside with its branched sugar chain including a charged sialic acid, and the sialic acid N-acetylneuraminic acid](assets/img/fig-10-16.png)

**Sterols**, of which the example is **cholesterol**, have three parts worth naming because each one does something: a small **polar head group** (a hydroxyl), a **rigid steroid ring structure**, and a short **nonpolar hydrocarbon tail**.

![The structure of cholesterol as a formula, a space-filling model and a schematic, with a small polar hydroxyl head, a rigid planar steroid ring structure and a short nonpolar hydrocarbon tail](assets/img/fig-10-04.png)

Cholesterol is amphipathic like the phospholipids, so it inserts in the same orientation, hydroxyl up beside the phospholipid head groups and rings packed against the first part of the tails. The rings are the point: they are **flat and rigid**, so wherever cholesterol sits, the neighbouring hydrocarbon chains cannot move as freely.

> **A correction to the rough note.** The note has it that more cholesterol makes the bilayer more rigid and more glycolipids make it more fluid. The first half is right. The second half is backwards: the **glycosphingolipids have long, largely saturated tails that pack tightly**, so regions rich in them are **more ordered, not less**. That is exactly why they cluster with cholesterol rather than with anything else, which is the next slide's subject. The slide itself says only that fluidity depends on composition; the detail is from the book.

What genuinely does raise fluidity, from the book rather than the slides, is **shorter tails** and **more *cis*-double bonds**. A kink stops chains packing against one another neatly, which is why the slide's phospholipid drawing bothers to label its *cis*-double bond.

![Unsaturated hydrocarbon chains with cis-double bonds packing loosely and irregularly, compared with saturated chains packing tightly and evenly](assets/img/fig-10-11.png)

# The fluid mosaic model

The model states that **a membrane is a fluid structure with a mosaic of various proteins embedded in it**. Two claims, and the lecture then spends three slides proving the first one.

**Fluidity depends on composition**, and composition is not fixed: different cell types, and different organelles within one cell, have different lipid compositions. A membrane is therefore tuned, not generic.

## Lipid rafts

**Lipid rafts are clusters of cholesterol and glycosphingolipids** within the membrane. Three consequences, in the order the slide gives them:

1. Rafts **move freely within the membrane**, as units.
2. Proteins embedded in a raft are **brought into proximity of other proteins** in the same raft.
3. Rafts can therefore **regulate cell signalling**.

![A model of a raft domain in which cholesterol, glycolipids, GPI-anchored proteins and transmembrane glycoproteins cluster together in a thicker patch of bilayer](assets/img/fig-10-13.png)

The logic here is the answer to "why would a cell care which lipids are next to which". A signalling cascade needs its components to meet. Diffusion in two dimensions already makes meeting far likelier than in three, and a raft narrows it further: it concentrates a chosen subset of proteins into one drifting patch, and excludes the rest. Lipid composition becomes a way of **organising protein encounters** without building a scaffold. How large and how stable a raft really is has been contested for as long as the idea has existed, and [[munro-2003-lipid-rafts]] is the review that puts the case for scepticism.

# Showing that a membrane is fluid

Three experiments, in the order the lecture gave them. They are a good set because they measure different things: the first shows that proteins move at all, the second measures how fast and what fraction, the third shows where the proteins sit.

## Frye and Edidin, 1970

Fuse a **mouse cell** with a **human cell** and you get a **heterokaryon**, one cell with both surfaces. Label the mouse membrane proteins with one fluorescent antibody and the human ones with another, and watch.

Immediately after fusion the two labels sit in **separate halves**. With time at 37 °C they **intermix**, until every part of the surface carries both. The slide quantifies it: of the doubly stained cells, essentially none have intermixed at 5 and 10 minutes, about half by 25 minutes, and effectively all by 40, with the 120 minute point complete.

![Slide 9: the Frye and Edidin heterokaryon experiment, with mouse and human cells fused, fluorescence images of the two labels and of a heterokaryon, and a bar chart of mosaic against non-mosaic double-stained cells over incubation time at 37 degrees](assets/img/slide-6-09.png)

Nothing about this requires a transport machine. The proteins simply diffuse, and the surface homogenises on a timescale of tens of minutes. That is the first direct demonstration that a membrane is a fluid rather than a fixed structure.

## Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP)

Frye and Edidin needed two cells and an hour. FRAP does the same measurement on one cell in seconds, and returns numbers.

Label the membrane protein of interest fluorescently, pick a **region of interest**, and **bleach** it with a strong laser pulse: the fluorophores in that spot are destroyed and the spot goes dark. Then watch the spot. Unbleached molecules from outside diffuse in, and the fluorescence **recovers**.

![Slide 10: FRAP shown as pre-bleach, bleaching of a region of interest, post-bleach and recovery images, a top and side view of the bleached patch refilling, and a recovery curve labelled with the diffusion coefficient and the mobile fraction](assets/img/slide-6-10.png)

The recovery curve carries two separate readings, and the slide labels both:

| Reading | Where it is on the curve | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Diffusion coefficient** | The **rate** of recovery | How fast the mobile molecules move |
| **Mobile fraction** | The **plateau** it recovers to | What proportion of the molecules can move at all |

The second is the more interesting one. If recovery stops short of the original intensity, some of the labelled protein never came back, which means a fraction of it is **anchored**. A membrane protein can be fluid and immobilised at the same time, in different pools, and FRAP is what separates them.

## Freeze-fracture

The third technique looks at structure rather than motion. Freeze the sample, then hit it with a knife. The fracture **splits the membrane along the middle of the lipid bilayer**, because the middle, where the two sets of tails meet, is the weakest plane in the whole structure. The two exposed faces are then imaged by **electron microscopy**.

![Slide 11: the freeze-fracture technique, with a knife splitting a frozen plasma membrane between its two monolayers, and electron micrographs of the inside of the extracellular layer and the inside of the cytoplasmic layer](assets/img/slide-6-11.png)

The faces are studded with particles, and those particles are the **transmembrane proteins**, each staying with one monolayer as the membrane splits. Two things follow. Proteins are genuinely **embedded in** the bilayer rather than lying on it, which is the "mosaic" half of the model. And the two faces are **not equally populated**: the inside of the cytoplasmic layer carries many more particles than the inside of the extracellular layer, so the two halves of a membrane are not equivalent.

# Membrane proteins

## How a protein can be attached to a membrane

One diagram, ten numbered proteins, six categories. The lecturer's categories:

| Category | Numbers | How it is held |
|---|---|---|
| **Transmembrane** | 1, 2, 5 | Crosses the bilayer, exposed on **both** faces |
| **Integral** | 3, 4 | Embedded in the bilayer, exposed on **one** face only |
| **Monotopic** | 6 | An α-helix lying **within one monolayer**, not crossing |
| **Lipid associated** | 7 | Held by a **covalently attached lipid chain**, on the cytosolic side |
| **GPI anchored** | 8 | Held by a **glycosylphosphatidylinositol anchor**, on the extracellular side |
| **Membrane associated** | 9, 10 | **Never touches the bilayer**: bound to another membrane protein |

![Slide 12: the lecturer's classification of membrane proteins, with ten numbered proteins drawn in and on a lipid bilayer and grouped into transmembrane, integral, monotopic, lipid-associated, GPI-anchored and membrane-associated](assets/img/slide-6-12.png)

> **A note on the word "integral".** The lecturer uses it narrowly, for a protein embedded in the bilayer but exposed on only one face. The textbook uses it much more broadly, for anything that can only be released by disrupting the bilayer with a detergent, transmembrane proteins included, with **peripheral** for everything else. The exam follows the slides, so use the lecturer's sense, but do not be surprised by the other one.

One principle sits behind the whole table: a protein is held in a membrane either because **part of the protein itself is hydrophobic**, or because **something hydrophobic has been attached to it**, or because it is **holding onto something that is already there**. Those are the only three options, and the six categories are variations on them.

## Transmembrane proteins

Four architectures, each with an example.

| Architecture | Built from | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Single pass** | 1 α-helix | Glycophorin A |
| **Multipass** | More than 1 α-helix | Bacteriorhodopsin, with 7 |
| **β-barrel** | A closed sheet of β-strands | Porins |
| **Monotopic** | A helix entering one leaflet only | COX-1, prostaglandin H2 synthase |

![Slide 13: the four transmembrane protein architectures drawn in a bilayer, single pass, multipass, beta barrel and monotopic, each with an example protein](assets/img/slide-6-13.png)

The α-helix is the default because it is the cheapest way to cross. Peptide bonds are polar, and a stretch of them inside the hydrocarbon core would have nowhere to put its hydrogen bonds; rolling the backbone into a helix **satisfies every backbone hydrogen bond internally** and leaves only the side chains facing the lipid, so the side chains can all be hydrophobic. The β-barrel solves the same problem the other way, by closing a sheet into a cylinder, which also gives a water-filled channel down the middle. That is why porins are barrels: the architecture and the function are the same fact.

**Monotopic** appears twice, and the two appearances disagree slightly. On the classification slide it is its own category, number 6, and not counted as transmembrane. On this slide it sits alongside the three transmembrane architectures. Remember it as a fourth way of engaging the bilayer rather than arguing about the label.

## Lipid-associated and GPI-anchored proteins

Four mechanisms, from a protein that is not itself hydrophobic.

| Mechanism | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Electrostatic attraction** | A **positively charged patch** on the protein surface is drawn to the negatively charged lipid head groups | Cytochrome *c* |
| **Covalent modification** | A **lipid chain is attached to the protein**, and that chain inserts into the bilayer | GPI-anchored proteins, Ras |
| **Specific protein-lipid binding** | The protein has a site that **binds one particular lipid head group** | Annexins |
| **Non-specific hydrophobic association** | A **hydrophobic stretch** of the protein buries itself in the bilayer without a dedicated binding site | MARCKS |

![Slide 14: lipid-associated and GPI proteins, with a drawing of a positively charged protein attracted to a negatively charged bilayer surface, and the four mechanisms listed](assets/img/slide-6-14.png)

The rough note has these four blurred together, and one point needs untangling. For a **GPI-anchored** protein, the name is glycosyl**phosphatidylinositol**: there are sugars in it, but the part that goes into the membrane is the **lipid**. Sugars do not insert into a bilayer, which is the whole reason a glycolipid needs a fatty tail in the first place.

The four differ in a way that matters for whether the association is regulated. An electrostatic or non-specific hydrophobic association is **weak and reversible**, so it can be switched by changing the protein's charge, which is what phosphorylation does. A covalently attached lipid is **not reversible on that timescale**. Cytochrome *c* sitting on the mitochondrial inner membrane by charge alone, and released from it, is the classic case of the first kind.

## Membrane-associated proteins

The example is the link between the extracellular matrix and the cytoskeleton. An **integrin dimer** crosses the membrane. Outside, it binds **fibronectin**, which in turn binds **collagen**. Inside, it binds **adaptor proteins**, which bind **actin**.

![Slide 15: an integrin dimer in the cell membrane linking fibronectin and collagen outside the cell to adaptor proteins and actin inside it](assets/img/slide-6-15.png)

Only the integrin is a transmembrane protein. Fibronectin and the adaptors are the membrane-associated ones: they never touch the bilayer, and are held there entirely by binding the integrin. What the arrangement achieves is **mechanical continuity** across the membrane, so that a pull on the matrix is felt by the cytoskeleton and vice versa.

## What membrane proteins do

Five functions, from the slide:

* **Enzymatic activity**
* **Signal transduction**
* **Cell-cell and cell-substrate adhesion**
* **Intercellular communication**
* **Docking for cytoskeletal proteins**

The integrin on the previous slide is three of these at once, which is the useful thing to notice: it is adhesion, it is docking for the cytoskeleton, and it signals. The categories describe jobs, not proteins.

# Membrane proteins and signalling: TGFβ/BMP

The worked example, and the one place in this half of the lecture where a whole pathway is traced. **TGFβ/BMP signalling is crucial during embryonic development as well as in cancer**, which is why it is the one chosen.

![Slide 17: TGF-beta and BMP signalling, from type I and type II receptors with SARA at the membrane, through receptor oligomerisation and phosphorylation, R-Smad recruitment and complex formation with Smad4, to the nucleus with a partner transcription factor](assets/img/slide-6-17.png)

The pieces, before the sequence:

* **Type I and type II receptors** are transmembrane proteins with **(self)catalytic moieties**, meaning they are their own kinases. In the book they are serine/threonine kinases.
* **SARA** is a **membrane-associated protein**, already sitting beside the receptors before any ligand arrives. This is the point of putting the pathway in this lecture: every class of membrane protein from the classification slide has a job here.
* **Smurf1/2** are named as other membrane-associated proteins controlling the signalling near the membrane.

Then the sequence:

1. **The TGF/BMP ligand binds.** The slide's phrasing is that binding of ligand **results in increased affinity of type I/II for other type I or type II receptors**. It does not say which receptor catches the ligand first; the book has TGFβ binding the type II receptor first, which then recruits type I.
2. **The receptors oligomerise**, and the activated complex drawn on the slide contains two of each type.
3. **The complex phosphorylates itself.** The slide marks the phosphate on the receptor complex.
4. **SARA presents the R-Smad** to the activated receptors, and the **R-Smad is phosphorylated**.
5. **The phosphorylated R-Smads leave the receptor and assemble with Smad4.** The complex drawn is **two R-Smads plus one Smad4**.
6. **That complex enters the nucleus**, where it sits on DNA **together with a partner transcription factor** and regulates transcription.

> **Three corrections to the rough note.** The note has SARA being *recruited* by the receptor phosphorylation; the slide shows SARA at the membrane from the start, before ligand. The note has a **tetrameric** Smad complex; the slide draws a **trimer**, two R-Smads and one Smad4. And the note has the Smad complex acting as the transcription factor on its own; the slide draws it **beside a separate TF** on the promoter, which matters because it is how one pathway gives different outputs in different cells.

The note also records that the type I and type II receptors sit in **lipid rafts** along with SARA. That is not on the slides, but it was said in the lecture, and it closes the loop with the raft slide: the raft is what holds the receptors and SARA close enough for any of this to happen quickly.

# Summary

The lecturer's own closing slide, which is as good a revision checklist as any:

* A cell is an **open system** exchanging molecules with the extracellular environment, and that exchange is **controlled by the plasma membrane**.
* Plasma membranes are **selectively permeable**, regulating the entry and exit of specific molecules.
* Plasma membranes are an **amphipathic lipid bilayer**, hydrophobic tails inwards and hydrophilic heads outwards.
* **Membrane proteins are embedded in the lipid bilayer.**
* Plasma membranes are **fluid**, and **lipid rafts move** within them.
